    Study 15: The Strongest Case for Eternal Torment -- Answer Key¶

    Examining the Best Arguments -- What Do the Texts Actually Say?

    A Note on How to Study

    Throughout this series, we distinguish between what the Bible explicitly says and what people infer from the Bible. These are not the same thing.

    • Explicit: You can point to a verse and quote it. The text directly says this.
    • Inference: Someone claims the Bible teaches something, but no single verse says it. They have drawn a conclusion -- possibly a good one, possibly not -- but the conclusion itself is not in the text.

    This distinction is critical in this study. We are examining the 8 passages that ECT (Eternal Conscious Torment) scholars identify as their strongest biblical case. The question is not whether these passages are important -- they are. The question is: what does each text actually say, and what must be added to reach the ECT conclusion?

    Section A: The Revelation Passages¶

    1. Read Revelation 14:10-11. The text says beast-worshippers "shall be tormented with fire and brimstone" and "the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night." What genre is this passage -- didactic teaching or apocalyptic vision?

    Apocalyptic vision. Revelation is explicitly described as communicating through signs and symbols (Rev 1:1, semaino). The passage occurs within a sequence of angelic announcements in a highly symbolic narrative. It is not a didactic epistle or direct teaching discourse.

    "The same shall drink of the wine of the wrath of God, which is poured out without mixture into the cup of his indignation; and he shall be tormented with fire and brimstone in the presence of the holy angels, and in the presence of the Lamb: And the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night, who worship the beast and his image." -- Revelation 14:10-11

    2. Now read Isaiah 34:10. This OT passage uses the exact same "smoke ascending forever" and "not quenched night nor day" language for Edom. Now read Isaiah 34:11-17. What creatures inhabit Edom afterward?

    Pelicans, hedgehogs, owls, ravens, wild goats, and other creatures inhabit the territory (Isa 34:11-17). Animals cannot dwell in a place where fire is actively burning. The "forever" smoke and "not quenched night nor day" fire completed its destructive work, and then went out. The identical imagery in the OT source describes completed destruction, not ongoing burning.

    "It shall not be quenched night nor day; the smoke thereof shall go up for ever." -- Isaiah 34:10

    "But the cormorant and the bittern shall possess it; the owl also and the raven shall dwell in it." -- Isaiah 34:11

    3. Read Revelation 18:8-10, 21 and Revelation 19:3. Babylon is destroyed -- then her "smoke rose up for ever and ever." What does "smoke rising forever" mean in Revelation's own usage?

    In Revelation's own usage, "smoke rising forever" describes completed, permanent destruction -- not ongoing torment. Babylon is "utterly burned with fire" (18:8), destroyed "in one hour" (18:10), and "thrown down, and shall be found no more at all" (18:21). Then her smoke rises forever (19:3). Babylon's basanismos IS her completed destruction. This is the same book that uses the identical formula for the beast-worshippers in Rev 14:11. If Revelation itself uses "smoke ascending forever" for something that is demonstrably destroyed and gone, the formula cannot be assumed to mean ongoing conscious existence.

    "Therefore shall her plagues come in one day, death, and mourning, and famine; and she shall be utterly burned with fire." -- Revelation 18:8

    "And her smoke rose up for ever and ever." -- Revelation 19:3

    4. Read Revelation 20:10. Who are the three subjects tormented day and night for ever and ever?

    The devil, the beast, and the false prophet. None is a literal human being. The devil is a supernatural entity. The beast and false prophet are symbolic figures in Revelation's apocalyptic framework. This is the only verse in the entire Bible that uses the "tormented for ever and ever" formula (basanisthesontai eis tous aionas ton aionon), and it applies exclusively to these three non-human/symbolic subjects.

    "And the devil that deceived them was cast into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and the false prophet are, and shall be tormented day and night for ever and ever." -- Revelation 20:10

    5. Now read Revelation 20:9. When human enemies face God's fire, what word does the text use?

    "Devoured" (katephagen). When the text describes what happens to human enemies, it uses destruction vocabulary, not torment vocabulary. They are consumed, not tormented.

    "And fire came down from God out of heaven, and devoured them." -- Revelation 20:9

    6. Read Revelation 20:14-15 and Revelation 21:8. When humans enter the lake of fire, what does the text call it?

    "The second death." Not "the second torment," not "eternal conscious torment" -- the text's own term is death. The same lake of fire where the devil, beast, and false prophet are tormented is called "the second death" when humans enter it. The text itself distinguishes between what happens to the symbolic entities and what happens to humans.

    "And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death." -- Revelation 20:14

    "But the fearful, and unbelieving... shall have their part in the lake which burneth with fire and brimstone: which is the second death." -- Revelation 21:8

    7. Read Revelation 6:9-11. What genre is this, and how should the fifth seal be read?

    Apocalyptic vision. In the same chapter, the four horsemen (6:1-8) are symbolic, and the cosmic upheaval (6:12-14 -- sun black, moon blood, stars falling, sky rolling up) is symbolic. Every other seal in the sequence uses symbolic imagery. The fifth seal sits between two symbolic seals and should be read within the same apocalyptic framework. The imagery follows the OT pattern of blood crying for justice (Gen 4:10 -- Abel's blood cries from the ground without Abel being literally conscious). Other elements in Revelation are personified the same way: the altar speaks (Rev 16:7), Death is personified (Rev 6:8).

    "I saw under the altar the souls of them that were slain for the word of God, and for the testimony which they held." -- Revelation 6:9

    Section B: The Gospel Passages¶

    8. Read Matthew 25:46. The Greek word for "punishment" is kolasis (G2851). Does it mean "torment" or "punishment/penalty"?

    Kolasis means punishment or penalty. It occurs only twice in the entire NT (Matt 25:46 and 1 John 4:18). It is a different Greek word from basanismos (torment/torture). The text says "everlasting punishment" -- not "everlasting torment." The nature of the punishment is not specified by the word kolasis; it simply identifies a penalty. Destruction can be a punishment. Death can be a punishment. The word itself does not require that the punishment involve ongoing conscious experience.

    "And these shall go away into everlasting punishment [kolasin aionion]: but the righteous into life eternal [zoen aionion]." -- Matthew 25:46

    9. Does the adjective aionios determine the nature of the noun it modifies, or only the duration?

    The adjective describes duration, not nature. Consider the parallel: "everlasting life" and "everlasting destruction" (2 Thess 1:9) both use aionios, but life and destruction are opposite natures. The same-adjective argument proves the two outcomes have the same duration scope -- both are permanent. It does not prove they have the same nature. The nature of each outcome is determined by the noun: zoe (life) and kolasis (punishment). The nouns, not the adjective, tell you what is being experienced. And kolasis means punishment -- not torment.

    10. Read Mark 9:43-48, quoting Isaiah 66:24. What are the subjects the worm and fire act upon?

    Dead bodies -- carcasses. The Hebrew word in Isaiah 66:24 is peger, which occurs 22 times in the OT and always refers to a corpse, never to a living person. Isaiah 66:16-17 states that the transgressors were slain and consumed. The worm and fire act upon dead bodies, not conscious living beings. "Unquenchable fire" in OT usage (Jer 17:27; Ezek 20:47) means fire that cannot be stopped until it finishes consuming -- it does not mean fire that burns forever. Jeremiah 17:27 describes Jerusalem's gates, which are not still burning.

    "And they shall go forth, and look upon the carcases [peger] of the men that have transgressed against me: for their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched." -- Isaiah 66:24

    11. Read Luke 16:19-31. Is this a parable or a doctrinal treatise? What does it describe -- the final fate or the intermediate state?

    It is a parable. Its stated teaching point is about heeding Moses and the prophets (vv.29, 31), not about the mechanics of the afterlife. The vocabulary used (basanos/odunao) is different from the eschatological torment vocabulary (basanizo/basanismos). ECT scholars themselves acknowledge that this passage describes hades (the intermediate state before final judgment), not the lake of fire (the final destination). It cannot be used as evidence for what happens in the final judgment because it does not describe the final judgment.

    "They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them." -- Luke 16:29

    "If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead." -- Luke 16:31

    Section C: The Epistle and OT Passages¶

    12. Read 2 Thessalonians 1:9. What does olethros (G3639) mean?

    Olethros means destruction or ruin. It occurs 4 times in the NT (2 Thess 1:9; 1 Thess 5:3; 1 Cor 5:5; 1 Tim 6:9), and in every occurrence it means destruction/ruin. No lexicon defines it as "ongoing conscious torment." Paul -- writing in a didactic epistle (the clearest genre for doctrinal teaching) -- chose a destruction word, not a torment word.

    "Who shall be punished with everlasting destruction [olethron aionion] from the presence of the Lord." -- 2 Thessalonians 1:9

    13. Does olethros mean "separation"? If someone redefines the word, is the resulting claim explicit or an inference?

    Olethros does not mean "separation." It means destruction. Redefining it as "ongoing conscious ruin" or "eternal separation from God's presence" overrides the word's lexical meaning. No Greek lexicon supports this redefinition. The resulting claim is not just an inference -- it is an inference that requires changing what the text actually says. It adds a meaning the word does not carry. Paul had available Greek words for "separation" (chorismos, chorizo) -- he did not use them. He used a word that means destruction.

    14. Read Daniel 12:2. The word dera'on (H1860) occurs only twice in the OT. In Isaiah 66:24, who views the dead with dera'on?

    The living onlookers view the dead with dera'on (contempt/abhorrence). In Isaiah 66:24, "they shall go forth, and look upon the carcases (peger)" -- the subjects of dera'on are dead bodies, and the contempt is the reaction of those who observe them. "Everlasting contempt" in Daniel 12:2, read in light of its only other occurrence, describes how the destroyed are permanently remembered by the living -- not a conscious experience of the dead. The dead are the objects of contempt, not the subjects experiencing it.

    "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt [dera'on olam]." -- Daniel 12:2

    "And they shall go forth, and look upon the carcases [peger] of the men that have transgressed against me." -- Isaiah 66:24

    Section D: The Big Picture¶

    15. Does any of the 8 passages use basanizo or basanismos for the final fate of generic human wicked in a didactic context?

    No. Not a single epistle, not a single Gospel, and not a single OT passage uses basanizo or basanismos for the final fate of generic human wicked. The torment vocabulary appears in Revelation's apocalyptic framework (Rev 14:10-11 for beast-worshippers; Rev 20:10 for the devil, beast, and false prophet) and in demon-speech (Matt 8:29, where demons ask "art thou come to torment us?"). When didactic passages address the fate of the wicked, they consistently use destruction/death vocabulary.

    16. What vocabulary do didactic passages use for the fate of the wicked?

    Destruction and death vocabulary -- consistently, across multiple authors:

    Passage Author Word Used Meaning
    Matt 10:28 Jesus apollymi Destroy
    John 3:16 John apollymi Perish
    Rom 6:23 Paul thanatos Death
    2 Thess 1:9 Paul olethros Destruction
    Phil 3:19 Paul apoleia Destruction
    Matt 7:13 Jesus apoleia Destruction

    The dominant word family across didactic passages is destruction/death -- not torment. No didactic passage uses torment vocabulary for the final fate of the wicked.

    Explicit or Inference? -- Answers¶

    A. "Rev 14:10-11 states that beast-worshippers are tormented with fire and brimstone in an apocalyptic vision, and that the smoke of their torment ascends forever." - Explicit. The text directly says this. One can point to the verse and quote it.

    B. "Rev 14:10-11 proves that all unsaved humans will experience eternal conscious torment." - Inference. The text describes beast-worshippers in an apocalyptic vision using imagery whose OT source (Isa 34:10) describes completed destruction. The claim extends the passage from specific subjects in apocalyptic genre to all unsaved humans, treats symbolic imagery as literal, and ignores the OT precedent where the same imagery describes destruction that has ended.

    C. "Rev 20:10 states that the devil, the beast, and the false prophet are tormented forever. None is a literal human being." - Explicit. The text names the three subjects. None is a literal human being.

    D. "Because the devil is tormented in the lake of fire, all humans cast into the lake of fire will also be tormented." - Inference. The text itself distinguishes between the three named subjects (torment vocabulary) and humans (death/destruction vocabulary). Human enemies are "devoured" (Rev 20:9). Human entry into the lake is called "the second death" (Rev 20:14-15). The claim adds the assumption that same destination = same experience, when the text provides different vocabulary for each.

    E. "Matt 25:46 uses the word kolasis (punishment), not basanismos (torment)." - Explicit. This is an observable lexical fact. The Greek text uses kolasis, not basanismos.

    F. "Mark 9:43-48 describes conscious beings experiencing ongoing torment in gehenna." - Inference. The text quotes Isaiah 66:24, where the subjects are peger (dead bodies/carcasses). The claim adds consciousness to subjects the source text identifies as corpses.

    G. "2 Thess 1:9 says 'everlasting destruction' using the word olethros, which means destruction/ruin." - Explicit. This is what the text says, and olethros means destruction in all 4 NT occurrences.

    H. "The ECT case rests entirely on explicit biblical statements that directly say human wicked will be consciously tormented forever." - Inference. No verse in the Bible directly states that human wicked will experience eternal conscious torment. All 8 ECT anchor texts require inference -- adding concepts, choosing between readings, or extending statements about non-human subjects to humans -- to reach the ECT conclusion.

    What the Bible Says vs. What People Say the Bible Teaches¶

    The Bible explicitly says... People often claim the Bible teaches...
    Rev 14:10-11 uses torment language for beast-worshippers in an apocalyptic vision; the OT source (Isa 34:10) describes completed destruction Rev 14:10-11 teaches that all unsaved humans will be tormented forever
    Rev 20:10 names three non-human subjects for torment; humans are "devoured" (v.9) and experience "second death" (vv.14-15) All humans in the lake of fire share the devil's experience of eternal torment
    Matt 25:46 says "everlasting punishment" (kolasis), not "everlasting torment" (basanismos) "Everlasting punishment" means ongoing conscious torment
    Mark 9:43-48 quotes Isa 66:24, which describes dead bodies (peger) Mark 9 describes conscious beings in eternal torment
    Luke 16:19-31 is a parable about hades; ECT scholars agree it does not describe the final fate Luke 16 teaches what happens to the wicked after final judgment
    2 Thess 1:9 says "everlasting destruction" (olethros = destruction) Olethros means "ongoing conscious ruin" or "separation"
    Dan 12:2 "everlasting contempt" (dera'on) -- the only other use describes onlookers' reaction to dead bodies "Everlasting contempt" means the wicked consciously experience shame forever
    No didactic passage uses torment vocabulary for the final fate of human wicked The Bible clearly and explicitly teaches eternal conscious torment
