    Study 3: What Does "Death" Mean in the Bible? -- Advanced Study¶

    Hebrew and Greek Word Studies on Death

    Evidence Tiers

    This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:

    1. Explicit (E) -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
    2. Necessary Implication (N) -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
    3. Inference (I) -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be added beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.

    The hierarchy matters: Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference. An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

    Section A: The Hebrew Words for Death -- Maveth (H4194) and Mut (H4191)¶

    1. Maveth (H4194, noun, "death") appears approximately 160 times in the OT. Mut (H4191, verb, "to die") appears approximately 835 times. Look up Genesis 2:17. The Hebrew reads mot tamut -- an emphatic infinitive absolute construction. This same construction appears in Exodus 21:12, 15-17 and Leviticus 20:2ff for judicial death penalties. What does mot tamut mean in every Torah death-penalty context?

    2. Adam did not physically die on the day he ate (Gen 5:5 records his death at 930 years). The phrase "in the day" (beyom) is used idiomatically in Genesis 2:4 for the entire creation period. Given the mot tamut pattern and God's definition of death in Gen 3:19 ("dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return"), what kind of death did God warn about?

    3. Read Deuteronomy 30:15, 19. God sets maveth and chayyim (life) before Israel as exhaustive alternatives. How many options does the text present? Is there a third category?

    4. Read Ezekiel 18:4, 20-21, 23. The Hebrew says the nephesh (soul) that sins -- mut (dies). In the same passage, what happens to the wicked person who turns from sin? Does God take pleasure in the maveth of the wicked?

    Section B: The Greek Words for Death -- Thanatos (G2288) and Apothnesko (G599)¶

    1. Thanatos (G2288, noun, "death") appears 119 times in the NT. Apothnesko (G599, verb, "to die") appears 112 times. The LXX translators rendered Hebrew maveth as thanatos 97 times. What does this translation consistency indicate about the semantic relationship between the Hebrew and Greek death vocabulary?

    2. Read Romans 5:12, 14, 17, 21. Paul uses thanatos throughout this passage to describe the death that entered through Adam. Does Paul use a different Greek word for "the death that entered through Adam" and "the death that is the wages of sin" (Rom 6:23)? Or is it the same word?

    3. Read Romans 6:23. The text contrasts thanatos (death) with zoe aionios (eternal life). If death were a form of conscious eternal existence, what logical problem would this contrast create?

    4. Read 1 Corinthians 15:21-22, 26, 54-56. Paul connects death to Adam (v.21-22), calls it the last enemy to be destroyed (v.26), and says it will be "swallowed up in victory" (v.54). If thanatos meant eternal conscious torment, what would "the last enemy that shall be destroyed" mean?

    Section C: The OT Description of Death -- Multiple Authors¶

    1. Construct a composite picture of death from the following OT passages. For each, note the author and what specific aspect of death is described:
    2. Psalm 146:4 (David)
    3. Ecclesiastes 9:5-6, 10 (Solomon)
    4. Psalm 6:5 (David)
    5. Psalm 115:17 (unknown psalmist)
    6. Psalm 88:10-12 (Heman the Ezrahite)
    7. Psalm 30:9 (David)
    8. Job 14:10-12, 21 (Job)
    9. Job 3:17-18 (Job)

    10. Isaiah 38:18-19 (Hezekiah)

    11. How many distinct biblical authors describe death in the passages above? Do any of them describe death as conscious existence in another realm?

    12. Read Ecclesiastes 3:19-20. What does Solomon say about the ruach (breath/spirit) of humans and animals? What fate do they share?

    Section D: Death as the Reversal of Creation¶

    1. Compare the creation formula in Genesis 2:7 with the death description in Ecclesiastes 12:7.

      • Creation: _ + = __
      • Death: _ returns to + __ returns to _____
      • What is left?

    2. Read Psalm 104:29 and Job 34:14-15. Both describe what happens when God withdraws breath. Does either passage limit this to humans, or does it apply to "all flesh"?

    3. Read James 2:26. The text says "the body without the spirit [pneuma] is dead." Does this verse define what happens to the spirit after it leaves, or does it only describe the state of the body?

    Section E: "Spiritual Death" -- Metaphorical Use of Death Vocabulary¶

    1. Read Ephesians 2:1, 5 and Colossians 2:13. These passages describe believers as having been "dead in trespasses and sins" before God "quickened" (made alive) them. The people described are physically alive. What literary device is at work when "dead" (nekros) is applied to living people?

    2. Read 1 Timothy 5:6. "She that liveth in pleasure is dead while she liveth." If "dead" here means "consciously existing in separation from God," does the sentence make sense? What meaning of "dead" makes the statement striking?

    3. Read Romans 8:6. "To be carnally minded is death [thanatos]; to be spiritually minded is life and peace." The same word thanatos is used here as in Romans 6:23 ("the wages of sin is death") and Revelation 20:14 ("this is the second death"). Does any verse in Scripture say that thanatos means something different in these three contexts?

    4. Read Ephesians 4:18. The text says the unsaved are "alienated from the life of God." Does the text say "alienated from God" or "alienated from the life of God"? What is the difference?

    Section F: The Second Death¶

    1. The phrase "second death" (ho thanatos ho deuteros) appears four times, all in Revelation: 2:11; 20:6; 20:14; 21:8. The word used is thanatos -- the same word used for death throughout the NT. Does the text introduce a new definition for thanatos when it adds the modifier "second"?

    2. Read Revelation 20:14. The subjects cast into the lake of fire here are "death and hades" -- personified/abstract entities. Now read Revelation 20:10. The subjects tormented "day and night for ever and ever" are the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. Are the subjects of Rev 20:10 human beings, or are they non-human/symbolic entities?

    3. Read Revelation 21:8. The wicked "shall have their part in the lake of fire and brimstone: which is the second death." The text calls the human fate "the second death" -- not "the second torment" or "the second separation." Given that thanatos has been defined throughout Scripture as cessation and return to dust, what does calling this "death" suggest about the nature of the final outcome?

    4. Read Matthew 10:28. Jesus says God can "destroy [apollymi] both soul [psyche] and body in gehenna." Does Jesus describe what happens to the wicked in gehenna as preservation or destruction?

    Section G: Counter-Arguments and Cross-Referencing¶

    1. Some argue that Genesis 2:17 refers to "spiritual death" (separation from God) rather than physical death, since Adam did not physically die that day. Evaluate this claim against three pieces of evidence:

      • What does Gen 3:19 say the threatened death is?
      • What does mot tamut mean in every other Torah usage?
      • What does "in the day" (beyom) mean in Gen 2:4?

    2. Some argue that "dead in trespasses and sins" (Eph 2:1) proves that "death" in the Bible can mean conscious existence apart from God, and that this meaning should apply to the second death. Consider: if "dead" in Eph 2:1 means "consciously existing apart from God," then the metaphor "dead while she liveth" (1 Tim 5:6) would mean "consciously existing apart from God while consciously existing apart from God." Does the metaphor work if death does not mean cessation?

    3. Read Revelation 20:10 carefully. The three subjects are the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. The beast and false prophet are symbolic entities from Revelation 13. Can this verse be used as evidence for what happens to human beings in the lake of fire without extending its subjects beyond what the text states?
