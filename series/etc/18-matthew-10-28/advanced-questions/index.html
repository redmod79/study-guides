    Study 18: Matthew 10:28 -- Advanced Study¶

    Same-Author Usage and the Fate of the Wicked

    Evidence Tiers

    This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:

    1. Explicit (E) -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
    2. Necessary Implication (N) -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
    3. Inference (I) -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be added beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.

    The hierarchy matters: Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference. An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

    Section A: Matthew's Psyche (G5590) Pattern¶

    1. Matthew uses psyche (G5590) 16 times across 11 verses. Eight occurrences are translated "life" (2:20; 6:25 2x; 10:39 2x; 16:25 2x; 20:28) and eight are translated "soul(s)" (10:28 2x; 11:29; 12:18; 16:26 2x; 22:37; 26:38). Examine this distribution. What does the even 50/50 split between "life" and "soul" tell you about the semantic range of psyche in Matthew's vocabulary?

    2. Read Matthew 10:28 and Matthew 10:39 together. Both are in the same discourse (Jesus' commissioning of the Twelve). Both use the identical word pair psyche + apollymi. In 10:28, the pair is translated "destroy the soul." In 10:39, the same pair is translated "lose his life." What does same-author, same-discourse usage of an identical word pair tell you about what the words mean?

    3. Read Matthew 16:25-26. In verse 25, psyche is translated "life" twice. In verse 26, psyche is translated "soul" twice. This transition occurs in consecutive verses of the same discourse. Does this support reading "soul" as a distinct metaphysical concept, or does it suggest Matthew uses psyche fluidly for "the living self/person"?

    4. Read Matthew 2:20 ("they are dead which sought the young child's life [psyche]") and Matthew 20:28 ("to give his life [psyche] a ransom for many"). In both cases, psyche refers to physical life that can be sought, taken, or given. How do these "life" usages inform the meaning of psyche in 10:28?

    Section B: Matthew's Apollymi (G622) Pattern¶

    1. Matthew uses apollymi (G622) approximately 18 times across 16 verses. Categorize these uses into three groups: (a) God/authority figures destroying the wicked (10:28; 21:41; 22:7), (b) the "lose one's life" usage (10:39; 16:25), and (c) other uses (lost sheep: 10:6; 15:24; 18:11; wineskins: 9:17; perish in storm: 8:25; etc.). What does each group reveal about the semantic range of apollymi?

    2. Focus on the judgment-context group: Matthew 10:28 (God destroys soul and body in gehenna), Matthew 21:41 (vineyard lord "miserably destroys" wicked tenants), Matthew 22:7 (king "destroyed" murderers and burned their city). When Matthew uses apollymi for divine or royal judgment against the wicked, does it consistently mean actual destruction/killing, or does it carry a "torment" sense in any of these passages?

    3. Read Matthew 9:17. Apollymi is used for wineskins that burst -- they "perish." The wineskins are ruined: the material persists but they cease to function. This is often cited as evidence that apollymi means "ruin, not annihilation." How does this non-judgment usage compare with the judgment usages in 10:28, 21:41, and 22:7? Is the wineskin context (household objects) the same type of context as divine judgment against the wicked?

    4. The verb in Matt 10:28 shifts from apokteino (kill outright, for men's action) to apollymi (destroy, for God's action). Both are aorist active infinitives -- punctiliar aspect, describing completed actions. What does the verb shift signal about the qualitative difference between human killing and divine destroying? What does the aorist aspect indicate about the duration of the action?

    Section C: Matthew's Gehenna (G1067) Pattern¶

    1. Matthew contains 7 of the 12 total NT gehenna occurrences (58%). Examine the vocabulary paired with gehenna in each Matthean passage:

      Passage Vocabulary Paired with Gehenna
      Matt 5:22 ___
      Matt 5:29-30 ___
      Matt 10:28 ___
      Matt 18:9 ___
      Matt 23:15 ___
      Matt 23:33 ___

      Does any Matthean gehenna passage use basanizo (torment), basanismos (torment-noun), or any conscious-ongoing-existence vocabulary?

    2. Read Matthew 18:9 alongside Mark 9:43, 45, 47. In all of these passages, what is consistently contrasted with gehenna? What does this "enter into life" vs. gehenna pattern suggest about what gehenna represents?

    3. Extend the search beyond Matthew: examine all 12 NT gehenna occurrences (Matt 5:22, 29-30; 10:28; 18:9; 23:15, 33; Mark 9:43, 45, 47; Luke 12:5; James 3:6). Does any gehenna passage in the entire NT use torment vocabulary (basanizo, basanismos) for human beings? What is the significance of this complete absence?

    Section D: The Synoptic Parallel -- Luke 12:4-5¶

    1. Compare the two accounts side by side:

      Element Matthew 10:28 Luke 12:4-5
      What men can do ___ ___
      What men cannot do ___ ___
      What God can do ___ ___
      Vocabulary for God's action ___ ___
      Reference to "soul" ___ ___

      What are the key differences? What does Luke's omission of both apollymi and psyche suggest?

    2. Luke 12:5 uses the construction meta to apokteinai ("after he hath killed") followed by "hath power to cast into gehenna." This is a temporal sequence: killing precedes casting into gehenna. How does this two-step process compare with Matthew's single-action description of God destroying "both soul and body in gehenna"?

    3. Both Matthew and Luke record this teaching, yet they use different vocabulary. Matthew uses apollymi (destroy); Luke uses apokteino (kill) + emballo (cast into). Despite the vocabulary differences, do the two accounts agree or disagree about whether gehenna involves torment? What vocabulary is absent from both accounts?

    Section E: Greek Grammar and the Phobeo (G5399) Framework¶

    1. The word phobeo (G5399, "fear") appears three times in Matt 10:28 (twice as negated imperative, once as positive imperative). Jesus says: do not fear those who kill the body; rather, fear Him who can destroy soul and body in gehenna. What is the logical structure of the fear-contrast? Does the text specify why God's action is more fearful -- because of duration (ongoing torment) or because of scope (whole person, not just body)?

    2. The correlative kai...kai ("both...and") construction in "both soul and body" emphasizes totality. What does this construction tell you about the scope of God's destruction? Is anything left over after God destroys "both soul and body"?

    3. Both apokteino and apollymi in Matt 10:28 are aorist active infinitives. The aorist aspect in Greek is punctiliar -- it views the action as a completed event, not an ongoing process. If the destruction of soul and body in gehenna is described with punctiliar aspect, what does this suggest about whether the destruction is a completed event or an ongoing process?

    Section F: ECT Counterarguments¶

    1. ECT Argument 1: "Not able to kill the soul" (Matt 10:28) implies body-soul dualism -- the soul is a separable, conscious entity that survives bodily death. Evaluate this argument against the following evidence:

      • Matthew uses psyche for "life" 8 times and "soul" 8 times (E448)
      • The same word pair (psyche + apollymi) means "lose one's life" in 10:39, same discourse (E449)
      • Luke's parallel omits psyche entirely (E456)
      • Ezekiel 18:4, 20: "the soul that sinneth, it shall die" (E016) Does the text require body-soul dualism, or is there an alternative reading consistent with Matthew's usage pattern?

    2. ECT Argument 2: Apollymi means "ruin" not "annihilate," based on the wineskins passage (Matt 9:17) -- the material persists but in a ruined state. Evaluate this against the judgment usages of apollymi in Matthew (10:28; 21:41; 22:7). Which context -- household objects or divine judgment -- is more relevant for determining what apollymi means in a divine judgment passage?

    3. ECT Argument 3: The warning structure of Matt 10:28 (fear God more than men) implies gehenna must involve something worse than death -- and mere cessation would not be qualitatively worse. Where does this reasoning come from -- the text itself, or a philosophical framework about what constitutes "worse"? Does the text specify the nature of the "worse," or could greater scope (whole person vs. body only) and greater finality (permanent vs. temporary) account for the greater fear?
