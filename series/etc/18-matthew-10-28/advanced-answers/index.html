    Study 18: Matthew 10:28 -- Advanced Study Answer Key¶

    Same-Author Usage and the Fate of the Wicked

    Evidence Tiers

    This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:

    1. Explicit (E) -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
    2. Necessary Implication (N) -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
    3. Inference (I) -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be added beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.

    The hierarchy matters: Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference. An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

    Section A: Matthew's Psyche (G5590) Pattern¶

    1. Matthew's 16 uses of psyche -- the 50/50 split.

    The even distribution (8 as "life," 8 as "soul") tells us that psyche is not a technical term in Matthew's vocabulary that consistently refers to one thing. It is a flexible word meaning "the living self/person" that English translators render differently depending on context. The 50/50 split makes it impossible to claim that psyche in Matthew exclusively or even primarily means "an immaterial soul." It means "life" just as often.

    The 8 "life" occurrences: 2:20 (child's life sought); 6:25 2x (do not worry about your life); 10:39 2x (lose/find life); 16:25 2x (lose/save life); 20:28 (give his life a ransom). The 8 "soul" occurrences: 10:28 2x (kill/destroy the soul); 11:29 (rest for your souls); 12:18 (my soul is well pleased); 16:26 2x (forfeit his soul); 22:37 (love God with all your soul); 26:38 (my soul is sorrowful).

    2. Matt 10:28 and 10:39 -- identical word pair, same discourse.

    This is the single most significant datum for interpreting Matt 10:28. The identical word pair psyche + apollymi appears in both verses, separated by only 11 verses, within the same continuous discourse (Jesus' commissioning speech to the Twelve, Matt 10:5-42). In 10:39, the pair unambiguously means "lose one's life." No reader of 10:39 understands it as "destroy an immaterial soul." Same-author, same-discourse usage of an identical word pair is the strongest possible linguistic evidence for what the words mean. Matthew's own usage in the immediate context treats psyche as "life/self" and apollymi as "lose/forfeit."

    "He that findeth his life [psyche] shall lose [apollymi] it: and he that loseth [apollymi] his life [psyche] for my sake shall find it." -- Matthew 10:39

    3. Matt 16:25-26 -- "life" to "soul" in consecutive verses.

    The same Greek word psyche transitions from "life" (v.25) to "soul" (v.26) within consecutive verses of the same discourse, with no signal that Matthew intends a different referent. This strongly supports reading psyche as a fluid term for "the living self/person" rather than a technical metaphysical category. If Matthew intended "soul" in v.26 to mean something categorically different from "life" in v.25, he would need to signal that shift. He does not.

    *4. Matt 2:20 and 20:28 -- physical life.

    In 2:20, those who "sought the young child's psyche" were Herod's agents seeking to physically kill Jesus. In 20:28, Jesus came to "give his psyche a ransom" -- He gave His physical life on the cross. Both usages are unmistakably about physical, bodily life. These inform 10:28 by establishing that when Matthew uses psyche, he frequently means the whole living person's life, not a separable metaphysical component.

    Section B: Matthew's Apollymi (G622) Pattern¶

    5. Three categories of Matthew's apollymi usage.

    (a) God/authority destroying the wicked (judgment context): - 10:28: God "destroys" soul and body in gehenna - 21:41: Vineyard lord will "miserably destroy" the wicked tenants - 22:7: King "destroyed" the murderers and burned their city

    In all three, apollymi describes actual destruction/killing. None appends torment vocabulary.

    (b) "Lose one's life" usage: - 10:39: "He that findeth his life shall lose [apollymi] it" - 16:25: "Whosoever will save his life shall lose [apollymi] it"

    Here apollymi means "forfeit, lose" -- the life is given up or forfeited.

    (c) Other uses: - 10:6; 15:24; 18:11: "Lost [apollymi] sheep" -- those on a trajectory toward destruction, whom Jesus came to save - 9:17: Wineskins "perish [apollymi]" -- burst and cease to function (ruin) - 8:25: "Lord, save us: we perish [apollymi]" -- facing death in the storm - 2:13: Herod seeks to "destroy [apollymi]" the child -- kill Him - 12:14: Pharisees plot to "destroy [apollymi]" Jesus -- kill Him - 26:52: "They that take the sword shall perish [apollymi] with the sword" -- die

    The semantic range spans from "lose/forfeit" to "ruin" to "destroy/kill." In every judgment or lethal context, the meaning is actual destruction or death.

    6. Judgment-context apollymi in Matthew.

    In all three judgment-context passages, apollymi means actual destruction: - 10:28: God destroys the whole person in gehenna - 21:41: The tenants are destroyed (killed) as punishment - 22:7: The murderers are destroyed and their city is burned

    No judgment-context use of apollymi in Matthew carries a "torment" sense. No passage appends torment vocabulary to the destruction. The pattern is consistent: when Matthew uses apollymi for divine judgment against the wicked, it means actual destruction.

    7. The wineskin argument -- Matt 9:17.

    The wineskins passage (9:17) demonstrates the "ruin" end of apollymi's semantic range: the wineskins burst, the material persists, but they cease to function as wineskins. This is a valid sense of the word. However, the context is entirely different: household objects in a non-judgment illustration vs. divine eschatological judgment against the wicked. The judgment usages (10:28; 21:41; 22:7) consistently mean actual destruction/killing -- not "ruin while continuing to exist."

    Applying the wineskin sense to 10:28 requires selecting the least representative context (physical objects in a domestic illustration) and importing it into the most consequential context (God's final judgment). Same-author usage in the same type of context (judgment) is a more reliable guide than same-author usage in a different type of context (household illustration).

    8. The verb shift (apokteino to apollymi) and aorist aspect.

    The shift from apokteino (kill outright, used for men's action on the body) to apollymi (destroy fully, used for God's action on soul and body) signals a qualitative difference. Men can only kill the body -- a partial destruction. God can destroy the entire person -- soul and body together. The verb change marks the difference in scope, not a difference in kind (killing vs. torment).

    Both verbs are aorist active infinitives. The aorist aspect in Greek is punctiliar -- it views the action as a completed event, a single occurrence, not an ongoing or continuous process. If Jesus intended to describe an ongoing process of torment, the present tense (continuous aspect) would be the natural choice. The aorist describes God's destruction of soul and body as a completed action.

    Section C: Matthew's Gehenna (G1067) Pattern¶

    9. Vocabulary paired with gehenna in Matthew.

    Passage Vocabulary Paired with Gehenna
    Matt 5:22 Krisis (judgment) -- "in danger of hell fire"
    Matt 5:29-30 Apollymi (perish) -- one member perishes vs. whole body cast into gehenna
    Matt 10:28 Apollymi (destroy) -- destroy soul and body in gehenna
    Matt 18:9 Contrasted with "enter into life" -- life vs. gehenna fire
    Matt 23:15 "Child of hell" (huios geennes) -- belonging/destination language
    Matt 23:33 Krisis (judgment/damnation) -- "how can ye escape the damnation of hell?"

    No Matthean gehenna passage uses basanizo (torment), basanismos (torment-noun), or any vocabulary describing conscious ongoing existence. Every passage uses destruction or judgment vocabulary. This is not a matter of one or two passages happening to omit torment language -- it is a complete, consistent pattern across all 7 occurrences in a single author who accounts for 58% of all NT gehenna references.

    10. The "enter into life" vs. gehenna pattern.

    Matthew 18:9 presents the choice as "enter into life" vs. "be cast into hell fire." Mark 9:43, 45, 47 repeat the same pattern: "enter into life" vs. "go into hell." The consistent contrast is life vs. gehenna -- not one kind of existence vs. another. If gehenna involved ongoing conscious existence (even in torment), the contrast with "life" would be incoherent: both outcomes would involve continued existence. The contrast works only if "life" and gehenna represent genuinely opposite outcomes.

    11. All 12 NT gehenna occurrences -- torment vocabulary search.

    The complete list: Matt 5:22, 29, 30; 10:28; 18:9; 23:15, 33; Mark 9:43, 45, 47; Luke 12:5; James 3:6. Not one of these 12 passages uses basanizo (torment) or basanismos (torment-noun) for human beings in gehenna. This is a complete absence across 4 NT authors (Matthew, Mark, Luke, James). The significance: the claim that gehenna involves eternal conscious torment cannot be sourced from any gehenna passage. It must be imported from passages that use different vocabulary (basanizo) in a different genre (apocalyptic: Rev 14:10-11; 20:10) about different subjects (beast-worshippers, devil/beast/false prophet).

    Section D: The Synoptic Parallel -- Luke 12:4-5¶

    12. Side-by-side comparison.

    Element Matthew 10:28 Luke 12:4-5
    What men can do Kill the body Kill the body
    What men cannot do Kill the soul (psyche) "After that have no more that they can do"
    What God can do Destroy (apollymi) both soul and body in gehenna After killing (apokteino), cast (emballo) into gehenna
    Vocabulary for God's action Apollymi (destroy) Apokteino (kill) + emballo (cast into)
    Reference to "soul" Yes -- psyche mentioned twice No -- psyche completely absent

    Key differences: (1) Luke omits apollymi entirely, using apokteino instead; (2) Luke omits psyche entirely -- no reference to the "soul" at all; (3) Luke specifies a temporal sequence (kill first, then cast into gehenna). Luke's omission of psyche suggests that the "soul" concept is not essential to the teaching. The point is about God's total authority over human destiny, not about the nature of the soul.

    13. Luke's two-step process vs. Matthew's single action.

    Luke describes a sequence: meta to apokteinai ("after the killing") + "hath power to cast into gehenna." Killing is complete before the casting into gehenna occurs. This temporal structure makes ongoing conscious torment in gehenna difficult to sustain: the person is already dead before being cast in.

    Matthew describes a single comprehensive action: God destroys "both soul and body" in gehenna. The kai...kai construction emphasizes that nothing is left over -- the destruction encompasses the entire person.

    Both accounts describe final, total judgment. Neither uses torment vocabulary. They differ in structure (single action vs. two-step process) but converge on the same outcome: God's judgment in gehenna is destructive, not preservative.

    14. Vocabulary absent from both accounts.

    Despite their vocabulary differences, both Matthew and Luke agree that gehenna does not involve torment. Matthew uses apollymi (destroy); Luke uses apokteino (kill) + emballo (cast into). Neither uses basanizo (torment), basanismos (torment-noun), or any word suggesting ongoing conscious existence. This is particularly significant because both authors had these words available in their vocabulary (Matthew uses basanizo in 8:6 and 8:29 for physical torment and demonic protest; Luke uses it in 8:28 and 16:23-28 in the parable of the rich man). They chose not to use torment vocabulary when describing gehenna.

    Section E: Greek Grammar and the Phobeo (G5399) Framework¶

    15. The fear-contrast structure.

    The logical structure is: do not fear X (men, who can only kill the body); rather, fear Y (God, who can destroy both soul and body in gehenna). The text specifies why God's action is more fearful by describing its scope: God destroys "both soul and body" -- the whole person -- whereas men can only destroy the body. The text does not say "fear God because His torment lasts forever." It says "fear God because He can destroy the entire person." The greater fear is grounded in greater scope and finality, not in duration of suffering.

    16. The kai...kai ("both...and") construction.

    The correlative kai...kai emphasizes totality: both soul and body. This construction means nothing is excluded from the destruction. After God destroys "both soul and body," there is no remainder -- no surviving component that continues to exist in torment. The emphasis on totality reinforces the completeness of the destruction, not its duration.

    17. Aorist aspect and duration.

    Both apokteino (kill) and apollymi (destroy) in Matt 10:28 are aorist active infinitives. The aorist aspect views an action as a complete, punctiliar event -- a single occurrence viewed as a whole, not an ongoing or repeated process. If Jesus had intended to describe an ongoing process of destruction or torment, the present infinitive (continuous aspect) would be the expected choice. The aorist indicates that the destruction of soul and body in gehenna is a completed action, not an ongoing one.

    This aligns with Luke's temporal sequence (after killing, cast into gehenna) and with the kai...kai totality construction. All three grammatical features point in the same direction: God's judgment in gehenna is a completed, total destruction.

    Section F: ECT Counterarguments¶

    18. ECT Argument 1 -- Body-soul dualism from "not able to kill the soul."

    The text states men cannot kill the psyche. Whether this implies body-soul dualism depends on which of two readings one chooses:

    (a) "Psyche as separable conscious soul": The soul is an immaterial entity that survives bodily death. Men can kill the body, but the soul lives on. This reading requires importing body-soul dualism -- a concept the text does not state.

    (b) "Psyche as the life/self that only God can end": Men can kill the body, but only God controls the person's ultimate destiny -- their psyche (life/self). This reading follows naturally from Matthew's own usage pattern: psyche = "life" in 50% of uses (E448); the same word pair = "lose life" in 10:39 (E449); Luke omits psyche entirely (E456); "the soul that sins shall die" (E016, Ezek 18:4, 20).

    The evidence distribution is heavily weighted against the dualism reading. The FOR evidence consists of one Contextually Clear item (E028 itself, which is ambiguous between the two readings). The AGAINST evidence includes multiple Plain items (E449, E016, E448, N066) that do not require interpretation. The same-author pattern governs the interpretation: psyche in 10:28 carries the same semantic weight as psyche in 10:39, 16:25, and the broader Matthean pattern.

    19. ECT Argument 2 -- Apollymi as "ruin" based on wineskins (Matt 9:17).

    The wineskin usage demonstrates one end of apollymi's semantic range: ruin (the material persists but the function is lost). The judgment usages demonstrate the other end: actual destruction/killing. The question is which sense applies in Matt 10:28 -- a divine judgment passage.

    The FOR evidence for the "ruin" reading consists of one Contextually Clear item (E452 -- wineskins in a non-judgment context). The AGAINST evidence consists of multiple Plain items: judgment apollymi = actual destruction in 21:41 and 22:7 (E451); apollymi is defined as "to destroy fully" (E262); gehenna is paired with destruction vocabulary, never torment vocabulary (E453); judgment apollymi is never followed by torment vocabulary (N067).

    The "ruin" reading requires selecting the least representative sense of apollymi (physical objects in a domestic illustration) and applying it to a judgment context where the word consistently means actual destruction. Same-author, same-context-type usage is a more reliable interpretive guide than same-author, different-context-type usage.

    20. ECT Argument 3 -- Cessation is "not worse enough" to justify the fear-contrast.

    This argument reasons that mere cessation of existence cannot be qualitatively worse than death, so gehenna must involve something beyond death (i.e., ongoing torment) to justify the "fear God more" warning. This reasoning comes from outside the text -- it is a philosophical framework about what constitutes "worse," not an exegetical observation about what the text says.

    The text itself specifies what makes God's judgment more fearful: scope (both soul and body, not just the body) and authority (God can do what men cannot). Greater scope and greater finality already account for the greater fear without requiring ongoing torment. Permanent, total, irreversible destruction of the entire person -- including the psyche that men cannot touch -- is qualitatively different from (and worse than) the temporary killing that men can inflict.

    This argument is classified as I-C (External Framework): it does not override any explicit statement, but adds a philosophical premise that the text does not contain.

    Evidence Classification Exercise -- Answers¶

    1. "God can destroy both soul and body in gehenna" (Matt 10:28) - Explicit (E). This directly quotes the verse. The text says exactly this.

    2. "Matthew uses the identical word pair psyche + apollymi to mean 'lose one's life' in 10:39, 11 verses after 10:28 in the same discourse" - Explicit (E). This is an observable textual fact. Matt 10:39 uses psyche and apollymi together, and the meaning "lose one's life" is unambiguous in that verse.

    3. "No gehenna passage in any NT author pairs gehenna with torment vocabulary" - Necessary Implication (N). This is an observable vocabulary distribution across all 12 NT gehenna passages. Both sides can verify this is what the text contains. No interpretation is required -- it is a factual observation about what vocabulary is present and absent.

    4. "Matt 10:28 teaches that the wicked will be destroyed (cease to exist) in gehenna" - Inference (I) -- I-A (Evidence-Extending). This systematizes multiple E/N items (E028: destroy soul and body; E451: judgment apollymi = destruction; E453: gehenna + destruction vocabulary; E449: same word pair = "lose life"; N067: judgment apollymi pattern) into a doctrinal claim. No single verse says "the wicked will cease to exist." All components are text-derived; the inference adds only the systematization. Criteria: #5 (systematizing).

    5. "'Not able to kill the soul' implies body-soul dualism" - Inference (I) -- I-B (Competing Evidence). FOR: E028 (men cannot kill the soul -- implies something survives bodily death). AGAINST: E448 (psyche = "life" in 50% of Matthew uses); E449 (same word pair = "lose life" in 10:39); E456 (Luke omits psyche); E016 (the soul that sins shall die); N005 (the soul is mortal). The claim requires choosing between two readings of psyche (separable soul vs. life/self) and importing body-soul dualism. Criteria: #2 (choosing between readings), #1 (adding body-soul dualism).

    6. "Apollymi means 'ruin' not 'annihilate' -- soul and body continue in torment" - Inference (I) -- I-B (Competing Evidence). FOR: E452 (wineskins "perish" = ruin in 9:17). AGAINST: E451 (judgment apollymi = actual destruction in 21:41; 22:7); E262 (apollymi = "to destroy fully"); E453 (gehenna + destruction, never + torment); N067 (judgment apollymi consistently = destruction). The claim requires choosing the "ruin" sense (attested in a non-judgment context) over the "destroy" sense (attested in judgment contexts) and adding "continue to exist in ongoing torment" -- something the text does not say. Criteria: #2 (choosing between readings), #1 (adding ongoing torment).

    7. "The warning structure implies ongoing conscious suffering -- cessation is not worse enough" - Inference (I) -- I-C (External Framework). The reasoning that cessation is "not worse enough" to justify the fear-contrast comes from outside the text. The text specifies scope (both soul and body) and authority (God can do what men cannot) as the basis for greater fear. The "not worse enough" premise is a philosophical judgment, not an exegetical observation. Criteria: #3 (external philosophical framework).

    What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said -- Answers¶

    What CAN be said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies):

    1. The text states God can destroy (apollymi) both soul (psyche) and body (soma) in gehenna (E028, Matt 10:28).

    2. The text states the same word pair (psyche + apollymi) means "lose one's life" in Matt 10:39, 11 verses later in the same discourse (E449, N066).

    3. The text states Matthew uses psyche for both "life" and "soul" in equal proportion; the word carries the sense of "the living self/person" (E448, E450).

    4. The text states the verb changes from apokteino (kill, for men) to apollymi (destroy, for God) in Matt 10:28 (E454).

    5. The text states no gehenna passage in any author pairs gehenna with torment vocabulary (E453, N068).

    6. The text states the soul that sins shall die (E016); the soul is mortal -- it can be destroyed (N005).

    7. The text states Luke's parallel (12:4-5) omits both apollymi and psyche, describing God as killing and casting into gehenna (E456, E457).

    8. The text states both verbs in Matt 10:28 are aorist infinitives -- punctiliar, completed action (E455).

    What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied -- requires adding something):

    1. The text does not state that the soul is immortal or indestructible. Matt 10:28 explicitly says God can destroy the soul.

    2. The text does not state that apollymi means "annihilate" (complete non-existence of all components). The word means "destroy fully" with a semantic range that includes ruin.

    3. The text does not state that apollymi means "ruin while continuing to exist in torment." No gehenna passage appends torment vocabulary.

    4. The text does not state whether the "soul" in 10:28 is a separable conscious entity or the person's life/self. Luke's parallel omits the concept; Matthew's usage pattern supports both readings to varying degrees.

    5. The text does not state that gehenna involves ongoing conscious torment. No gehenna passage uses basanizo or basanismos for human beings.

    6. The text does not state that the wicked will cease to exist. The text says God can destroy them; the duration and nature of that destruction is inferred, not stated.

    7. The text does not state the precise mechanism by which God destroys soul and body in gehenna.
