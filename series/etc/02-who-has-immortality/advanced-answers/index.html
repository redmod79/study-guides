    Study 2: Who Has Immortality? — Advanced Study Answer Key¶

    Immortality Vocabulary, Word Studies, and Evidence Classification

    Evidence Tiers

    This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:

    1. Explicit (E) — The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
    2. Necessary Implication (N) — No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed — every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
    3. Inference (I) — Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be added beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.

    The hierarchy matters: Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference. An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

    Section A: The Greek Word for "Immortality" — Athanasia (G110)¶

    1. Athanasia appears only 3 times in the entire New Testament. Look up all three occurrences: 1 Timothy 6:16, 1 Corinthians 15:53, and 1 Corinthians 15:54. In each case, who possesses athanasia?

    • 1 Timothy 6:16: God "only hath" athanasia — it is God's present, exclusive possession.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:53: "This mortal must put on" athanasia — believers will receive it at the resurrection. They do not have it now.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:54: "When this mortal shall have put on" athanasia — the same future event described as accomplished.

    In all three occurrences, athanasia is either (a) God's present exclusive possession or (b) a future acquisition for believers at the resurrection. Zero occurrences apply it to humans as a present possession.

    2. In 1 Timothy 6:16, the word monos modifies who has athanasia. The same word monos appears in 1 Timothy 1:17 and John 17:3. In those cases, does monos carry a qualified or absolute sense?

    In 1 Timothy 1:17 ("the only wise God") and John 17:3 ("the only true God"), monos is understood absolutely — God is not "the only self-existent wise God" with room for other wise gods, nor "the only true God in a special sense." Monos marks exclusivity. The same word governing "hath immortality" in 1 Timothy 6:16 should be read with the same absolute force: God alone has it.

    3. In 1 Corinthians 15:53-54, the verb is enduo. Paul uses the same verb elsewhere. In every case, does enduo describe acquiring something one already has?

    No. Enduo always describes putting on something not currently possessed or worn: - Romans 13:14: "Put on the Lord Jesus Christ" — from a state of not being clothed with Christ. - Galatians 3:27: "Put on Christ" — at baptism, a new state. - Ephesians 6:11: "Put on the whole armour of God" — from unarmed to armed. - Colossians 3:10: "Put on the new man" — from old to new.

    The mortal "putting on" immortality describes a transition from not having it to having it — not the reaffirmation of something already possessed.

    Section B: Incorruptibility — Aphtharsia (G861) and Aphthartos (G862)¶

    4. Look up all 8 NT occurrences of aphtharsia. In how many is it described as a present human possession?

    • Romans 2:7: Must be sought — not a present possession.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:42: The body is raised in incorruption — future event.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:50: Corruption cannot inherit incorruption — the two are mutually exclusive states.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:53: The corruptible must put on incorruption — future transformation.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:54: "When this corruptible shall have put on" incorruption — future event.
    • Ephesians 6:24: Grace to those who love Christ "in sincerity/incorruption" — modifies the love, not the person's nature.
    • 2 Timothy 1:10: Christ brought life and incorruption to light through the gospel — revealed, not inherent.
    • Titus 2:7: "In doctrine showing incorruptness" — integrity of teaching.

    Zero occurrences describe aphtharsia as an inherent present human possession. It is consistently something to seek, something that will be put on, or something Christ revealed.

    5. Look up all 7 NT occurrences of aphthartos. To whom or what is it applied? Is it ever applied to the human soul?

    • Romans 1:23: Describes God — "the uncorruptible God."
    • 1 Corinthians 9:25: An "incorruptible crown" — the believer's future reward.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:52: "The dead shall be raised incorruptible" — future transformation.
    • 1 Timothy 1:17: Describes God — "immortal, invisible."
    • 1 Peter 1:4: An "incorruptible inheritance" reserved in heaven — a future possession.
    • 1 Peter 1:23: "Incorruptible seed" — the word of God, not the human soul.
    • 1 Peter 3:4: "That which is not corruptible" — the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit (describing a quality of character, not asserting ontological immortality of the soul).

    Aphthartos is never applied to the human soul as an inherent attribute. It describes God (2x), future rewards/inheritance (2x), the resurrection state (1x), the word of God (1x), and a quality of spirit (1x).

    6. In Romans 1:23, aphthartos describes God and phthartos describes man — in the same verse. What interpretive move is required to maintain that humans possess an incorruptible component?

    One must argue that Paul is describing only the body as corruptible while an unstated, unmentioned soul is incorruptible. This requires adding a body/soul distinction that Paul does not make in the verse. Paul's contrast is between God and man — not between God and the human body. The interpretive move is an inference that imports a framework (body-soul dualism) not present in the text.

    Section C: Mortality — Thnetos (G2349) and Corruption — Phthora (G5356)¶

    7. Look up all 6 NT occurrences of thnetos. In each passage, what is described as mortal?

    • Romans 6:12: "Your mortal body (soma)."
    • Romans 8:11: "Your mortal bodies."
    • 2 Corinthians 4:11: "Our mortal flesh (sarx)."
    • 2 Corinthians 5:4: "Mortality" (to thneton) — used absolutely, no noun specified.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:53: "This mortal" (touto to thneton) — used absolutely.
    • 1 Corinthians 15:54: "This mortal" — used absolutely.

    In three passages, thnetos modifies "body" or "flesh." In three passages, it is used absolutely — "this mortal" or "mortality" — without restricting the referent to the body alone.

    8. In 1 Corinthians 15:53, Paul writes "this mortal" without the noun "body." Compare with Romans 6:12 where he writes "your mortal body." What is the significance?

    When Paul wants to specify the body, he does so explicitly (Rom 6:12: "mortal body"; Rom 8:11: "mortal bodies"; 2 Cor 4:11: "mortal flesh"). In 1 Corinthians 15:53, he uses the absolute form — "this mortal" (touto to thneton) — without specifying "body." The absolute form describes the whole mortal entity, not a specific part. Paul's own usage shows he is capable of restricting the referent to the body when he intends to. Here he does not.

    9. Look up key occurrences of phthora. In Galatians 6:8, what is the alternative to "reaping corruption"?

    "He that soweth to the flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption (phthora); but he that soweth to the Spirit shall of the Spirit reap life everlasting." — Galatians 6:8

    The alternative to corruption is "life everlasting." The contrast is exhaustive and mutually exclusive — corruption or everlasting life. There is no third option of "corruption plus continued conscious existence." This parallels Romans 6:23 (death vs. eternal life) and John 3:16 (perish vs. everlasting life).

    10. Read 2 Peter 2:12. How many destruction-related words does Peter use? What does the comparison to animals suggest?

    Peter uses three destruction terms in a single verse: phtheiro ("destroyed"), kataphtheiro ("utterly perish"), and phthora ("corruption"). He compares false teachers to "natural brute beasts, made to be taken and destroyed." The comparison to animals — whose destruction is physical and final — suggests the same kind of destruction: cessation, not ongoing conscious torment.

    Section D: The Conditional Structure of Eternal Life¶

    11. Read John 3:16, 3:36, 5:40, 6:53, 10:28, and 1 John 5:11-12. What is the common condition? What happens without it?

    The common condition is relationship with Christ — believing in him, coming to him, having him. Without it: - John 3:16: the person perishes (apollymi). - John 3:36: "shall not see life." - John 5:40: they lack life — "that ye might have life" (implying they do not). - John 6:53: "ye have no life in you." - John 10:28: perishing is the negated alternative — "they shall never perish" (implying perishing is the default without Christ). - 1 John 5:12: "he that hath not the Son of God hath not life."

    Six explicit statements from two authors (Jesus and John) present the same pattern: life is through Christ; without Christ, the person does not have life. None of these texts describes the alternative as continued conscious existence.

    12. Read Romans 6:23 and Galatians 6:8 side by side. List both pairs of outcomes.

    • Romans 6:23: death vs. eternal life.
    • Galatians 6:8: corruption vs. everlasting life.

    In neither text is a third option mentioned. The contrasts are exhaustive: one or the other. Both pairs present the same structure — the outcome of sin is cessation/decay; the gift of God through Christ is life.

    13. Read 2 Thessalonians 1:9. Does the text describe unending conscious existence, or unending destruction?

    The text says "everlasting destruction" (olethros aionios). The noun olethros means ruin or destruction. The adjective aionios modifies the destruction, not the consciousness. The text does not say "everlasting conscious torment" or "everlasting punishing" — it says "everlasting destruction." (This passage receives more detailed study in a later etc6 study.)

    14. Read John 5:26. What does this suggest about whether humans have life inherently?

    The Father "hath life in himself" and "hath given to the Son to have life in himself." The phrase "life in himself" describes self-existent life — life that is not derived from another source. Even the Son receives this by the Father's gift. If self-existent life belongs to God and is given to Christ by special act, there is no basis for claiming humans possess it by nature.

    Section E: The Absence of "Immortal Soul"¶

    15. Search a concordance for "immortal soul." How many occurrences exist?

    Zero. The phrase "immortal soul" does not appear anywhere in the Old or New Testament. No verse states "the soul is immortal," "the soul cannot die," or "the soul lives forever." The concept must be constructed entirely from inference — and the inferences required conflict with verses that directly state the soul can die (Ezek 18:4) and be destroyed (Matt 10:28).

    16. Read Ezekiel 18:4 and Matthew 10:28. What must be done to "die" and "destroy" to maintain the immortal-soul claim?

    • Ezekiel 18:4: "The soul that sinneth, it shall die" — mut in Hebrew. To maintain that the soul is immortal, "die" must be redefined to mean something other than death (typically "spiritual death" or "separation from God").
    • Matthew 10:28: "Fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell" — apollymi in Greek. To maintain the soul is indestructible, "destroy" must be redefined to mean something other than destruction (typically "ruin without cessation" or "make miserable").

    Both redefinitions require overriding the plain lexical meaning of the words. This is why the claim of an immortal soul is classified as I-D (counter-evidence inference) — it requires the text to mean something other than what it says.

    17. Read Psalm 49:7-9, 12, 15, 20. How does the psalmist describe the fate of "man that is in honour"?

    The psalmist says "man being in honour abideth not: he is like the beasts that perish" (v.12, 20). The comparison to animals that perish equates the human fate with animal death. However, the psalmist also states an exception: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave" (v.15). Redemption from death is divine intervention, not inherent survival. The natural state of man is to perish like the beasts; God's redemption is what saves the soul from that fate.

    Section F: Counter-Arguments and Competing Readings¶

    18. "God only hath immortality" means God alone has self-existent immortality — leaving room for humans to have derived immortality. Does the text make this distinction?

    No. 1 Timothy 6:16 says "who only (monos) hath immortality (athanasia)." The text does not say "who only hath self-existent immortality" or "who only hath underived immortality." The derived/underived distinction is not present in the passage. It must be imported from an external theological framework. This is classified as an I-C inference (compatible external) — it does not directly override the text, but it qualifies it by adding a distinction the text does not make.

    19. Does Paul name an immortal counterpart to the mortal body?

    No. Paul calls the body mortal (Rom 6:12; 8:11; 2 Cor 4:11), and he says "this mortal must put on immortality" (1 Cor 15:53). But he never states that while the body is mortal, the soul or spirit is immortal. The claim that thnetos applies only to the body while the soul is exempt requires adding information Paul never provides. Paul never names an immortal counterpart to the mortal body anywhere in his letters.

    20. Can John's "life" concept be separated from Paul's "immortality" concept?

    John does use "has eternal life" in the present tense (John 3:36; 5:24; 6:47), indicating a present dimension to zoe aionios. However, Jesus also connects eternal life to the future resurrection repeatedly: "I will raise him up at the last day" (John 6:40, 44, 54). The attempt to separate John's "life" concept from Paul's "immortality" concept — so that "hath not life" does not bear on the immortality question — requires imposing a hermeneutical framework that the text does not prescribe. Jesus himself ties eternal life to both the present relationship and the future resurrection.

    21. Read Luke 20:37-38. Is Jesus arguing for the resurrection, or for a conscious intermediate state?

    Jesus is arguing for the resurrection. His explicit point is stated in v.37: "Now that the dead ARE RAISED, even Moses shewed at the bush." The statement "all live unto him" (v.38) functions as the theological ground for why God will raise them — God regards them as living because their resurrection is certain. The passage is not about the patriarchs' current conscious state; it is about whether the dead will be raised. Reading it as proof of present consciousness requires importing a framework Jesus does not invoke.

    Section G: Cross-Study Connections¶

    22. How does the immortality word study reinforce or challenge the Study 1 findings?

    It reinforces them completely. Study 1 found that nephesh (soul) can die (Ezek 18:4), be destroyed (Matt 10:28), and applies to both animals and humans. Study 2 adds that all 18 NT occurrences of immortality vocabulary (athanasia, aphtharsia, aphthartos) describe either God's nature or the future resurrection state — zero describe the human soul as presently immortal. The soul's mortality (Study 1) is confirmed by the total absence of immortality vocabulary applied to it (Study 2).

    23. Read 1 Corinthians 15:21-22 and Romans 5:12, 17. How did death enter?

    "By man came death" (1 Cor 15:21). "By one man's offence death reigned" (Rom 5:17). "By one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin" (Rom 5:12). Death is the result of sin, entering through Adam. Before sin, man had access to the tree of life (Gen 2:9; 3:22). After sin, he was barred from it. The human condition before the Fall included access to life; after the Fall, death reigns. This confirms that mortality is the post-Fall human condition, not the original design.

    24. Read Acts 17:18, 32. Why would the resurrection provoke mockery if Greek culture accepted the immortality of the soul?

    The Greek philosophers at Athens already accepted that the soul survived death — Platonic immortality of the soul was common in Hellenistic thought. Yet when Paul preached the resurrection, they mocked him (Acts 17:32). If the apostolic message had been "the soul is immortal and goes to heaven at death," it would have been unremarkable to Greek ears. The resurrection provoked mockery precisely because it was a different message — life after death through bodily resurrection, not through inherent soul immortality. This confirms that the apostolic message was resurrection-centered, not inherent-immortality-centered.

    Evidence Classification Exercise — Answers¶

    1. "God only hath immortality" (1 Tim 6:16) - Explicit (E). Paul directly states this in the text.

    2. "Man does not inherently possess immortality." - Necessary Implication (N). God only has immortality (1 Tim 6:16 — E). Immortality must be sought (Rom 2:7 — E). It must be put on at resurrection (1 Cor 15:53 — E). Three explicit statements converge: "only" means exclusively; "seek" means pursue what is not yet held; "must put on" means does not currently have. Every reader, regardless of theological position, must acknowledge what these words mean. The conclusion is unavoidable.

    3. "The human soul is inherently immortal and survives death as a conscious entity." - Inference (I-D: Counter-Evidence External). No verse states this. It requires: - Adding the concept "immortal soul" (absent from Scripture). - Redefining "only" in 1 Tim 6:16 to mean "only in a special sense." - Redefining "die" in Ezek 18:4 to mean something other than death. - Redefining "destroy" in Matt 10:28 to mean something other than destruction. - Importing a Platonic philosophical framework not found in Scripture. - This is the weakest type of inference because it requires overriding what the text explicitly states.

    4. "This mortal must put on immortality" (1 Cor 15:53) - Explicit (E). Paul directly states this in the text.

    5. "Immortality is acquired at the resurrection, not before." - Necessary Implication (N). 1 Cor 15:52: "The dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed" — at "the last trump." 1 Cor 15:53-54: "This mortal must put on immortality" — in the same resurrection context. Luke 20:36: The resurrected "can die no more" (ouketi — "no longer") — implying they previously could die. The timing is explicitly placed at the resurrection. No interpretation is needed.

    6. "'God only hath immortality' means only self-existent immortality; humans may still possess a derived form." - Inference (I-C: Compatible External). The text does not say "self-existent immortality" — it says "immortality." The derived/underived distinction is not present in the passage. It must be imported from an external theological framework. It is I-C rather than I-D because it qualifies the text rather than directly overriding it — but it still adds a concept the text does not contain.

    7. "The wages of sin is death; the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (Rom 6:23) - Explicit (E). Paul directly states this in the text.

    What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said — Answers¶

    What CAN be said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies):

    1. God alone (monos) possesses immortality (athanasia) (1 Tim 6:16).
    2. God is incorruptible (aphthartos); man is corruptible (phthartos) — explicitly contrasted in the same verse (Rom 1:23).
    3. The human body/flesh is mortal (thnetos) (Rom 6:12; 8:11; 2 Cor 4:11; 5:4).
    4. Mortals must "put on" immortality (athanasia) at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54).
    5. Immortality (aphtharsia) must be sought (Rom 2:7) and was brought to light through the gospel (2 Tim 1:10).
    6. Eternal life is a gift (Rom 6:23), a promise (Tit 1:2; 1 John 2:25), conditional on relationship with Christ (1 John 5:11-12; John 3:16, 36).
    7. Man does not inherently possess immortality (N — from 1 Tim 6:16 + Rom 2:7 + 1 Cor 15:53).
    8. The alternatives to eternal life are perishing (John 3:16), death (Rom 6:23), corruption (Gal 6:8), destruction (2 Thess 1:9), and absence of life (John 3:36; 6:53; 1 John 5:12). (Note: this is an I-A inference — it systematizes what individual texts say into a composite claim. Each individual text is explicit, but the comprehensive statement requires combining them.)

    What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied — requires adding something):

    1. It cannot be said that Scripture teaches humans have an "immortal soul." The phrase does not appear in the Bible. No NT passage applies athanasia, aphtharsia, or aphthartos to the human soul as an inherent, present possession. The claim requires overriding Ezekiel 18:4, Matthew 10:28, and 1 Timothy 6:16.
    2. It cannot be said that 1 Timothy 6:16 means only "self-existent" immortality. The text does not qualify monos. The derived/underived distinction must be imported from outside.
    3. It cannot be said that "mortal" applies only to the body while the soul is immortal. Paul never names an immortal counterpart to the mortal body. In 1 Cor 15:53, "this mortal" is used absolutely.
    4. It cannot be said that eternal life is an inherent human possession. Every passage presents it as a gift, promise, hope, or something to seek — never as something humans possess by nature.
    5. It cannot be said that Luke 20:38 describes the patriarchs as presently conscious. Jesus' argument is for the resurrection (v.37), not for a conscious intermediate state.
    6. It cannot be said that any NT passage applies immortality vocabulary to the human soul as an inherent, present possession. All 18 NT occurrences of athanasia, aphtharsia, and aphthartos describe either God's nature, the future resurrection state, spiritual/heavenly qualities, or something to seek. Zero describe the human soul as presently immortal.
