    Study 20: Series Review — Answer Key¶

    Comprehensive Review of All 19 Studies

    Section A: Foundational Anthropology (Studies 1-4)¶

    1. According to Genesis 2:7, what is the formula for man's creation? Did man receive a soul or become one?

    Dust + breath of life (neshamah) = living soul (nephesh chayyah). Man became a living soul — the Hebrew verb hayah means "became." The soul is what man is, not something placed inside him.

    2. What Hebrew word is translated "soul" and also "creature"? Is it applied only to humans, or to animals as well?

    Nephesh (H5315). It appears approximately 780 times with over 40 translations including soul, life, creature, person, body, and dead body. Animals are called nephesh chayyah in Genesis 1:20-21,24 — the same phrase used for man in Genesis 2:7.

    3. Who alone possesses immortality according to 1 Timothy 6:16? When do believers receive it (1 Cor 15:53-54)?

    God alone (monos) has immortality (athanasia). Believers receive it at the resurrection: "This mortal must put on immortality." It is not an inherent human possession — it must be sought (Rom 2:7) and was brought to light through the gospel (2 Tim 1:10).

    4. What do the dead know, according to Ecclesiastes 9:5? What happens to thoughts at death (Ps 146:4)?

    "The dead know not any thing" (Ecc 9:5). "In that very day his thoughts perish" (Ps 146:4). Multiple authors across both testaments describe the dead as unconscious, inactive, and without knowledge.

    5. What word do at least seven biblical authors use to describe death?

    Sleep. Used by Moses (Deut 31:16), Job (Job 14:12), Daniel (Dan 12:2), Jesus (John 11:11-14), Luke (Acts 7:60), Paul (1 Cor 15:51; 1 Thess 4:14), and others.

    Section B: Key Words (Studies 5-8)¶

    6. Name the four words translated "hell" in English Bibles. Which one is the general abode of all the dead?

    Sheol (Hebrew, 67 OT occurrences), Hades (Greek, 11 NT occurrences), Gehenna (Greek, 12 NT occurrences), Tartaroo (Greek, 1 NT occurrence). Sheol/Hades is the general abode of all the dead — characterized by silence, unconsciousness, and darkness. No word inherently means "eternal torment."

    7. What is the primary vocabulary the Bible uses for the fate of the wicked — destruction words or torment words?

    Destruction words. Hebrew: abad (perish/destroy) and shamad (destroy/annihilate). Greek: apollymi (destroy/perish) and olethros (destruction). These appear hundreds of times. No lexicon defines any of them as "torment." The torment word family (basanizo/basanismos) is separate and is never applied to the final fate of generic human wicked in didactic genre.

    8. Does the Hebrew word olam ("forever") always mean endless duration? Give two examples where it clearly ended.

    No. Jonah was in the fish "forever" (olam) — three days (Jonah 2:6). A slave serves "forever" (olam) — until death (Exodus 21:6). The Aaronic priesthood was "forever" — ended. Two olam promises were explicitly revoked by God (1 Sam 2:30; 13:13).

    9. Does the Greek word aionios inherently mean "eternal"? What separate Greek word exists for "everduring"?

    No. Aionios (G166) functions like its Hebrew source olam — its duration is determined by the nature of the subject it modifies. It is used for past time in Romans 16:25, 2 Timothy 1:9, and Titus 1:2. The separate word aidios (G126), meaning "everduring," appears only twice in the NT and was available if the authors wanted to unambiguously express endless duration.

    Section C: Difficult Passages (Studies 9-13)¶

    10. Is the Rich Man and Lazarus a parable or literal history?

    A parable. It is situated within a series of parables (Luke 15-16), uses a formulaic introduction ("a certain rich man"), contains imagery found nowhere else in Scripture (Abraham's bosom, conversation across a gulf), and its stated teaching point is about Moses and the prophets (v.29-31), not afterlife geography. It fails the genre gate (parabolic, not didactic) and subject gate (allegorical characters).

    11. Are the "souls under the altar" (Rev 6:9-11) conscious dead people?

    No — this is an apocalyptic vision where all surrounding elements are symbolic (seals, horses, stars falling). The imagery echoes Abel's blood "crying from the ground" (Gen 4:10) — blood does not literally speak; it is a figure for injustice demanding justice. The souls are located "under the altar" — corresponding to OT sacrificial blood poured at the altar base (Lev 4:7). The same group "lived" at the first resurrection (Rev 20:4-5), indicating they were not alive before.

    12. What OT passage is the source of the "smoke ascending forever" imagery?

    Isaiah 34:9-10 describes Edom: "The smoke thereof shall go up for ever; from generation to generation it shall lie waste." Edom is not still burning — it has been desolate for millennia. The formula describes the permanence of the destruction, not ongoing combustion. Jude 7 and 2 Peter 2:6 confirm Sodom was reduced to ashes, not perpetually burning.

    13. In Revelation 20:10, who are the three subjects? Are any generic human beings?

    The devil (a non-human spirit being), the beast (a symbolic entity from apocalyptic vision), and the false prophet (a symbolic entity). None is a generic human being. When humans enter the lake of fire (Rev 20:14-15; 21:8), the text uses "second death" — death vocabulary, not torment vocabulary.

    14. What does Revelation 20:14 say the lake of fire IS?

    "This is the second death." The text provides its own interpretation using a predicate nominative construction. The lake of fire = the second death. When humans are described as entering it, the term is "second death," not "eternal torment." Death and hades themselves are cast into the lake — death itself is destroyed (cf. 1 Cor 15:26: "The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death").

    Section D: Evaluation (Studies 14-19)¶

    15. How many of the eight major judgment passages use basanizo (torment) for human wicked?

    Zero. None of the eight passages (Rev 20:11-15, Matt 25:31-46, Dan 12:2, Isa 66:22-24, John 5:28-29, 2 Thess 1:7-10, Heb 10:26-31, Rom 2:5-11) uses basanizo for the fate of the human wicked. Four use destruction/death vocabulary.

    16. How many of the eight strongest ECT arguments produced an E-item?

    Zero. All eight arguments, when evaluated at the E/N/I tier, produced only inferences: 4 I-B (contested readings, all resolved Strong toward the conditionalist reading), 7 I-C (concepts the text does not contain), and 1 I-D (overriding lexical definitions).

    17. What is the historical origin of the "immortal soul" concept?

    Greek philosophy — specifically Plato's Phaedo (c. 360 BC). The concept entered Judaism through Hellenistic influence, then Christianity through Alexandrian writers (Clement, Origen). Augustine, a former Neoplatonist, synthesized the ECT doctrine (c. 430 AD). The soul's immortality was declared dogma only in 1513 (Fifth Lateran Council). Conditional immortality was the earliest Christian position, held by apostolic fathers like Clement, Ignatius, and Irenaeus.

    18. Is the argument "sin against an infinite God requires infinite punishment" found in any Bible verse?

    No. This argument originates with Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1098 AD) in Cur Deus Homo. No biblical text contains this reasoning. Explicit biblical statements describe punishment as proportional: "according to deeds" (Rom 2:6; Job 34:11; Ps 62:12), "many stripes" vs. "few stripes" (Luke 12:47-48), "more tolerable" for some cities than others (Matt 10:15).

    19. In Matthew 10:28, what does Jesus say God is able to do?

    "Destroy (apollymi) both soul and body in gehenna." When Matthew uses apollymi elsewhere for divine judgment (Matt 21:41; 22:7), it consistently means actual destruction. The same word pair (psyche + apollymi) appears in Matt 10:39 meaning "lose one's life." All seven Matthean gehenna passages pair gehenna with destruction/judgment vocabulary — never with basanizo.

    20. In Jesus' judgment parables, what word does Jesus use for what happens to the wicked?

    Katakaio (G2618) — "burn up completely." In Jesus' own interpretation of the wheat and tares parable (Matt 13:40-42), the tares are gathered and burned. The kata- prefix intensifies: complete consumption. Basanizo (torment) does not appear in any of Jesus' judgment parables. "Gnashing of teeth" (brygmos) expresses rage/hostility in the OT and Acts 7:54, not physical pain.

    Section E: The Big Picture¶

    21. What is the difference between an explicit statement and an inference?

    An explicit statement is something the text directly says — you can point to a verse and quote it. An inference is a conclusion someone draws from the text, but no verse actually says it. Something must be added: a concept, a framework, a choice between readings, or a philosophical argument. The hierarchy is: Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference. An inference cannot override what the text explicitly says.

    22. Three things the Bible explicitly says about the fate of the wicked:

    1. "The wages of sin is death" (Rom 6:23)
    2. "The soul that sinneth, it shall die" (Ezek 18:4)
    3. God "is able to destroy both soul and body in gehenna" (Matt 10:28)

    (Also: "shall not perish but have everlasting life" — John 3:16, where the alternative to life is perishing. "The wicked shall perish... they shall consume; into smoke shall they consume away" — Ps 37:20.)

    23. Three things that are inferred (not explicitly stated):

    1. "The soul is immortal and cannot die" — no verse says this; it requires overriding Ezek 18:4, Matt 10:28, and 1 Tim 6:16
    2. "The wicked will be tormented consciously forever" — no didactic verse says this; it requires reading apocalyptic imagery as doctrine, extending non-human subjects to humans, assuming "forever" means endless duration despite dozens of biblical examples where olam/aionios describe things that clearly ended, and importing the concept of an immortal soul that the text never teaches
    3. "Sin against an infinite God requires infinite punishment" — no verse contains this reasoning; it is Anselmian philosophy

    24. Why does the distinction matter?

    Because many widely held beliefs about the afterlife are inferences, not explicit statements. When an inference contradicts what the text explicitly says — for example, claiming the soul is immortal when the text says "the soul that sinneth, it shall die" — the inference must yield to the explicit statement. Confusing inference with explicit teaching leads to building doctrines on what interpreters add to the text rather than on what the text says.

    25. What does the Bible, at the level of explicit statements, teach about the final fate of the wicked?

    The wicked perish, are destroyed, die, are consumed, become ashes, experience the second death, and cease to exist. This is stated directly, repeatedly, across both testaments, by multiple authors (Moses, David, Solomon, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Obadiah, Malachi, Jesus, Paul, Peter, John, James, Jude), in didactic genre, using the Bible's own destruction vocabulary. The alternative position (eternal conscious torment) produces zero explicit statements and zero necessary implications across 632 classified evidence items — its entire case rests on inferences that require adding concepts the text does not contain.

    Evidence Summary Exercise — Answers¶

    Question What the Bible explicitly says What is inferred (not stated)
    Is the soul immortal? The soul can die (Ezek 18:4) and be destroyed (Matt 10:28). Nephesh meth = "dead soul" (Lev 21:11). "The soul is inherently immortal" — no verse says this.
    What do the dead know? "The dead know not any thing" (Ecc 9:5). Thoughts perish at death (Ps 146:4). "The dead are conscious in another realm" — inferred, not stated.
    Who has immortality? God only (1 Tim 6:16). Humans seek it (Rom 2:7), put it on at resurrection (1 Cor 15:53). "All humans possess immortality" — contradicts explicit statements.
    What are the wages of sin? Death (Rom 6:23). "The wages of sin is eternal conscious torment" — the text says death, not torment.
    What can God do to the soul? Destroy both soul and body in gehenna (Matt 10:28). "Destroy means preserve in torment" — redefines the word.
    What does "forever" always mean? Duration depends on the subject. Jonah's olam = 3 days. Slave's olam = until death. "Forever always means endless" — dozens of counter-examples.
    Who is "tormented forever" in Rev 20:10? The devil, beast, and false prophet — non-human/symbolic entities. "This applies to all wicked humans" — the text names three subjects, none human.
    What is the lake of fire? "This is the second death" (Rev 20:14). "The lake of fire is a place of eternal torment" — the text says it IS the second death.
    What vocabulary does the Bible use? Destruction: abad, apollymi, shamad, kalah, olethros, apoleia. "Destruction means ongoing conscious ruin" — no lexicon supports this.
    Is punishment proportional? "According to deeds" (Rom 2:6; 11+ passages). "Many stripes" vs. "few" (Luke 12:47-48). "Infinite punishment for finite sin" — Anselm (1098 AD), not Scripture.
