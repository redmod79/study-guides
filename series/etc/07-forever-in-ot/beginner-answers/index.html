    Study 7: Does "Forever" Mean Eternal? — Answer Key¶

    The Hebrew Word Olam in the Old Testament

    A Note on How to Study

    Throughout this series, we distinguish between what the Bible explicitly says and what people infer from the Bible. These are not the same thing.

    • Explicit: You can point to a verse and quote it. The text directly says this.
    • Inference: Someone claims the Bible teaches something, but no single verse says it. They have drawn a conclusion — possibly a good one, possibly not — but the conclusion itself is not in the text.

    This distinction is especially important in this study. The English word "forever" carries a strong sense of endlessness. But does the Hebrew word behind it always mean the same thing?

    Section A: What Does "Forever" Actually Mean in Hebrew?¶

    1. The Hebrew word olam (H5769) is translated "forever," "everlasting," "perpetual," "of old," "ancient," and over 40 other ways in the KJV. What does this wide range of translations suggest about whether olam simply means "eternal"?

    A word with over 40 different English translations — including "for ever" (198 times), "everlasting" (56 times), "perpetual" (22 times), "of old" (13 times), and "ancient" (4 times) — clearly does not carry a single, fixed meaning of "eternal." The range of translations reflects a range of meanings determined by context.

    2. Strong's Concordance defines olam as "properly, concealed, i.e. the vanishing point; generally, time out of mind." The root word alam means "to conceal" or "to hide." Based on this, does olam describe a specific length of time, or a time whose endpoint is hidden from view?

    Olam describes time whose endpoint is not visible — hidden, concealed. It does not specify a particular length. It could mean three days, a lifetime, an age, or eternity, depending on the subject. The core meaning is "as far as the eye can see" in terms of time — the horizon, not infinity.

    3. Read Genesis 6:4. The "mighty men" are described as being "of old" — the Hebrew word is olam. Does olam here mean "eternal," or does it mean "ancient" / "long ago"?

    It means "ancient" or "long ago." These were men from the distant past, not men who existed eternally.

    "There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old [olam], men of renown." — Genesis 6:4

    4. Read Deuteronomy 32:7 and Jeremiah 6:16. Both use olam for "old" or "ancient." If olam meant "infinite/endless," could it be used to describe the past?

    No. A word meaning "infinite" could not be applied to a finite past period. The fact that olam means "of old" and "ancient" in approximately 30 occurrences demonstrates that its core meaning is "indefinite duration" — not "infinite duration." It can point forward (future) or backward (past), and context determines the actual scope.

    "Remember the days of old [olam], consider the years of many generations." — Deuteronomy 32:7

    "Thus saith the LORD, Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old [olam] paths." — Jeremiah 6:16

    Section B: Where "Forever" Clearly Ended¶

    5. Read Jonah 2:6. Jonah says "the earth with her bars was about me for ever" (olam). Now read Jonah 1:17. How long was Jonah actually in the fish?

    Three days and three nights. Jonah uses olam — "forever" — to describe a period of three days. This is the shortest documented olam in Scripture.

    "The earth with her bars was about me for ever [olam]." — Jonah 2:6

    "Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights." — Jonah 1:17

    6. Read Exodus 21:6. A slave who chooses to stay with his master "shall serve him for ever" (olam). Does this mean the slave will serve for eternity, or for his remaining lifetime?

    For his remaining lifetime. No one reads this as the slave serving for eternity. The olam of service ends at the slave's death — universally acknowledged.

    "Then his master shall bring him unto the judges; he shall also bring him to the door, or unto the door post; and his master shall bore his ear through with an aul; and he shall serve him for ever [olam]." — Exodus 21:6

    7. Read 1 Samuel 1:22. Hannah says Samuel will "abide for ever" (olam) at the tabernacle. Now read 1 Samuel 25:1. What happened to Samuel?

    Samuel died. His olam at the tabernacle lasted his lifetime — not eternity.

    "I will not go up until the child be weaned, and then I will bring him, that he may appear before the LORD, and there abide for ever [olam]." — 1 Samuel 1:22

    "And Samuel died." — 1 Samuel 25:1

    8. Read Exodus 40:15. The Aaronic priesthood is called "an everlasting priesthood" (olam). Now read Hebrews 7:12. What happened to this priesthood?

    It was changed. The "everlasting" Aaronic priesthood gave way to the priesthood of Melchizedek through Christ. An olam institution was superseded.

    "And thou shalt anoint them, as thou didst anoint their father, that they may minister unto me in the priest's office: for their anointing shall surely be an everlasting [olam] priesthood throughout their generations." — Exodus 40:15

    "For the priesthood being changed, there is made of necessity a change also of the law." — Hebrews 7:12

    9. Read 1 Kings 8:13 and 1 Kings 9:3. Solomon says the temple is God's dwelling "for ever" (olam). What happened to Solomon's temple in 586 BC? And again in AD 70?

    It was destroyed — twice. Nebuchadnezzar destroyed it in 586 BC. The rebuilt temple was destroyed by the Romans in AD 70. An olam dwelling no longer exists.

    "I have surely built thee an house to dwell in, a settled place for thee to abide in for ever [olam]." — 1 Kings 8:13

    10. Read Genesis 17:13. Circumcision is called "an everlasting covenant" (olam). Now read Galatians 5:2-6. Is circumcision still required?

    No. Paul explicitly states circumcision is no longer required in the new covenant. An olam covenant was superseded.

    "My covenant shall be in your flesh for an everlasting [olam] covenant." — Genesis 17:13

    "If ye be circumcised, Christ shall profit you nothing." — Galatians 5:2

    Section C: "Forever" Fire and Judgment¶

    11. Read Isaiah 34:10. The smoke of Edom's judgment goes up "for ever" (olam). Now read Isaiah 34:11-17. What creatures inhabit the same territory afterward? Can animals live in a place where fire is still burning?

    Pelicans, hedgehogs, owls, ravens, wild goats, and other creatures inhabit the territory (Isa 34:11-17). Animals cannot dwell in a place where fire is actively burning. The "forever" fire completed its work of destruction, then went out. The result — desolation — is permanent. The process — burning — was not endless.

    "It shall not be quenched night nor day; the smoke thereof shall go up for ever [olam]." — Isaiah 34:10

    "But the cormorant and the bittern shall possess it; the owl also and the raven shall dwell in it." — Isaiah 34:11

    12. Read Jeremiah 17:4, 27. Fire will burn in Jerusalem's gates and "shall not be quenched" — it will burn olam. Nebuchadnezzar burned Jerusalem in 586 BC. Is that fire still burning today?

    No. The fire burned Jerusalem, accomplished its destruction, and went out. It is not still burning. The olam fire describes a completed, irreversible judgment — not a literally endless flame.

    "Ye have kindled a fire in mine anger, which shall burn for ever [olam]." — Jeremiah 17:4

    13. Read Jude 1:7. Sodom and Gomorrah suffered "the vengeance of eternal fire." Are those cities still burning?

    No. Sodom and Gomorrah are not burning today. They serve as an example of completed destruction. The fire was "eternal" in its effect (the cities were permanently destroyed), not in its duration (the fire went out long ago).

    "Even as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities about them in like manner, giving themselves over to fornication, and going after strange flesh, are set forth for an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire." — Jude 1:7

    14. Based on these three examples (Edom, Jerusalem, Sodom), what does "eternal fire" or "forever fire" describe — a fire that never stops burning, or a fire whose destructive results are permanent?

    In every verifiable OT case, "eternal fire" or "forever fire" describes a fire whose destructive results are permanent — not a fire that never stops burning. Edom's fire went out (animals inhabit it). Jerusalem's fire went out (it burned in 586 BC). Sodom's fire went out (the cities are ruins, not active furnaces). The pattern is consistent: olam fire means irreversible destruction, not endless burning.

    Section D: Revoked "Forever" Promises¶

    15. Read 1 Samuel 2:30. God says: "I said indeed that thy house, and the house of thy father, should walk before me for ever: but now the LORD saith, Be it far from me." What happened to this olam promise?

    God explicitly revoked it. He had said "for ever" — and then cancelled it because of Eli's sons' wickedness. The phrase "but now the LORD saith, Be it far from me" is a direct divine reversal of an olam promise.

    "Wherefore the LORD God of Israel saith, I said indeed that thy house, and the house of thy father, should walk before me for ever: but now the LORD saith, Be it far from me." — 1 Samuel 2:30

    16. Read 1 Samuel 13:13-14. Samuel tells Saul: "The LORD would have established thy kingdom upon Israel for ever. But now thy kingdom shall not continue." Why was Saul's olam kingdom taken away?

    Because of Saul's disobedience. God would have established his kingdom olam — but Saul's failure meant the promise was immediately revoked. An olam kingdom lasted zero generations beyond Saul himself.

    "Thou hast done foolishly: thou hast not kept the commandment of the LORD thy God, which he commanded thee: for now would the LORD have established thy kingdom upon Israel for ever. But now thy kingdom shall not continue." — 1 Samuel 13:13-14

    17. If olam meant "unconditional and irrevocable," could God cancel an olam promise? What do these two examples tell you about whether olam guarantees permanence?

    If olam meant "unconditional and irrevocable," God could not cancel it — but He did, twice. These examples demonstrate that olam does not inherently mean "unconditional" or "irrevocable." It describes the intended duration under certain conditions, not an absolute guarantee of endlessness.

    Section E: What Determines the Duration?¶

    18. Read Psalm 90:2. "From everlasting to everlasting, thou art God." Here olam is applied to God. Is God genuinely eternal? Does olam carry its full weight here?

    Yes. God is genuinely eternal, and olam carries its maximal force when applied to Him. But this is because God is eternal by nature — not because olam inherently means "eternal." The word takes its force from its subject.

    "Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting [olam] to everlasting [olam], thou art God." — Psalm 90:2

    19. Now compare Psalm 90:2 (olam for God = genuinely eternal) with Exodus 21:6 (olam for slave service = one lifetime) and Jonah 2:6 (olam for Jonah's confinement = three days). The same word is used in all three cases. What determines the actual duration — the word olam itself, or the nature of the subject it describes?

    The nature of the subject determines the duration. When olam modifies God, it means genuinely eternal — because God is genuinely eternal. When it modifies slave service, it means a lifetime — because the slave is mortal. When it modifies Jonah's confinement, it means three days — because that was the actual duration. The word olam is elastic; it stretches to fit the subject it describes. It does not impose a fixed duration on its subject.

    Subject Olam Duration Reference
    God Genuinely eternal Psalm 90:2
    Aaronic priesthood Ended (changed) Exodus 40:15; Hebrews 7:12
    Solomon's temple Ended (destroyed) 1 Kings 8:13
    Slave service One lifetime Exodus 21:6
    Samuel's service One lifetime 1 Samuel 1:22
    Jonah's confinement Three days Jonah 2:6
    Edom's fire Completed judgment Isaiah 34:10-17
    "Of old" / "ancient" Past time Genesis 6:4; Jeremiah 6:16

    Explicit or Inference? — Answers¶

    A. "Olam is applied to subjects that have demonstrably ended — the Aaronic priesthood, Solomon's temple, slave service, and Jonah's three-day confinement." - Explicit. Each example can be verified directly from Scripture. The priesthood was changed (Heb 7:12). The temple was destroyed (historical fact confirmed by Scripture). The slave serves for life (Exo 21:6). Jonah was confined three days (Jon 1:17). All are called olam.

    B. "When the Bible says 'forever,' it always means eternally without end." - Inference. No verse states this rule. The examples in this study demonstrate that olam frequently describes durations that have ended. The claim that "forever always means eternally" requires ignoring dozens of counterexamples in the text itself.

    C. "God explicitly revoked two olam promises — to Eli's house and to Saul's kingdom." - Explicit. 1 Samuel 2:30 quotes God saying "I said for ever: but now, Be it far from me." 1 Samuel 13:13-14 states "would have established thy kingdom for ever. But now thy kingdom shall not continue." Both are direct quotations.

    D. "The 'eternal fire' that destroyed Sodom and Edom is still burning today." - Inference. No one claims Sodom or Edom is still burning. Isaiah 34:11-17 explicitly describes animals living in the territory after the "forever" fire. The fire completed its work and went out. The claim that "eternal fire" means literally endless burning contradicts observable reality in every verifiable OT instance.

    E. "The duration of olam depends on the nature of the subject it describes, not on the word itself." - Necessary Implication. No single verse states this rule explicitly. However, it unavoidably follows from the evidence: olam = three days (Jonah), one lifetime (slave), ended institutions (priesthood, temple), and genuinely eternal (God). The pattern is observable across 400+ uses and over 50 olam-described institutions have demonstrably ended. The only conclusion that accounts for all the data is that the subject — not the word — determines the duration.

    What the Bible Says vs. What People Say the Bible Teaches¶

    The Bible explicitly says... People often claim the Bible teaches...
    Olam is applied to things that have ended (priesthood, temple, ceremonies) "Forever" always means eternally without end
    Jonah's olam was three days (Jon 2:6; 1:17) The word "forever" has a fixed, infinite meaning
    God revoked two olam promises (1 Sam 2:30; 13:13) Olam promises are unconditional and irrevocable
    Olam fire in Edom went out — animals inhabit the land (Isa 34:10-17) "Eternal fire" means fire that never stops burning
    Olam means "of old" / "ancient" in ~30 occurrences (Gen 6:4) Olam only refers to the future and always means endless
    The same word describes three days and God's eternity "Forever" carries the same meaning in every passage
