    Study 19: Jesus' Judgment Parables -- Answer Key¶

    Wheat and Tares, Dragnet, Outer Darkness, and the Furnace of Fire

    A Note on How to Study

    Throughout this series, we distinguish between what the Bible explicitly says and what people infer from the Bible. These are not the same thing.

    • Explicit: You can point to a verse and quote it. The text directly says this.
    • Inference: Someone claims the Bible teaches something, but no single verse says it. They have drawn a conclusion -- possibly a good one, possibly not -- but the conclusion itself is not in the text.

    In this study, we examine the vocabulary Jesus actually used in his judgment parables. We will ask: do these parables use the language of destruction, or the language of ongoing torment?

    Section A: The Wheat and Tares (Matt 13:24-30, 36-43)¶

    1. Read Matthew 13:24-30. In the parable, what happens to the tares (weeds) at harvest time? Are they preserved or consumed?

    The tares are gathered into bundles and burned. They are consumed by fire -- not preserved in it. No farmer bundles weeds to keep them burning indefinitely; the purpose is disposal through destruction.

    "Gather ye together first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn them: but gather the wheat into my barn." -- Matthew 13:30

    2. Read Matthew 13:36-43. Jesus interprets his own parable. Who do the tares represent? Who are the reapers? When does the harvest take place?

    Jesus provides his own interpretation: the tares are "the children of the wicked one" (v.38), the reapers are "the angels" (v.39), and the harvest is "the end of this world" (v.39). This is significant because Jesus moves the parable from imagery to didactic explanation -- he tells us what each element means.

    3. In Matthew 13:40, Jesus says: "As therefore the tares are gathered and burned in the fire; so shall it be in the end of this world." What happens to tares when they are burned? Does anything remain?

    Tares are consumed by fire -- nothing remains. Jesus says the fate of the wicked at the end of the world corresponds to what happens to tares in fire: "so shall it be." If the wicked were preserved in fire rather than consumed, the comparison would break down. Tares do not burn forever; they are consumed.

    "As therefore the tares are gathered and burned in the fire; so shall it be in the end of this world." -- Matthew 13:40

    4. Read Matthew 13:42-43. What happens to the wicked (v.42)? What happens to the righteous (v.43)? Are both groups described as continuing to exist, or is one group destroyed while the other shines?

    The wicked are cast into the furnace of fire (v.42). The righteous "shine forth as the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (v.43). The contrast is existence-in-glory vs. consumption-by-fire -- not two forms of ongoing existence. The echo of Daniel 12:3 ("they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament") places this in the resurrection context.

    "And shall cast them into a furnace of fire: there shall be wailing and gnashing of teeth. Then shall the righteous shine forth as the sun in the kingdom of their Father." -- Matthew 13:42-43

    Section B: The Dragnet (Matt 13:47-50)¶

    5. Read Matthew 13:47-50. When fishermen sort their catch, what do they do with the bad fish? Are the bad fish kept for some purpose, or thrown away as worthless?

    The bad fish are "cast away" -- thrown out as worthless refuse. No fisherman discards bad fish in order to preserve them in suffering. They are disposed of because they have no value. This disposal image reinforces the destruction reading.

    "...gathered the good into vessels, but cast the bad away." -- Matthew 13:48

    6. Compare Matthew 13:50 with Matthew 13:42. These two verses are nearly identical in the Greek. What phrase appears in both? What does this suggest about whether the formula is a set judgment expression?

    Both verses contain the identical formula: "And shall cast them into the furnace of fire: there shall be wailing and gnashing of teeth." The verbatim repetition confirms this is a set judgment expression -- a formulaic way of describing the fate of the wicked at the end of the age. The disposal imagery of the dragnet (throwing away worthless fish) reinforces the destruction meaning of the furnace formula.

    Section C: Outer Darkness (Matt 8:12; 22:13; 25:30)¶

    7. Read Matthew 8:11-12. Who sits at the feast with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob? Who is "cast out into outer darkness"? What is the image -- being sent to a place of torment, or being excluded from a celebration?

    Many from east and west (Gentiles) sit at the feast with the patriarchs, while the "children of the kingdom" (those who expected to be included) are cast into outer darkness. The image is exclusion from a lit celebration -- banishment from the kingdom feast into the darkness outside. It is not a description of hell but of being shut out from the party.

    "And I say unto you, That many shall come from the east and west, and shall sit down with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, in the kingdom of heaven. But the children of the kingdom shall be cast out into outer darkness." -- Matthew 8:11-12

    8. Read Matthew 22:11-13. What happens to the guest without a wedding garment? Where is he sent? Is the image one of punishment inside, or banishment outside?

    The guest without a wedding garment is bound hand and foot and cast into outer darkness. The setting is a wedding feast -- being cast out means banishment from the celebration. The image is expulsion from the lit banquet hall into the darkness outside, not confinement in a place of torment.

    9. Read Matthew 25:30. The unprofitable servant is cast into outer darkness. What do the faithful servants receive instead (v.21, 23)? What is the contrast between the two outcomes?

    The faithful servants enter "the joy of thy lord" (vv.21, 23). The unfaithful servant is cast into outer darkness. The contrast is entrance into joy vs. exclusion from it. One group joins the celebration; the other is shut out.

    10. "Outer darkness" appears exactly 3 times in the Bible, all in Matthew. All three are in the context of a feast or celebration. Does any of these passages mention fire, gehenna, or the lake of fire alongside "outer darkness"?

    No. None of the three "outer darkness" passages mentions fire, gehenna, or the lake of fire. The image is darkness -- the absence of the light of the kingdom feast. This is notable: "outer darkness" (no light) and "furnace of fire" (bright flames) are incompatible as literal descriptions of the same place. Both are figurative, highlighting different aspects of judgment -- the furnace emphasizes destruction, the darkness emphasizes exclusion.

    Section D: Weeping and Gnashing of Teeth¶

    11. Read Luke 13:28. This is the only occurrence where the trigger for "weeping and gnashing of teeth" is explicitly stated. What causes the weeping and gnashing? Is it physical pain, or the realization of being excluded from the kingdom?

    The trigger is seeing "Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and all the prophets, in the kingdom of God, and you yourselves thrust out." The weeping and gnashing are caused by the realization of exclusion -- seeing the kingdom and knowing you have been shut out. The text does not describe ongoing physical pain. It describes the emotional response at the moment of judgment.

    "There shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth, when ye shall see Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and all the prophets, in the kingdom of God, and you yourselves thrust out." -- Luke 13:28

    12. Read Acts 7:54. The Sanhedrin "gnashed on [Stephen] with their teeth." Were they in physical pain? What emotion does their gnashing express?

    The Sanhedrin were not in physical pain. They were furious -- "cut to the heart" with rage. They gnashed their teeth at Stephen in murderous anger. This is the only NT use of teeth-gnashing outside the judgment formula, and it unambiguously means fury, not physical torment.

    "When they heard these things, they were cut to the heart, and they gnashed on him with their teeth." -- Acts 7:54

    13. Read Psalm 112:10. What does the wicked person do when he sees the righteous prosper? What happens to the wicked afterward -- does the verse say he endures forever, or that he "melts away" and his desire "perishes"?

    The wicked person gnashes his teeth in frustrated rage at seeing the righteous prosper. But the verse does not end with ongoing gnashing -- it says he "shall melt away" and "the desire of the wicked shall perish." Rage is followed by dissolution and perishing. The gnashing is the reaction; the perishing is the outcome.

    "The wicked shall see it, and be grieved; he shall gnash with his teeth, and melt away: the desire of the wicked shall perish." -- Psalm 112:10

    14. The phrase "weeping and gnashing of teeth" appears 7 times in the NT. Based on the Luke 13:28, Acts 7:54, and Psalm 112:10 evidence, does "gnashing of teeth" describe physical pain in a place of torment, or fury and grief at the moment of judgment?

    All three lines of evidence point the same direction. Luke 13:28 identifies the trigger as exclusion from the kingdom. Acts 7:54 shows teeth-gnashing as an expression of murderous rage, not physical pain. Psalm 112:10 describes gnashing as the reaction to seeing the righteous prosper, followed by melting away and perishing. The gnashing is fury and grief at the moment of judgment -- not the ongoing experience of torment.

    Explicit or Inference? -- Answers¶

    A. "The tares are gathered and burned in the fire; so shall it be in the end of this world." - Explicit. Matthew 13:40 directly states this. Jesus is interpreting his own parable.

    B. "The furnace of fire in Jesus' parables describes a place of eternal conscious torment." - Inference. No verse in these parables says "eternal conscious torment." The furnace formula appears in a parabolic context, the OT furnace traditions describe instruments that destroy their contents, and the verb Jesus chose (katakaio) means "burn up completely." The claim of eternal conscious torment requires adding a concept the text does not state.

    C. "There shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth, when ye shall see Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and all the prophets, in the kingdom of God, and you yourselves thrust out." - Explicit. Luke 13:28 directly states this.

    D. "Outer darkness is a description of hell where the wicked suffer forever." - Inference. No "outer darkness" passage mentions hell, gehenna, fire, or the lake of fire. No "outer darkness" passage mentions duration or eternal suffering. All three passages are in feast/banquet contexts where the image is exclusion from the celebration. Equating "outer darkness" with "eternal hell" requires adding concepts the text does not contain.

    E. "The weeping and gnashing of teeth describes the ongoing experience of the wicked in an eternal hell." - Inference. The formula describes the reaction at the moment of judgment (Luke 13:28 -- "when ye shall see... and you yourselves thrust out"). It does not specify duration or say the weeping and gnashing continue forever. The OT and NT evidence identifies gnashing as rage, not physical pain. Reading this as a description of ongoing conditions in an eternal hell requires adding duration and changing the meaning of gnashing from rage to pain.

    What the Text Says vs. What People Say It Teaches¶

    The text explicitly says... People often claim it teaches...
    Tares are gathered and burned (Matt 13:40) The wicked are preserved in fire forever
    Bad fish are cast away as worthless (Matt 13:48) The wicked are kept for eternal torment
    The excluded are thrust out from the kingdom (Luke 13:28) The excluded are sent to a fiery hell
    Gnashing = rage at exclusion (Luke 13:28; Acts 7:54) Gnashing = physical agony in torment
    Outer darkness = exclusion from the feast (Matt 8:12; 22:13; 25:30) Outer darkness = a region of eternal hell
    No torment vocabulary (basanizo) in any of these passages These passages describe eternal conscious torment
