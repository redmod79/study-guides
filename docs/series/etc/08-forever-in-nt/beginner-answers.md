# Study 8: Does "Forever" Mean Eternal? (New Testament) — Answer Key

*The Greek Word Aionios and the Duration of Punishment*

---

> **A Note on How to Study**
>
> Throughout this series, we distinguish between what the Bible **explicitly says** and what people **infer** from the Bible. These are not the same thing.
>
> - **Explicit:** You can point to a verse and quote it. The text directly says this.
> - **Inference:** Someone claims the Bible teaches something, but no single verse says it. They have drawn a conclusion — possibly a good one, possibly not — but the conclusion itself is not in the text.
>
> In Study 7 we examined the Hebrew word *olam* and found that its duration is determined by the subject it describes, not by the word itself. This study asks the same question about the Greek New Testament equivalent: does *aionios* inherently mean "eternal/endless," or does it function the same way *olam* does?

---

## Section A: What Does *Aionios* Mean?

**1. The Greek word *aionios* (G166) is derived from *aion* (G165), a noun meaning "an age." Strong's defines *aionios* as "perpetual (also used of past time, or past and future as well)." Based on this definition, does *aionios* describe a fixed duration, or does it mean "pertaining to an age" with the actual duration determined by context?**

*Aionios* does not describe a fixed duration. Strong's own definition notes it is used for "past time" as well as future — a word meaning "strictly endless" could not be applied to past time. The word means "pertaining to an age," and the actual duration depends on the subject it modifies — exactly as Hebrew *olam* functions.

**2. Read Romans 16:25. Paul writes of the mystery "kept secret since the world began" — literally "in *aionios* times" (*chronois aioniois*). Here *aionios* refers to past time. If *aionios* meant "strictly endless future," could it describe the past?**

No. A word meaning "endless future" cannot refer to the past. The KJV translators recognized this by rendering it "since the world began." *Aionios* here means "in past ages" — a finite period that has already elapsed.

> "Now to him that is of power to stablish you according to my gospel, and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery, which was kept secret since the world began [*chronois aioniois*]." — Romans 16:25

**3. Read 2 Timothy 1:9. God's grace was given "before the world began" — literally "before *aionios* times" (*pro chronon aionion*). If *aionios* meant "eternal," what would "before eternity" mean? Does the phrase make sense?**

"Before eternity" is logically impossible — there is nothing "before" eternity. But "before the ages" makes perfect sense. The KJV translators rendered it "before the world began" precisely because they recognized that *aionios* here refers to past ages, not to infinity.

> "Who hath saved us, and called us with an holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began [*pro chronon aionion*]." — 2 Timothy 1:9

**4. Read Titus 1:2. In this single verse, Paul uses *aionios* for both "eternal life" (*zoe aionios*, looking forward) and "before the world began" (*pro chronon aionion*, looking backward). If the same word can point both forward and backward in the same sentence, what does this tell you about whether *aionios* means "endless future"?**

It demonstrates conclusively that *aionios* does not mean "endless future." In a single verse, the same author uses the same word for a future hope and for a past time period. This is the most concentrated evidence that *aionios* means "pertaining to the ages" rather than "infinite."

> "In hope of eternal life [*zoe aionios*], which God, that cannot lie, promised before the world began [*pro chronon aionion*]." — Titus 1:2

## Section B: Two Greek Words — *Aionios* vs. *Aidios*

**5. Greek has a separate word, *aidios* (G126), derived from *aei* (G104, "always"). Strong's defines *aidios* as "everduring (forward and backward, or forward only)." *Aidios* occurs only twice in the NT. If Greek already had a word that specifically meant "everduring," what does it suggest that the NT predominantly uses *aionios* (from *aion*, "age") instead?**

It suggests that the NT authors did not intend *aionios* to mean "everduring" — because they had *aidios* available for that purpose and chose not to use it in eschatological contexts. The existence of both words in the same language confirms they are not synonymous. If they meant the same thing, there would be no reason for two separate words derived from different roots.

**6. Read Romans 1:20. Paul describes God's "eternal power" using *aidios* — not *aionios*. Why might Paul choose the "everduring" word (*aidios*) for God's nature rather than the "age-pertaining" word (*aionios*)?**

Paul chose *aidios* because he was describing something genuinely everlasting — God's power and divine nature, which have no beginning and no end. For that concept, the "strictly everduring" word was the right choice. Paul knew the difference between the two words and selected accordingly.

> "For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal [*aidios*] power and Godhead." — Romans 1:20

**7. Read Jude 1:6-7. In verse 6, Jude uses *aidios* for the "everlasting chains" holding fallen angels. In the very next verse (v.7), he uses *aionios* for the "eternal fire" that destroyed Sodom. The same author, in adjacent verses, uses two different words. If *aidios* and *aionios* meant the same thing, why would Jude switch words?**

Jude switched because the words mean different things. The angelic chains are genuinely everduring — they hold the angels until judgment day, an ongoing restraint. Sodom's fire completed its destructive work and went out. The chains are an ongoing process (*aidios*). The fire produced a permanent result (*aionios*). The deliberate alternation by the same author in adjacent verses is strong evidence that these are distinct words with distinct meanings.

> "And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting [*aidios*] chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day. Even as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities about them in like manner, giving themselves over to fornication, and going after strange flesh, are set forth for an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal [*aionios*] fire." — Jude 1:6-7

**8. Sodom's "eternal fire" (Jude 1:7) destroyed the city. Is Sodom still burning today? What does this tell you about the kind of "eternal" that *aionios* describes here?**

Sodom is not still burning. The fire completed its work of destruction, and the result is permanent — but the fire itself went out long ago. *Aionios* fire here means fire whose destructive result is permanent, not fire that burns endlessly. This is the identical pattern documented for OT *olam* fire in Study 7 (Edom's fire, Jerusalem's fire).

## Section C: *Aionios* in Key Passages

**9. Read Matthew 25:46. "These shall go away into everlasting punishment (*kolasin aionion*): but the righteous into life eternal (*zoen aionion*)." The same adjective (*aionios*) modifies both "punishment" and "life." Some argue this means both must have identical duration. Now recall Titus 1:2, where the same word (*aionios*) points both forward and backward in the same verse. Does a single adjective necessarily carry the same duration for every noun it modifies?**

No. Titus 1:2 demonstrates that *aionios* can carry different temporal scopes depending on its subject — forward for "eternal life" and backward for "before aionios times" — in the same verse. The same principle was documented for *olam* in Study 7: God's reign is *olam* (genuinely eternal, Exo 15:18) while slave service is *olam* (one lifetime, Exo 21:6) in the same book. The adjective does not force identical duration; the subject determines the duration.

**10. Read 2 Thessalonians 1:9. The wicked "shall be punished with everlasting destruction (*olethros aionios*)." The word translated "destruction" (*olethros*, G3639) means "ruin" or "death." No Greek lexicon defines *olethros* as "torment" or "ongoing suffering." What does this verse describe — eternal torment, or eternal destruction?**

Eternal destruction. The vocabulary is destruction vocabulary (*olethros* — ruin, death), not torment vocabulary. *Aionios* modifies the destruction, describing it as permanent in result. The verse says the wicked are destroyed permanently, not tormented endlessly.

> "Who shall be punished with everlasting destruction [*olethros aionios*] from the presence of the Lord, and from the glory of his power." — 2 Thessalonians 1:9

**11. Read Hebrews 9:12. Christ obtained "eternal redemption" (*aionios lytrosis*) "once" (*hapax*). Does "eternal redemption" mean the act of redeeming goes on forever, or that the result of the redemption is permanent?**

The result is permanent. No one reads "eternal redemption" as meaning the act of redeeming takes forever. Christ redeemed us once, and the result stands permanently. *Aionios* describes the permanence of the result, not the duration of the process.

> "Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal [*aionios*] redemption for us." — Hebrews 9:12

**12. Now consider "eternal judgment" (Heb 6:2), "eternal salvation" (Heb 5:9), and "eternal inheritance" (Heb 9:15). In each case, the event is completed and the result is permanent. What does this pattern tell you about how *aionios* functions — does it describe an ongoing process, or a permanent result?**

A permanent result. In every Hebrews usage, the act is completed (judgment rendered, salvation accomplished, inheritance received, redemption obtained "once") and *aionios* describes the permanence of the outcome. If "eternal redemption" and "eternal judgment" describe permanent results of completed acts, the same pattern applies to "eternal punishment" — a punishment whose result is permanent, not a process that continues endlessly.

## Section D: "For Ever and Ever" in Revelation

**13. The phrase "for ever and ever" (*eis tous aionas ton aionon*, literally "unto the ages of the ages") is used approximately 19 times for God and Christ in doxological passages. It is used in judgment contexts only 3 times — all in Revelation (14:11; 19:3; 20:10). What does this distribution suggest about whether these three judgment passages should be read as literal didactic teaching or as apocalyptic imagery?**

The distribution suggests caution. The formula is overwhelmingly doxological (praising God/Christ). The three judgment uses all occur within Revelation, a book written in apocalyptic vision language — the least literal genre in the NT. Three apocalyptic uses should not override the clear didactic teaching found elsewhere in the NT about the fate of the wicked (destruction, perishing, death).

**14. Read Revelation 14:11. "The smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever." Now read Isaiah 34:10, where the smoke of Edom's judgment "shall go up for ever." Is Edom still burning? If the OT "smoke ascending forever" described a completed judgment, what does the same language in Revelation describe?**

Edom is not still burning. The OT precedent is clear: "smoke ascending forever" describes a completed, irreversible judgment — not an ongoing fire. Revelation 14:11 uses the same formula from the same vocabulary tradition (the LXX translates the Isaiah passage with the same Greek words). The OT pattern, where every verifiable "smoke ascending forever" case describes a judgment that ended, governs the reading of the same formula in Revelation.

> "The smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever." — Revelation 14:11

> "It shall not be quenched night nor day; the smoke thereof shall go up for ever." — Isaiah 34:10

**15. Read Revelation 20:9-10. In verse 9, fire comes down from heaven and "devoured" (*katesthion* — consumed/destroyed) the human enemies. In verse 10, the devil, the beast, and the false prophet are "tormented day and night for ever and ever." Note that the beast and false prophet are symbolic entities (Rev 19:20). Are the human enemies given the same fate as the devil and the symbolic entities, or a different fate?**

A different fate. The human enemies are "devoured" — destruction vocabulary. The devil, beast, and false prophet are "tormented" — but the beast and false prophet are symbolic entities (not literal humans), and the devil is a non-human spirit being. The text itself distinguishes between what happens to human enemies (devoured/consumed) and what happens to the devil and symbolic entities (tormented). Extending the devil's fate to all the wicked requires adding something the text does not say.

> "And fire came down from God out of heaven, and devoured them." — Revelation 20:9

> "And the devil that deceived them was cast into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and the false prophet are, and shall be tormented day and night for ever and ever." — Revelation 20:10

## Explicit or Inference? — Answers

**A. "*Aionios* is used for past time in Romans 16:25, 2 Timothy 1:9, and Titus 1:2 — proving it does not inherently mean 'endless future.'"**
- **Explicit.** Each passage can be checked directly. The KJV translates *aionios* as "since/before the world began" in these verses. The observation that *aionios* refers to past time is a textual fact. The conclusion that a word used for past time does not inherently mean "endless future" is a necessary implication — no interpretation required.

**B. "*Aionios* always means 'eternal/endless' regardless of context."**
- **Inference.** No verse states this rule. The claim is contradicted by the three passages where *aionios* refers to past time (Rom 16:25; 2 Tim 1:9; Tit 1:2) and by Strong's own definition, which notes the word is "also used of past time."

**C. "Jude uses *aidios* (G126) for the angels' chains and *aionios* (G166) for Sodom's fire in adjacent verses."**
- **Explicit.** This is directly observable in the Greek text of Jude 1:6-7. Any reader can verify the two different words in adjacent verses by the same author.

**D. "Because the same adjective (*aionios*) modifies both 'punishment' and 'life' in Matthew 25:46, both must last exactly the same duration."**
- **Inference.** The observation that the same adjective modifies both nouns is explicit. But the conclusion that this requires identical duration is an added assumption. Titus 1:2 uses *aionios* in both temporal directions (future and past) in the same verse, and *olam* carries different durations for different subjects in the same book (Exo 15:18 vs. 21:6). The "same adjective = same duration" rule is not stated in any text — it is imported from outside.

**E. "The 'smoke ascending for ever and ever' in Revelation 14:11 teaches that all the wicked will experience literal endless conscious torment."**
- **Inference.** The verse uses the same "smoke ascending" formula found in Isaiah 34:10 for Edom — a judgment that has demonstrably ended. The passage is in apocalyptic genre (the least literal genre in the NT). Reading it as literal didactic teaching about the fate of all the wicked requires: (a) ignoring the OT precedent, (b) treating apocalyptic imagery as literal, and (c) extending the vision's imagery beyond what the text states.
