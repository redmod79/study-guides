# Study 15: The Strongest Case for Eternal Torment -- Advanced Study

*Examining the Best Arguments -- What Do the Texts Actually Say?*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section C: The Subject Gate

*Gate 1 in the E/N/I classification: Is the subject of the statement the same as the subject of the ECT claim (generic human wicked)?*

7. **Rev 14:9-11**: The subjects are "beast-worshippers" within John's apocalyptic vision. Are "beast-worshippers" equivalent to "all unsaved humans"? What must be added to extend this to a universal statement?

8. **Rev 20:10**: The three subjects are the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. None is a literal human being. The "tormented for ever and ever" formula (*basanisthesontai eis tous aionas ton aionon*) appears exactly once in the Bible -- here. Does the subject gate pass for a claim about human wicked?

9. **Rev 6:9-11**: The subjects are *psychas* (souls) under the altar in an apocalyptic vision. In the same chapter, personified horsemen ride colored horses, and Death is personified (Rev 6:8). The altar itself speaks in Rev 16:7. Does the subject gate pass for a claim about literal conscious disembodied souls?

10. **Luke 16:19-31**: The subject is a parabolic character (the rich man). ECT scholars acknowledge this describes hades (intermediate state), not the final lake of fire. Does this passage address the final fate of the wicked at all?

Evaluate: does *aionios* determine the nature of the noun, or the scope of the noun? Consider *olethros aionios* (2 Thess 1:9) -- "everlasting destruction." ## Section E: Cross-Referencing with Prior Studies

14. **Isa 34:10 and Rev 14:11** (cf. Study 7, *olam* in OT): Isaiah 34:10 uses the identical "smoke ascending forever" and "not quenched night nor day" language. The OT source describes completed destruction (animals inhabit the ruins). Apply SIS (Scripture Interprets Scripture, #4a): does the OT source text (direct-speech prophecy) interpret the Revelation imagery (apocalyptic vision)? Which genre is clearer?

15. **Isa 66:24, Mark 9:48, and Dan 12:2** (cf. Studies 5-6, destruction vocabulary): Isaiah 66:24 is the direct source for Mark 9:48. The word *dera'on* links Isa 66:24 to Dan 12:2 (only 2 OT occurrences). In Isa 66:24, the objects are *peger* (dead bodies). Apply SIS: does the OT source govern the meaning of the NT quotation and the Daniel parallel?

16. **2 Thess 1:9 and Matt 25:46** (cf. Study 6, *olethros*): Both use *aionios* with a penalty word. 2 Thess 1:9 is didactic epistle; Matt 25:46 is parabolic discourse. Apply SIS and genre hierarchy: does the didactic passage interpret the parabolic one? Does *olethros aionios* (everlasting destruction) clarify what *kolasis aionios* (everlasting punishment) looks like?

17. **Rev 20:9-10 and Rev 20:14-15** (cf. Study 11, Rev 20 torment passage): The text uses "devoured" for human enemies (v.9), "tormented" for the devil/beast/false prophet (v.10), and "second death" for humans entering the lake (vv.14-15). Apply the text's own vocabulary distinctions: does the text itself differentiate between the fate of symbolic entities and the fate of humans?
