# Study 15: The Strongest Case for Eternal Torment

*Examining the Best Arguments -- What Do the Texts Actually Say?*

---

> **A Note on How to Study**
>
> Throughout this series, we distinguish between what the Bible **explicitly says** and what people **infer** from the Bible. These are not the same thing.
>
> - **Explicit:** You can point to a verse and quote it. The text directly says this.
> - **Inference:** Someone claims the Bible teaches something, but no single verse says it. They have drawn a conclusion -- possibly a good one, possibly not -- but the conclusion itself is not in the text.
>
> This distinction is critical in this study. We are examining the 8 passages that ECT (Eternal Conscious Torment) scholars identify as their strongest biblical case. The question is not whether these passages are important -- they are. The question is: what does each text *actually say*, and what must be *added* to reach the ECT conclusion?

---

## Section A: The Revelation Passages

1. Read **Revelation 14:10-11**. The text says beast-worshippers "shall be tormented with fire and brimstone" and "the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night." What genre is this passage -- didactic teaching (direct instruction) or apocalyptic vision (symbolic imagery)?

2. Now read **Isaiah 34:10**. This OT passage says of Edom's judgment: "the smoke thereof shall go up for ever... it shall not be quenched night nor day." The exact same "smoke ascending forever" and "not quenched night nor day" language appears here. Now read **Isaiah 34:11-17**. What creatures inhabit Edom's territory afterward? Can animals live in a place that is still burning?

3. Read **Revelation 18:8-10, 21** and **Revelation 19:3**. Babylon is "utterly burned with fire" and destroyed "in one hour." Babylon is "thrown down, and shall be found no more at all." Then in 19:3 her "smoke rose up for ever and ever." If Babylon is demonstrably destroyed -- not still being tormented -- what does "smoke rising forever" mean in Revelation's own usage?

4. Read **Revelation 20:10**. Who are the three subjects that are "tormented day and night for ever and ever"? Are any of them ordinary human beings?

5. Now read **Revelation 20:9**. When human enemies face God's fire, what word does the text use for what happens to them? Does it say "tormented" or "devoured"?

6. Read **Revelation 20:14-15** and **Revelation 21:8**. When humans are cast into the lake of fire, what does the text call this event? Does it call it "torment" or "the second death"?

7. Read **Revelation 6:9-11**. What genre is this passage? In the same chapter, are the four horsemen (Rev 6:1-8) and the cosmic upheaval (Rev 6:12-14) meant to be taken literally? What does this suggest about how the fifth seal should be read?

---

## Section B: The Gospel Passages

8. Read **Matthew 25:46**. The text says the wicked go to "everlasting punishment." The Greek word for "punishment" here is *kolasis* (G2851). Look up *kolasis* -- does it mean "torment" (*basanismos*) or "punishment/penalty"?

9. The same verse uses the adjective *aionios* ("everlasting") for both outcomes -- "everlasting punishment" and "life eternal." Some argue this means both must have identical nature as well as identical duration. But does the adjective *aionios* determine the nature of the noun it modifies, or only the duration? Consider: does "everlasting life" and "everlasting destruction" have the same *nature* just because the same adjective appears?

10. Read **Mark 9:43-48**. Jesus warns about gehenna with "the worm that dieth not and the fire that is not quenched." He is quoting **Isaiah 66:24**. Read that passage. What are the subjects that the worm and fire act upon? The Hebrew word is *peger* -- does it mean living, conscious beings, or dead bodies (carcasses)?

11. Read **Luke 16:19-31**. Is this passage a parable or a doctrinal treatise? What is the stated teaching point -- what does Jesus say in verses 29 and 31? Does this passage describe the final fate of the wicked (the lake of fire), or does it describe hades (the intermediate state before judgment)?

---

## Section C: The Epistle and OT Passages

12. Read **2 Thessalonians 1:9**. Paul says the punishment is "everlasting destruction." The Greek word is *olethros* (G3639). What does *olethros* mean -- "destruction/ruin" or "ongoing conscious torment"? Check its other NT occurrences (1 Thess 5:3; 1 Cor 5:5; 1 Tim 6:9).

13. Some interpret 2 Thess 1:9 as "everlasting separation from God's presence" rather than "everlasting destruction." Does the word *olethros* mean "separation"? If someone redefines a word to mean something other than its lexical definition, is the resulting claim explicit or an inference?

14. Read **Daniel 12:2**. The wicked rise to "shame and everlasting contempt." The Hebrew word for "contempt" is *dera'on* (H1860), which occurs only twice in the entire OT -- here and in **Isaiah 66:24**. In Isaiah 66:24, who views the dead with *dera'on* -- is it the dead themselves experiencing contempt, or is it the living onlookers reacting to dead bodies (*peger*)?

---

## Section D: The Big Picture

15. Across all 8 passages, does any single text use the word *basanizo* (torment) or *basanismos* (torment/torture) for the final fate of generic human wicked in a didactic (direct teaching) context? Or does the torment vocabulary appear only in Revelation's apocalyptic framework?

16. When didactic passages -- epistles, direct teaching of Jesus -- describe the fate of the wicked, what vocabulary do they use? Compare Matthew 10:28 ("destroy soul and body in gehenna"), Romans 6:23 ("the wages of sin is death"), John 3:16 ("should not perish"), and 2 Thessalonians 1:9 ("everlasting destruction"). What is the dominant word family -- destruction/death or torment?
