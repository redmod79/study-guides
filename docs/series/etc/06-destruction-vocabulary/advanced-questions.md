# Study 6: Destruction Vocabulary -- Advanced Study

*Hebrew and Greek Word Studies on the Fate of the Wicked*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: Hebrew Destruction Words -- Abad (H6) and Shamad (H8045)

1. *Abad* (H6) occurs approximately 184 times in the OT and means "to perish, to destroy." Read **Psalm 37:20**, **Psalm 68:2**, **Psalm 73:27**, **Psalm 92:9**, and **Job 20:7**. In each verse, what is the subject of *abad*, and what do these verses collectively say about the fate of the wicked?

2. *Shamad* (H8045) occurs approximately 90 times and means "to destroy utterly, to exterminate." Read **Psalm 145:20**, **Psalm 37:38**, **Psalm 92:7**, and **Isaiah 13:9**. What action does *shamad* describe? In Psalm 145:20, what is the antithetical parallel to *shamad*?

3. The Septuagint (LXX) translates *abad* as *apollymi* (G622) 141 times -- a dominant mapping with a PMI score of 30.45. What does this translation pattern tell us about how pre-Christian Jewish scholars understood *abad*? What does it mean for NT passages that use *apollymi* for the fate of the wicked?

4. Read **Isaiah 26:14**. This verse uses both *shamad* (destroyed) and *abad* (perish). What does it say about the dead who have been destroyed? Can they rise? What has happened to their memory?

## Section B: Hebrew Destruction Words -- Kalah (H3615) and Shachath (H7843)

5. *Kalah* (H3615) occurs approximately 206 times and means "to consume, to finish, to complete." Read **Psalm 59:13**, **Psalm 104:35**, and **Psalm 73:19**. In Psalm 59:13, what is the stated result of consumption -- what does the psalmist say they should become?

6. *Shachath* (H7843) occurs approximately 147 times and means "to corrupt, to decay, to ruin." The LXX translates *shachath* as *diaphtheiro* (to rot thoroughly, to destroy) 48 times. How does the "decay/corruption" semantic field differ from the "torment" semantic field?

7. Read **Psalm 37:20**. This verse uses both *abad* (perish) and *kalah* (consume) together, with the added imagery of smoke. How do the two destruction verbs plus the smoke simile reinforce each other?

8. Compare **Psalm 59:13** ("consume them, that they may not be") with **Psalm 104:35** ("let the wicked be no more") and **Obadiah 1:16** ("as though they had not been"). All three use non-existence language. What Hebrew word appears in all of these, and what does it mean?

## Section C: Greek Destruction Words -- Apollymi (G622), Apoleia (G684), Olethros (G3639)

9. *Apollymi* (G622) is derived from *apo* + the base of *olethros*. Strong's defines it as "to destroy fully." It occurs approximately 92 times in the NT. Read **Matthew 10:28**, **John 3:16**, **Luke 13:3**, **Romans 2:12**, **1 Corinthians 15:18**, and **2 Peter 3:9**. In each passage, what is the subject, and how is *apollymi* used? Does any of these contexts suggest "ongoing torment" rather than "destruction"?

10. In **Matthew 9:17**, *apollymi* is used for wineskins that burst -- they "perish." What happens to burst wineskins? Are they tormented, or do they cease to function? What does this non-theological usage reveal about the word's meaning?

11. *Apoleia* (G684) is the noun form of *apollymi*. Strong's defines it as "ruin or loss." Read **Matthew 7:13**, **Philippians 3:19**, **2 Peter 3:7**, and **Romans 9:22**. In each passage, what is *apoleia* the destination or outcome of? Does any passage define *apoleia* as "torment"?

12. *Olethros* (G3639) occurs only 4 times in the NT and is etymologically related to *ollymi*, the root of *apollymi*. Strong's defines it as "ruin, i.e. death, punishment." Read **2 Thessalonians 1:9**, **1 Thessalonians 5:3**, and **1 Timothy 6:9**. What does it mean that the word defined as "ruin/death" is paired with "everlasting" (*aionios*) in 2 Thessalonians 1:9?

13. In **1 Timothy 6:9**, Paul uses *olethros* (destruction) and *apoleia* (perdition) together in the same sentence. What does the pairing of two destruction words tell you about the semantic field Paul is working in?

## Section F: Destruction vs. Torment Vocabulary

19. The Bible uses two distinct word families when discussing the fate of the wicked:
    - **Destruction family:** *apollymi* (G622), *apoleia* (G684), *olethros* (G3639)
    - **Torment family:** *basanizo* (G928), *basanismos* (G929), *kolasis* (G2851)

    These are different Greek words with different definitions. Approximately how many passages use destruction vocabulary for the fate of the wicked, and how many use torment vocabulary? What is the ratio?

20. Read **Revelation 14:10-11** (torment vocabulary, apocalyptic genre) and **Matthew 10:28** (destruction vocabulary, didactic genre). Which passage uses the more common vocabulary? Which passage is in the clearer genre (didactic vs. apocalyptic)?
