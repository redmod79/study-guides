# Study 13: The Lake of Fire and the Second Death -- Advanced Study

*Revelation 20:14; 21:8; 2:11; 20:6*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: Word Study -- *Thanatos Deuteros* (Second Death)

1. The phrase "second death" (*deuteros thanatos*) occurs four times in Scripture -- all in Revelation (2:11; 20:6; 20:14; 21:8). It appears nowhere else in the Bible. However, the Aramaic Targum tradition uses *mota tinyana* ("second death") in multiple OT passages (Deut 33:6; Isa 22:14; 65:6,15; Jer 51:39,57). In the Targums, *mota tinyana* consistently means dying a second time or exclusion from the world to come -- death vocabulary, not torment vocabulary. What does this pre-Christian Jewish usage reveal about the expected meaning of "second death"?

2. The word *thanatos* (G2288, "death") appears three times in Revelation 20:14 alone: "death (*thanatos*) and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death (*thanatos*)." The text chose *thanatos* to identify what the lake of fire does. The Greek language had *basanizo* (G928, "to torment") and *basanismos* (G929, "torment") available -- and in fact uses them in Rev 20:10 for non-human entities. Why is the vocabulary the author chose for the identification statement significant?

3. The adjective *deuteros* (G1208, "second") is an ordinal number implying a first. Read **Revelation 2:10-11**: "faithful unto death" (physical death -- the first death) is immediately paired with "not be hurt of the second death." What does the ordinal distinction tell you about the nature of the second death in relation to the first?

4. Read **Romans 6:23**: "The wages of sin is death (*thanatos*)." This is the same word used in "second death." Paul's statement is in a didactic epistle -- direct theological teaching. In the SIS (Scripture Interprets Scripture) framework, how does this didactic passage govern the interpretation of "second death" in Revelation's apocalyptic genre?

## Section B: The Lake of Fire as the Text's Own Interpretation

5. Read **Revelation 20:14**: "This is the second death." This follows the same "X is Y" interpretive formula used throughout Scripture for explaining symbolic imagery: "the seven good kine are seven years" (Gen 41:26), "Thou art this head of gold" (Dan 2:38), "the seven heads are seven mountains" (Rev 17:9), "the odours are the prayers of saints" (Rev 5:8). In each case, the symbolic image is explained in plain language. What is the symbolic image here, and what is the plain-language explanation?

6. The phrase "lake of fire" (*limne tou puros*) appears five times in Scripture -- all in Revelation (19:20; 20:10; 20:14; 20:15; 21:8). The Greek word *limne* (G3041) occurs 10 times in the NT: 5 literal uses in Luke for the Lake of Gennesaret, and 5 "lake of fire" uses in Revelation. Given that the phrase is unique to Revelation's apocalyptic vision and the text provides its own definition ("the second death"), what evidence supports reading the lake of fire as a literal place rather than as symbolic imagery the text itself interprets?

7. Five categories of subjects enter the lake: (a) the beast and false prophet (symbolic entities, cast "alive," Rev 19:20), (b) the devil (non-human, "tormented day and night for ever and ever," Rev 20:10), (c) death and hades (personified conditions, Rev 20:14), (d) humans not in the book of life (Rev 20:15, no torment formula), (e) eight categories of human wicked (Rev 21:8, identified as "the second death"). The text uses different vocabulary for different subjects. Document the vocabulary used for each category.

## Section C: Cross-Referencing with Prior Studies

8. In Study 12, we examined Revelation 20:10 ("tormented day and night for ever and ever"). The subjects of this torment formula are the devil, beast, and false prophet. In the present study, we see that when human subjects enter the lake (Rev 20:15; 21:8), the text uses "the second death" rather than the torment formula. How does this vocabulary distinction between subjects inform the question of what the lake does to humans?

9. In Study 11, we examined Revelation 14:9-11 ("the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever"). That passage uses *basanizo* (torment vocabulary) for "beast-worshippers" within a highly symbolic vision. Compare this with Revelation 20:14-15 and 21:8, where the text uses *thanatos* (death vocabulary) for humans. Are these the same vocabulary? Does the presence of torment vocabulary in Rev 14:10-11 change the vocabulary the text uses in Rev 20:14-15?

10. Read **Revelation 19:20-21**. The beast and false prophet are cast "alive" into the lake. The human remnant is "slain" (*apektanthesan*) with the sword. Read **Revelation 20:9**: fire from heaven "devoured" (*katephagen*) human armies. Document the vocabulary pattern: what verbs does the text use for symbolic entities versus human beings?

11. Read **Matthew 25:41**. The everlasting fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels." How does this primary designation -- prepared for non-human entities -- align with Revelation's pattern of applying the torment formula to non-human/symbolic entities?

## Section D: The Destruction of Death Itself

12. Death and hades are personified throughout Revelation: Death rides a pale horse (6:8), Christ holds keys of death and hades (1:18), death and hades deliver up the dead (20:13), death and hades are cast into the lake (20:14). Read these four passages and trace the personification. What does it mean that death itself is treated as an entity that can be destroyed?

13. The casting of death into the lake results in **Revelation 21:4**: "no more death." Cross-reference this with the following vocabulary chain:
   - **1 Corinthians 15:26**: "The last enemy that shall be destroyed (*katargeo*) is death."
   - **Isaiah 25:8**: "He will swallow up death in victory."
   - **1 Corinthians 15:54**: "Death is swallowed up in victory" (quoting Isa 25:8).
   - **Hosea 13:14**: "O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction."
   - **2 Timothy 1:10**: Christ "hath abolished (*katargeo*) death."
   What is the consistent vocabulary used for the fate of death across these five passages?

14. The ECT (Eternal Conscious Torment) position requires ongoing pain and suffering. Read **Revelation 21:4** again: "there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain." This is an unqualified, universal declaration. How does this statement function if conscious suffering continues eternally in the lake of fire?

## Section E: Counter-Arguments and Evaluation

15. An ECT defender argues: "Humans are cast into the same lake where the devil is tormented (Rev 20:10,15), so they must experience the same torment." Evaluate this claim. Does the text apply the same vocabulary to both? Does being cast into the same place necessarily mean experiencing the same outcome when the text uses different vocabulary for each subject?

16. An ECT defender argues: "The 'second death' is a metaphor -- it does not mean actual death but spiritual death, which is separation from God." Evaluate this claim. Does any verse define the second death as separation from God? Does the text qualify *thanatos* in the phrase "second death" as meaning something other than death?

17. An ECT defender argues: "Death and hades are cast into the lake, and they are abstract concepts, so the lake cannot mean literal death for anyone -- it must represent something else." Evaluate this claim. The text states that the casting of death into the lake results in "no more death" (Rev 21:4). Does the text itself explain what happens when death is cast in? Does this explanation support or undermine the claim?

18. Nave's Topical Bible classifies "DEATH - SECOND" and lists passages using death/destruction vocabulary (Ezek 18:4; Matt 10:28; Rom 6:23; 2 Thess 1:9; 2 Pet 2:12) alongside the four "second death" references. Nave's also classifies fire in judgment under "Of the destruction of the wicked" and classifies everlasting fire as FIGURATIVE. What does this traditional reference work's classification indicate about how the second death has historically been understood?
