# Study 13: The Lake of Fire and the Second Death -- Advanced Study Answer Key

*Revelation 20:14; 21:8; 2:11; 20:6*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: Word Study -- *Thanatos Deuteros* (Second Death)

**1. The phrase "second death" (deuteros thanatos) occurs four times in Scripture -- all in Revelation (2:11; 20:6; 20:14; 21:8). The Aramaic Targum tradition uses *mota tinyana* ("second death") in multiple OT passages. In the Targums, *mota tinyana* consistently means dying a second time or exclusion from the world to come -- death vocabulary, not torment vocabulary. What does this pre-Christian Jewish usage reveal about the expected meaning of "second death"?**

The Targum tradition demonstrates that pre-Christian Judaism understood "second death" as death vocabulary -- dying again, being excluded from future life. The Targums use *mota tinyana* in passages such as Deuteronomy 33:6, Isaiah 22:14, 65:6,15, and Jeremiah 51:39,57. In every case, the phrase describes dying a second time or being cut off from the world to come. No Targum passage defines the "second death" as eternal conscious torment or separation from God. This pre-existing semantic field -- death, not torment -- is the context in which John's audience would have understood the phrase.

**2. The word thanatos (G2288, "death") appears three times in Revelation 20:14 alone. The text chose thanatos to identify what the lake of fire does. The Greek language had basanizo (G928, "to torment") and basanismos (G929, "torment") available -- and in fact uses them in Rev 20:10 for non-human entities. Why is the vocabulary the author chose for the identification statement significant?**

The author had both vocabulary sets available and demonstrably knew both -- *basanizo* is used in Rev 20:10 just four verses earlier. When the text identifies what the lake of fire IS, the author chose *thanatos* (death) rather than *basanismos* (torment). This is a deliberate vocabulary choice. The text's own name for the lake's function is "death" -- a second death. If the author intended to convey that the lake produces torment, the vocabulary was at hand. The author chose a different word.

**3. The adjective deuteros (G1208, "second") is an ordinal number implying a first. Read Revelation 2:10-11. What does the ordinal distinction tell you about the nature of the second death in relation to the first?**

The ordinal creates a direct parallel: there is a first death (physical death, which all humans experience) and a second death (which follows for those not in the book of life). Rev 2:10-11 makes the pairing explicit: "faithful unto death" (physical death -- the first death) is paired with the promise of exemption from "the second death." The second death is a second instance of death -- a dying again -- not a fundamentally different category (such as torment or separation). The word "death" carries the same core meaning in both uses; only the ordinal adjective distinguishes them.

> "Be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life." -- Revelation 2:10
>
> "He that overcometh shall not be hurt of the second death." -- Revelation 2:11

**4. Read Romans 6:23: "The wages of sin is death (thanatos)." In the SIS framework, how does this didactic passage govern the interpretation of "second death" in Revelation's apocalyptic genre?**

Romans 6:23 is a didactic epistle -- the clearest genre of Scripture for direct theological teaching. It defines the penalty for sin as *thanatos* (death). The "second death" (*deuteros thanatos*) uses the same word. In the SIS (Scripture Interprets Scripture) framework, clearer genres govern more obscure genres: didactic governs apocalyptic. Paul's plain statement that the wages of sin is death governs the reading of Revelation's apocalyptic imagery. The shared vocabulary (*thanatos*) creates a verified SIS connection (#4a): the penalty for sin in didactic teaching (death) is the same word used for what happens in the lake of fire (the second death).

> "For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord." -- Romans 6:23

## Section B: The Lake of Fire as the Text's Own Interpretation

**5. Read Revelation 20:14: "This is the second death." This follows the same "X is Y" interpretive formula used throughout Scripture. What is the symbolic image here, and what is the plain-language explanation?**

The symbolic image is the lake of fire. The plain-language explanation is "the second death." The text follows the same interpretive pattern found throughout Scripture's visions: the symbolic object is named, then explained in plainer terms. In Genesis 41:26-27, the seven kine ARE seven years. In Daniel 2:38, "Thou art this head of gold." In Revelation 17:9, the seven heads ARE seven mountains. In Revelation 5:8, the odours ARE the prayers of saints. In Rev 20:14, the lake of fire IS the second death. The text interprets its own symbol: the fiery image is a way of depicting what is, at its core, a second death.

**6. The phrase "lake of fire" (limne tou puros) is unique to Revelation. The text provides its own definition ("the second death"). What evidence supports reading the lake of fire as a literal place rather than as symbolic imagery the text itself interprets?**

The text itself provides the interpretive framework: "This is the second death." The lake of fire is Revelation's own apocalyptic imagery, and the text explains what the imagery represents -- death. The phrase appears exclusively in a book filled with symbolic imagery (beasts, horns, seals, trumpets, bowls). The word *limne* (lake) in its other five NT uses refers to a literal body of water in Luke. In Revelation, it becomes "lake of fire" -- a construction that appears nowhere else in Scripture. The text does not treat this as a geographic location; it treats it as an image that is explained by the phrase "the second death."

**7. Five categories of subjects enter the lake. Document the vocabulary used for each category.**

| Subject Category | Reference | Vocabulary Used |
|---|---|---|
| Beast and false prophet (symbolic entities) | Rev 19:20 | Cast "alive" (*zontes*) into the lake |
| The devil (non-human supernatural being) | Rev 20:10 | "Tormented (*basanisthesontai*) day and night for ever and ever" |
| Death and hades (personified conditions) | Rev 20:14 | "Cast into" the lake; result: "no more death" (Rev 21:4) |
| Humans not in the book of life | Rev 20:15 | "Cast into the lake of fire" -- no torment formula |
| Eight categories of human wicked | Rev 21:8 | "Shall have their part in the lake... which is the second death" |
| Human armies (pre-lake) | Rev 20:9 | "Devoured" (*katephagen*) by fire from heaven |
| Human remnant (pre-lake) | Rev 19:21 | "Slain" (*apektanthesan*) with the sword |

The text uses torment vocabulary (*basanizo*) only for non-human/symbolic entities (the devil, beast, false prophet). For human subjects, the text uses death vocabulary (*thanatos*), destruction vocabulary (*katephagen* -- devoured), and killing vocabulary (*apektanthesan* -- slain). The vocabulary distinction is consistent and deliberate.

## Section C: Cross-Referencing with Prior Studies

**8. In Study 12, we examined Revelation 20:10. In the present study, we see different vocabulary for human subjects. How does this vocabulary distinction inform the question of what the lake does to humans?**

Study 12 established that the torment formula in Rev 20:10 is applied to three specific subjects: the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. Gate 1 (subject) and Gate 3 (genre) of the Tree 3 classification framework both flagged this passage: the subjects are non-human/symbolic, and the genre is apocalyptic. The present study confirms that when the text addresses human subjects in the lake (Rev 20:15; 21:8), it switches vocabulary -- from *basanizo* (torment) to *thanatos* (death). The text does not extend the torment formula to humans. It provides a different identification: "the second death." This vocabulary distinction means the torment of Rev 20:10 cannot be assumed to apply to all subjects in the lake without importing it across a vocabulary boundary the text itself maintains.

**9. In Study 11, we examined Revelation 14:9-11 ("the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever"). Compare this with Revelation 20:14-15 and 21:8. Are these the same vocabulary?**

They are not the same vocabulary. Revelation 14:10-11 uses *basanizo*/*basanismos* (torment vocabulary) for "beast-worshippers" within a highly symbolic vision (the third angel's message). Revelation 20:14-15 and 21:8 use *thanatos* (death vocabulary) for humans in the lake of fire. These are different Greek word families with different semantic ranges. *Basanizo* means "to torment, to test with a touchstone." *Thanatos* means "death." No lexicon defines one as the other. The presence of torment vocabulary in Rev 14:10-11 does not change the vocabulary that the text uses in Rev 20:14-15 -- those passages use death vocabulary, and each passage must be read with its own terms.

**10. Read Revelation 19:20-21 and 20:9. Document the vocabulary pattern for symbolic entities versus human beings.**

The text maintains a consistent vocabulary distinction:

- **Symbolic entities** (beast, false prophet): Cast "alive" (*zontes*) into the lake (Rev 19:20). They survive entry.
- **Human remnant** (same passage): "Slain" (*apektanthesan*) with the sword (Rev 19:21). They die.
- **Human armies** (Rev 20:9): "Devoured" (*katephagen*) by fire from heaven. They are consumed.
- **Human subjects generally** (Rev 20:14-15; 21:8): "Cast into the lake of fire" -- identified as "the second death."

The pattern is: symbolic entities are cast alive, survive, and are tormented. Human beings are slain, devoured, and die the second death. The text does not use the same verbs for both categories.

**11. Read Matthew 25:41. How does this primary designation -- prepared for non-human entities -- align with Revelation's pattern?**

Matthew 25:41 states the everlasting fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels" -- non-human entities. This aligns with Revelation's pattern: the torment formula is applied to the devil, beast, and false prophet (Rev 20:10) -- non-human/symbolic entities. The fire's primary design is for supernatural beings. When human beings encounter the lake of fire, the text identifies the result differently: "the second death." Both Matthew and Revelation point to the same distinction: the fire was prepared for the devil and his angels; humans who enter it experience a different outcome described with different vocabulary.

> "Then shall he say also unto them on the left hand, Depart from me, ye cursed, into everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels." -- Matthew 25:41

## Section D: The Destruction of Death Itself

**12. Death and hades are personified throughout Revelation. Trace the personification and explain what it means that death itself can be destroyed.**

The personification of death forms a narrative arc across Revelation:

- **Rev 6:8**: Death rides the pale horse -- an active agent, given authority to kill.
- **Rev 1:18**: Christ holds "the keys of hell and of death" -- authority over death is in Christ's hands.
- **Rev 20:13**: Death and hades "delivered up the dead which were in them" -- they are compelled to release their captives.
- **Rev 20:14**: Death and hades are "cast into the lake of fire" -- they are themselves destroyed.

The arc moves from death as a powerful agent to death as a conquered enemy. The text treats death as a force that can be -- and ultimately is -- permanently eliminated. If death itself is cast into the lake and the result is "no more death" (Rev 21:4), then the lake of fire functions as the mechanism by which death is permanently destroyed.

**13. Cross-reference the destruction-of-death vocabulary chain across five passages.**

| Passage | Statement | Vocabulary |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Corinthians 15:26 | "The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death" | *katargeo* (G2673) -- to render inoperative, abolish |
| Isaiah 25:8 | "He will swallow up death in victory" | *bala* (H1104) -- to swallow, engulf |
| 1 Corinthians 15:54 | "Death is swallowed up in victory" | *katapino* (G2666) -- to swallow down |
| Hosea 13:14 | "O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction" | *qeteb* (H6986) -- destruction, plague |
| 2 Timothy 1:10 | Christ "hath abolished death" | *katargeo* (G2673) -- same as 1 Cor 15:26 |

The vocabulary is consistently destruction/abolition. Death is destroyed (*katargeo*), swallowed up (*bala*/*katapino*), and plagued with destruction (*qeteb*). Across Isaiah, Hosea, Paul, and Revelation, the fate of death itself is described with destruction vocabulary. Rev 20:14 (death cast into the lake) and Rev 21:4 ("no more death") stand as the final realization of what these passages anticipate.

**14. Revelation 21:4 states "no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain." How does this function if conscious suffering continues eternally?**

The statement is unqualified and universal: "no more death... no more pain." If the lake of fire produces eternal conscious suffering, then pain continues forever -- and Rev 21:4 is not true in an absolute sense. The ECT position must qualify the statement: "no more death or pain *for the redeemed* -- but eternal death and pain for the wicked continue." Yet the text does not add this qualification. The declaration follows immediately after death is cast into the lake (Rev 20:14) and describes the resulting state of reality ("the former things are passed away"). The conditionalist reading takes the text at face value: death itself has been destroyed; the wicked have perished in the second death; "the former things" -- including the wicked who died the second death -- "are passed away." The result is genuinely "no more pain" in the entire created order.

> "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away." -- Revelation 21:4

## Section E: Counter-Arguments and Evaluation

**15. "Humans are cast into the same lake where the devil is tormented (Rev 20:10,15), so they must experience the same torment." Evaluate.**

The shared-destination argument assumes identical outcomes for all subjects. The text undermines this assumption: the text uses *basanizo* (torment) for the devil, beast, and false prophet (Rev 20:10) but *thanatos* (death) for humans (Rev 20:14-15; 21:8). In Rev 19:20-21, symbolic entities are cast "alive" into the lake while humans are "slain." In Rev 20:9, fire from heaven "devours" human armies. The text maintains a consistent vocabulary distinction between what happens to non-human/symbolic entities and what happens to humans. Being cast into the same lake does not require the same outcome -- the text itself provides different vocabulary for different subjects. Consider: death and hades are also cast into the same lake (Rev 20:14), and their outcome is destruction ("no more death" -- Rev 21:4), not torment.

**16. "The 'second death' is a metaphor -- it does not mean actual death but spiritual death, which is separation from God." Evaluate.**

No verse in the Bible defines the second death as separation from God. The concept of "death as separation" is a theological framework, not a textual statement. The text uses *thanatos* without qualifying it as metaphorical or redefining it as separation. The same word *thanatos* is used in Rom 6:23 ("the wages of sin is death"), Ezek 18:4 ("the soul that sinneth, it shall die"), and throughout the NT for the penalty of sin -- in all cases meaning death, not separation. To read "second death" as "separation from God" requires adding a concept the text does not contain. Furthermore, Rev 2:10-11 pairs the second death with literal physical death ("faithful unto death") -- suggesting the second death is in the same category (death), not a different category (separation).

**17. "Death and hades are abstract concepts cast into the lake, so the lake cannot mean literal death." Evaluate.**

The text itself explains the outcome: the casting of death and hades into the lake results in "no more death" (Rev 21:4). Death is destroyed. This parallels 1 Cor 15:26 ("the last enemy that shall be destroyed is death") and Isa 25:8 ("He will swallow up death in victory"). Rather than proving that the lake does not deal in death, the casting of death into the lake demonstrates that the lake destroys what enters it. Death enters the lake and ceases to exist. This supports -- rather than undermines -- the reading that the lake's function is the permanent elimination of its subjects.

**18. Nave's Topical Bible classifies "DEATH - SECOND" alongside death/destruction vocabulary passages. What does this indicate?**

Nave's -- a widely used, traditional reference work -- classifies the second death alongside Ezek 18:4 ("the soul that sinneth, it shall die"), Matt 10:28 ("destroy soul and body in gehenna"), Rom 6:23 ("the wages of sin is death"), 2 Thess 1:9 ("everlasting destruction"), and 2 Pet 2:12 ("made to be taken and destroyed"). These are death/destruction vocabulary passages. Nave's also classifies fire in judgment under "Of the destruction of the wicked" and classifies everlasting fire as FIGURATIVE. This indicates that the traditional understanding of the second death connected it to the broader biblical theme of death and destruction as the fate of the wicked -- not to a separate category of eternal conscious torment.

## Evidence Classification Exercise -- Answers

**1. "Rev 20:14 states: 'This is the second death' -- a direct predicate nominative identifying the lake of fire as the second death."**
- **Explicit (E).** The verse directly states this. The predicate nominative is observable grammar.

**2. "The text uses thanatos (death) vocabulary, not basanizo (torment) vocabulary, to identify what the lake does."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** Both sides accept that the text says "This is the second death" (*thanatos*) and that *basanizo* is not used in the identification statement. The observation is about which vocabulary the text chose -- an observable, unavoidable fact that follows from the E-items.

**3. "The second death means permanent cessation of existence -- the wicked die a second time and cease to exist."**
- **Inference (I) -- specifically I-A (Evidence-Extending).** This systematizes the death vocabulary (E: Rev 20:14 uses *thanatos*), the identification statement (E: "This is the second death"), the wages-of-sin-is-death principle (E: Rom 6:23), and the destruction-of-death theme (E/N: 1 Cor 15:26; Rev 21:4). Every component is text-derived. It is an inference because it extends the documented pattern to a categorical claim about the nature of the second death. Added: the conclusion that *thanatos* in "second death" means cessation -- a systematization, not a new concept.

**4. "The second death involves eternal conscious torment -- 'death' here is a metaphor for separation from God."**
- **Inference (I) -- specifically I-B (Competing Evidence).** FOR: The torment formula in Rev 20:10 (same lake); humans cast into the same lake (Rev 20:15). AGAINST: The text identifies the lake as "the second death" using *thanatos* (not *basanizo*); no torment formula for humans; the text chose death vocabulary; wages of sin is death (didactic); humans slain/devoured; death itself destroyed; "no more death." Added: (a) "death means separation from God" -- no verse defines the second death this way; (b) importing the torment formula from non-human entities to humans across a vocabulary boundary.

**5. "The torment formula is applied to the devil, beast, and false prophet (Rev 20:10), not to human subjects."**
- **Explicit (E).** Rev 20:10 directly states who is tormented. The absence of the torment formula in Rev 20:15 and 21:8 is an observable textual fact.

**6. "All subjects of the lake of fire experience the same fate -- since the devil is tormented, humans must also be tormented."**
- **Inference (I) -- specifically I-B (Competing Evidence).** This requires adding the assumption that shared destination = identical outcome. The text uses different vocabulary for different subjects (torment for non-human entities, death for humans, destruction for death itself). Added: the interpretive rule "same place = same outcome" when the text uses different vocabulary.

**7. "Death itself is destroyed -- cast into the lake results in 'no more death' (Rev 21:4), paralleling 1 Cor 15:26."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** Both sides accept that Rev 21:4 says "no more death" and that this follows the events of Rev 20:14. Both accept 1 Cor 15:26 says death is destroyed. The connection is immediate context (Rev 21:4 follows Rev 20:14) and shared vocabulary (*thanatos*/*katargeo*). Zero concepts added.

## What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said -- Answers

**What CAN be said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies):**

1. The "lake of fire" is unique to Revelation (five occurrences) and the "second death" appears four times, all in Revelation. The Aramaic Targum tradition uses "second death" (*mota tinyana*) consistently as death vocabulary -- dying a second time or exclusion from the world to come.

2. Rev 20:14 identifies the lake of fire as "the second death" -- a direct predicate nominative following the "X is Y" interpretive formula used throughout Scripture's visions. The text uses *thanatos* (death) vocabulary, not *basanizo* (torment) vocabulary, to identify the lake's function.

3. Five categories of subjects enter the lake, and the text uses different vocabulary for different subjects: torment for the devil, beast, and false prophet (non-human/symbolic); death for human subjects; destruction for death and hades.

4. The torment formula ("tormented day and night for ever and ever") is applied to the devil, beast, and false prophet (Rev 20:10). When humans enter the lake, the text's term is "the second death" (Rev 20:14-15; 21:8). No torment formula is stated for human subjects.

5. Rev 2:11 promises overcomers will "not be hurt of the second death" (harm/injury language). Rev 20:6 states the second death has no "power" (*exousia* -- authority) over first-resurrection participants. Both passages use harm/authority language consistent with death terminology.

6. The casting of death and hades into the lake results in "no more death" (Rev 21:4), paralleling 1 Cor 15:26 ("the last enemy destroyed is death"), Isa 25:8 ("swallow up death"), Hos 13:14 ("O death, I will be thy destruction"), and 2 Tim 1:10 ("abolished death"). The destruction-of-death vocabulary is consistent across Isaiah, Hosea, Paul, and John.

7. Rom 6:23 states "the wages of sin is death" (*thanatos*) -- the same word used in "second death." The penalty for sin is death in didactic teaching; the lake of fire is "the second death" in apocalyptic vision.

8. Matt 25:41 states the everlasting fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels" -- its primary designation is for non-human entities.

9. The "second death" is distinguished from the first death (physical death) by the ordinal adjective *deuteros*. Rev 2:10-11 pairs the two deaths in immediate context.

**What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied -- requires adding something):**

1. **It cannot be said that the "second death" means eternal conscious torment.** The text defines the second death using *thanatos* (death), not *basanizo* (torment). No torment formula is applied to humans at the lake. The claim requires adding "death means torment" -- a concept the text does not contain.

2. **It cannot be said that the "second death" means separation from God.** No verse defines the second death as separation from God. The text uses *thanatos* without qualifying it as separation. The "death as separation" concept is a theological framework applied to the text, not derived from it.

3. **It cannot be said that all subjects of the lake of fire experience the same fate.** The text uses "tormented" for the devil, beast, and false prophet; "devoured" for human armies; "slain" for the human remnant; and "second death" for humans in the lake. The vocabulary differs by subject category.

4. **It cannot be said that the lake of fire is the same as gehenna, sheol, or hades.** Hades is explicitly cast INTO the lake of fire (Rev 20:14) -- they are not the same. Gehenna and the lake of fire are never equated in the text. These are distinct terms.

5. **It cannot be said that the "lake of fire" concept appears outside Revelation.** The phrase occurs five times, all in Revelation. No OT passage, no Gospel, and no Epistle uses "lake of fire."
