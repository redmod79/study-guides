# Study 14: Judgment Passages — Advanced Study Answer Key

*Rev 20:11-15, Matt 25:31-46, Dan 12:2, Isa 66:22-24, John 5:28-29, 2 Thess 1:7-10, Heb 10:26-31, Rom 2:5-11*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** — The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** — No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed — every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** — Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: Revelation 20:11-15 — Vocabulary Analysis

**1. Revelation 20:14 uses a predicate nominative construction: "This is the second death" (*houtos ho thanatos ho deuteros*). The text identifies the lake of fire as *thanatos* (death). Revelation 20:10 uses *basanizo* (torment) for the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. When the text transitions to human subjects (20:14-15), what vocabulary shift occurs? What is the significance of the text choosing *thanatos* over *basanizo* for humans?**

The text makes a deliberate vocabulary shift. For the three named subjects (devil, beast, false prophet) in 20:10, the text uses *basanizo* (torment). For human subjects in 20:14-15, the text switches to *thanatos* (death) — "the second death." If the author intended the same fate for humans, the simplest way to communicate that would be to use the same vocabulary. The shift from *basanizo* to *thanatos* when the subject changes from supernatural/symbolic entities to humans is a vocabulary choice the text makes, and reading *basanizo* back into *thanatos* requires overriding the author's word selection.

**2. The beast and the false prophet (Rev 19:20) are symbolic entities within Revelation's apocalyptic framework — they represent systems, not individual human beings. The devil is a supernatural being. If the three subjects of Rev 20:10 include one supernatural being and two symbolic entities, what hermeneutical step is required to extend their fate (*basanizo*) to all individual human beings in Rev 20:15?**

Extending the fate of the three named subjects to all human beings requires two hermeneutical steps the text does not make: (1) treating symbolic entities (the beast and false prophet are political/religious systems in Revelation's apocalyptic framework) as representative of individual humans; (2) importing the vocabulary used for those specific subjects (*basanizo*) into the vocabulary used for human subjects (*thanatos*). The text itself, when describing human fate, changes vocabulary. The extension is an inference — possibly a reasonable one — but the text does not make it.

**3. Rev 20:12-13 repeat the phrase "judged according to their works" twice. This judgment-by-works formula is consistent across the NT (Matt 16:27; 2 Cor 5:10; Rom 2:6). What does the emphasis on works-based judgment suggest about the nature of the outcome — is it a uniform fate for all the unsaved, or a proportional response?**

The repeated emphasis on works-based judgment suggests proportional response. If every unsaved person received the identical fate regardless of their deeds, the elaborate process of opening books and judging "according to their works" would serve no purpose. The double emphasis on works implies the judgment produces differentiated outcomes proportional to what each person has done. This is consistent across multiple NT passages.

> "And the dead were judged out of those things which were written in the books, according to their works." — Revelation 20:12

## Section B: Matthew 25:46 — *Kolasis* Word Study

**4. *Kolasis* (G2851) occurs only twice in the NT: Matt 25:46 ("punishment") and 1 John 4:18 ("fear hath torment/punishment"). It derives from *kolazo* (G2849), meaning "to curtail, prune, restrain." In classical Greek, Aristotle distinguished *kolasis* (corrective/restraining punishment) from *timoria* (retributive vindication). In the LXX, *kolasis* appears in contexts of capital punishment (Wisdom 19:4; 2 Macc 4:38). Given the full lexical range, does *kolasis* inherently mean "ongoing conscious suffering"?**

No. *Kolasis* does not inherently mean ongoing conscious suffering. Its root (*kolazo*: to curtail, prune) suggests restraint or cutting off, not prolonged suffering. The Aristotelian distinction classified *kolasis* as corrective/restraining versus *timoria* as retributive/vindictive. In the LXX, *kolasis* appears in contexts where the penalty inflicted is death — Wisdom 19:4 (the Egyptians dying in the Red Sea) and 2 Maccabees 4:38 (the execution of Andronicus). The LXX usage is particularly significant because it demonstrates the word was commonly associated with capital punishment. *Kolasis* means penalty/infliction. What kind of penalty is a separate question the word itself does not answer — and the didactic passages (2 Thess 1:9, Heb 10:27, Rom 2:12) specify that penalty as destruction.

**5. Matt 25:46 uses *aionios* to modify both *kolasis* (punishment) and *zoe* (life): *kolasin aionion* and *zoen aionion*. Some argue the same adjective requires identical duration and nature for both outcomes. Evaluate this argument: does *aionios* determine the nature of what it modifies, or only the duration/permanence? If "everlasting destruction" (*olethron aionion*, 2 Thess 1:9) is Paul's vocabulary for the same eschatological event, what does the didactic passage contribute to interpreting the parabolic discourse?**

*Aionios* modifies the permanence or duration of the noun it accompanies; it does not determine the nature of that noun. "Everlasting life" means life that is everlasting. "Everlasting destruction" (2 Thess 1:9) means destruction that is everlasting — not destruction that is ongoing as a process, but destruction whose result is permanent. By the same logic, "everlasting punishment" means punishment whose result is everlasting — not necessarily a punishment that is an ongoing conscious process. The "same adjective = same nature" argument fails because adjectives modify nouns; they do not transform the noun's meaning. *Aionios* + *kolasis* = permanent penalty. *Aionios* + *olethros* = permanent destruction. Paul's didactic epistle (2 Thess 1:9) uses *olethron aionion* for the same eschatological event Jesus describes in Matt 25:46. The didactic genre has greater interpretive clarity than parabolic discourse, and Paul's word choice specifies the nature of the penalty: destruction.

**6. Matt 25:31-46 is a parable (or parabolic discourse). 2 Thess 1:7-10 is a didactic epistle — Paul directly teaching a church about the same eschatological event (the Lord's return, the fate of the wicked). When a didactic passage and a parabolic passage describe the same event, which genre has greater interpretive clarity? How does this affect the relative weight of *kolasis* (Matt 25:46) versus *olethros* (2 Thess 1:9)?**

Didactic passages have greater interpretive clarity than parabolic passages. Parables teach through imagery and analogy; their details may serve the narrative rather than functioning as doctrinal definitions. Didactic epistles contain direct theological instruction intended to be understood plainly. When both genres describe the same event (the Lord's coming, the judgment of the wicked), the didactic passage governs the interpretation of the parabolic one. This means *olethros* (destruction — 2 Thess 1:9, didactic) interprets *kolasis* (penalty — Matt 25:46, parabolic): the everlasting penalty is everlasting destruction. The SIS principle (Scripture Interprets Scripture) applies: shared subject matter, same eschatological event, and the didactic genre takes precedence over the parabolic genre in clarity.

## Section C: Daniel 12:2 and Isaiah 66:24 — The *Dera'on* Connection

**7. *Dera'on* (H1860) occurs only twice in the entire OT: Dan 12:2 ("everlasting contempt") and Isa 66:24 ("abhorring"). In Isaiah 66:24, the objects of *dera'on* are *peger* (dead bodies/carcasses — 22 OT occurrences, always meaning corpse). The living "go forth and look upon" the carcasses. Does *dera'on* describe the experience of the dead, or the reaction of the living who view the dead?**

*Dera'on* describes the reaction of the living who view the dead — not the experience of the dead themselves. In Isaiah 66:24, the structure is: onlookers go forth, they look upon *peger* (dead bodies), and those dead bodies are an *dera'on* (abhorring) "unto all flesh." The *dera'on* is what "all flesh" (the living onlookers) feel when they view the carcasses. The dead bodies do not experience the abhorrence — they are the objects of it. Since *dera'on* occurs only twice in the entire OT, and its one other occurrence (Dan 12:2) is the passage in question, the Isaiah 66:24 usage is the only independent data point for the word's function. It describes the observers' reaction to the dead.

**8. Dan 12:2 uses *olam* for both outcomes: "everlasting life" (*chayyei olam*) and "everlasting contempt" (*dera'on olam*). Some argue that because *olam* modifies both, both outcomes must have identical duration and character. Evaluate this claim using the data from Study 7: In Psalm 90:2, *olam* describes God (genuinely eternal). In Exodus 21:6, *olam* describes slave service (one lifetime). In Jonah 2:6, *olam* describes three days. Does the "same adjective = same nature" argument hold across documented *olam* usage?**

The "same adjective = same nature" argument does not hold. *Olam* takes its duration from the nature of the subject it modifies — not the reverse. The same word describes God (genuinely eternal — Ps 90:2), slave service (one lifetime — Exo 21:6), Jonah's confinement (three days — Jon 2:6), and institutions that ended (Aaronic priesthood — Exo 40:15; Heb 7:12). If the same-adjective-same-duration rule were valid, slave service would need to be as eternal as God. The observable evidence from 400+ *olam* uses demonstrates that the word's force varies with the subject it modifies. In Dan 12:2, "everlasting life" is genuinely eternal because life with God is genuinely eternal. "Everlasting contempt" is permanent because the contempt (the living's reaction to the destroyed) never ceases — but this does not require the objects of that contempt to be conscious.

**9. Isaiah 66:16-17 states the transgressors are "slain" (*chalal*) and "consumed" (*suph*) before verse 24 describes their carcasses. The sequence is: (1) judgment by fire and sword, (2) slain, (3) consumed, (4) carcasses viewed with abhorrence. Does this sequence support reading Isaiah 66:24 as describing conscious beings, or as describing dead bodies that result from the judgment?**

The sequence decisively supports reading Isaiah 66:24 as describing dead bodies. The transgressors are first slain (v.16 — *chalal*, killed by sword/fire) and consumed (v.17 — *suph*, finished/consumed together), and only after these events does verse 24 describe the onlookers viewing their *peger* (carcasses). Reading verse 24 as describing conscious beings requires ignoring the explicit death/consumption vocabulary in verses 16-17 and the meaning of *peger* (which never refers to a living person). The passage is a sequential narrative: judgment, death, consumption, then viewing of remains.

> "For by fire and by his sword will the LORD plead with all flesh: and the slain of the LORD shall be many." — Isaiah 66:16

## Section D: John 5 — *Krisis* in Context

**10. *Krisis* (G2920) appears in John 5:22, 24, 27, 29, and 30. In verse 22, the Father has committed "all judgment" (*krisis*) to the Son. In verse 27, the Son has "authority to execute judgment" (*krisis*). In verse 24, the believer "shall not come into condemnation" (*krisis*). In verse 29, the wicked face "the resurrection of damnation" (*krisis*). Across all five uses in this chapter, does *krisis* describe a judgment process/authority, or does it specify the nature of the outcome (torment vs. destruction)?**

*Krisis* consistently describes a judgment process or authority throughout John 5. The Father commits the act of judging to the Son (v.22). The Son has authority to execute judgment (v.27). The believer escapes the judgment process (v.24). The wicked face a resurrection unto judgment (v.29). In no instance does *krisis* specify whether the outcome of that judgment is torment, destruction, or something else. It describes the judicial decision, not the sentence's nature. The word tells us there will be a judgment — it does not tell us what the judgment produces. Other passages (2 Thess 1:9, Heb 10:27, Rom 2:12) specify the outcome using destruction vocabulary.

**11. John 5:24 states the believer "is passed from death (*thanatos*) unto life (*zoe*)." The binary is death versus life — not torment versus life. What does this binary tell you about what *krisis* (judgment, v.29) leads to for those who fail the judgment?**

The binary Jesus establishes is death versus life. The believer escapes *krisis* (judgment) and passes "from death unto life." The logical corollary: those who do not pass from death unto life remain in death. If the binary were torment versus life, Jesus would have said "passed from torment unto life" — but He said death. This is consistent with the destruction/death vocabulary used across the other judgment passages. The *krisis* leads to *thanatos* (death), and the alternative is *zoe* (life).

> "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." — John 5:24

## Section E: 2 Thessalonians 1:9 — *Olethros* and the *Apo* Question

**12. *Olethros* (G3639) appears four times in the NT: 1 Cor 5:5 ("destruction of the flesh"), 1 Thess 5:3 ("sudden destruction"), 2 Thess 1:9 ("everlasting destruction"), 1 Tim 6:9 ("destruction and perdition"). In all four, the word means destruction or ruin. An ECT argument redefines *olethros* as "ongoing conscious existence in a state of ruin" rather than actual destruction. What lexical evidence would be required to sustain this redefinition, and does such evidence exist?**

To sustain the redefinition, one would need: (a) at least one clear NT or LXX usage of *olethros* meaning "conscious ongoing existence in ruin" rather than destruction; (b) a lexical authority that defines *olethros* as something other than destruction/ruin; (c) a contextual marker in 2 Thess 1:9 that signals the word is being used atypically. None of these exist. All four NT occurrences mean destruction. No major lexicon defines *olethros* as "ongoing conscious existence." The context of 2 Thess 1:8 (fire as instrument of vengeance) and the shared vocabulary with Heb 10:27 (fire devours) support the plain meaning. Redefining *olethros* to mean its opposite — not destruction but ongoing existence — overrides the word's lexical meaning. This is an I-D (counter-evidence external) inference.

**13. 2 Thess 1:9 reads "everlasting destruction *from* (*apo*) the presence of the Lord." The preposition *apo* can mean "away from" (separation) or "from" (source/proceeding from). Paul's language (*apo prosopou... tes doxes tes ischuos*) closely echoes the LXX of Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, where people flee FROM God's terrifying manifested glory. In Isaiah 2, does destruction come from being separated from God's presence, or from God's presence as the source of the judgment? How does this OT allusion affect the reading of *apo* in 2 Thess 1:9?**

In Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, the people flee into rocks and caves "from the terror of the LORD, and from the glory of his majesty." The destruction comes FROM God's presence — His glory is the source of the terror and judgment, not something they are separated from while continuing to exist. The Isaiah 2 allusion supports reading *apo* as "proceeding from" (source): the everlasting destruction proceeds from God's glorious presence. While both readings of *apo* remain grammatically possible, the Isaiah 2 background — which Paul's language closely echoes — favors the source reading: God's manifested glory is the source from which the destruction comes, not a location from which the wicked are merely banished.

**14. 2 Thess 1:8 describes the Lord "in flaming fire taking vengeance." The fire is an instrument of judgment — vengeance vocabulary. Compare this with Heb 10:27 (fire "shall devour") and Isa 66:16 (judgment by fire and sword -> slain). Across these three passages, what does fire consistently do to its subjects — preserve them in torment, or destroy/consume them?**

Across all three passages, fire destroys or consumes its subjects. In 2 Thess 1:8, fire is the instrument of vengeance, and the result is "destruction" (*olethros*, v.9). In Heb 10:27, fire "devours" (*katesthio* — consumes/eats up) the adversaries. In Isa 66:16, fire and sword produce "slain" — dead people whose carcasses are then viewed (v.24). The pattern is consistent: fire in judgment passages is an agent of destruction, not an agent of ongoing conscious preservation. No judgment-fire passage describes fire preserving its subjects alive in perpetual torment.

## Section F: Hebrews 10:26-31 — The "Sorer Punishment" Question

**15. Heb 10:28 establishes the paradigm: "He that despised Moses' law died without mercy under two or three witnesses." The punishment under the old covenant was death. Heb 10:29 asks: "Of how much sorer punishment (*timoria*, G5098) shall he be thought worthy?" *Timoria* occurs only once in the NT. If the baseline punishment (v.28) is death, and the new-covenant punishment is "sorer," does the text specify that the specific way it is sorer is duration of consciousness? Or could it be sorer in scope (body and soul rather than body alone)?**

The text does not specify the manner in which the punishment is "sorer." It states only that it is worse than death under the Mosaic law. The ECT inference fills in this blank with "duration of conscious suffering." But the passage's own vocabulary fills it in differently: fire "devours" (v.27), and the alternative to salvation is "perdition" (*apoleia* — destruction, v.39). Additionally, Jesus states in Matt 10:28 that God can "destroy both soul and body in gehenna" — destruction of soul and body is "sorer" than the old-covenant death penalty (body only) without requiring eternal conscious torment. *Timoria* (retributive penalty/vindication) does not specify the nature of the penalty; the context does. And the context uses destruction vocabulary.

> "He that despised Moses' law died without mercy under two or three witnesses: Of how much sorer punishment, suppose ye, shall he be thought worthy..." — Hebrews 10:28-29

**16. Heb 10:27 says fire will "devour" (*katesthio* — consume, eat up) the adversaries. Heb 10:39 contrasts "perdition" (*apoleia* — destruction) with "saving of the soul." The passage's own vocabulary — devour, perdition — is destruction terminology. If you read Heb 10:26-39 without any prior theological framework, what kind of punishment does the passage's own vocabulary describe?**

Read without a prior framework, the passage describes destruction. Its own vocabulary is consistent: fire that devours (v.27), a punishment that is sorer than death (v.28-29), and a binary between perdition (destruction) and salvation (v.39). The author frames the choice as destruction versus being saved. Every vocabulary choice in the passage points toward the penalty being the destruction of the apostate, not their preservation in an ongoing torment state. The contrast of *apoleia* (destruction/perdition) with "saving of the soul" is particularly telling — the alternative to being saved is being destroyed, not being tormented.

> "But we are not of them who draw back unto perdition; but of them that believe to the saving of the soul." — Hebrews 10:39

## Section G: Romans 2:5-12 — Immortality and Perishing

**17. Rom 2:7 states those who seek "immortality" (*aphtharsia*, G861) receive "eternal life." Rom 2:8-9 states the disobedient face "indignation and wrath, tribulation and anguish." Rom 2:12 states sinners "perish" (*apollymi*). Chart the sequence: what do the righteous seek and receive? What do the wicked face, and what is the final outcome word?**

| | Righteous | Wicked |
|---|---|---|
| Pursuit/Condition | Seek glory, honour, immortality (v.7) | Contentious, disobey truth (v.8) |
| Response | Eternal life (v.7) | Indignation, wrath, tribulation, anguish (v.8-9) |
| Final outcome | Eternal life | Perish — *apollymi* (v.12) |

The righteous seek immortality and receive eternal life. The wicked face wrath, tribulation, and anguish, and the final outcome is that they *perish*. The concluding vocabulary is *apollymi* — destruction — not eternal continuation in the wrath/tribulation state.

**18. If immortality must be sought and received (Rom 2:7), what does this imply about whether the wicked possess immortality? If they do not possess it, what is the logical consequence of "tribulation and anguish" (v.9) — does it continue forever (requiring immortality) or culminate in perishing (v.12)?**

If immortality must be sought and received with eternal life, then those who do not seek it do not receive it. Without immortality, the wicked cannot experience anything forever — conscious or otherwise. The tribulation and anguish (v.9) would be the experience of the judgment event, not an eternal state, and it culminates in *apollymi* (perishing, v.12). The passage's own logic rules out eternal conscious suffering: immortality is conditional, the wicked do not receive it, and the outcome is perishing.

**19. An ECT defender might argue that "tribulation and anguish" (Rom 2:9) describe eternal conscious suffering. But verse 12 uses *apollymi* (perish/destroy) as the outcome vocabulary. What is the relationship between the tribulation/anguish (v.9) and the perishing (v.12) — does the tribulation describe the eternal state, or the judgment process that results in perishing?**

The tribulation and anguish describe the judgment process, not the eternal state. Verse 12 provides the concluding vocabulary for the wicked's fate: *apollymi* — perish. If the tribulation were the eternal state, the word "perish" would contradict it (you cannot perish and also continue existing in tribulation). The natural reading: the wicked face tribulation and anguish as part of the judgment event, and the result of that judgment is that they perish. The text adds nothing about the tribulation continuing forever — that duration must be imported from outside the passage.

## Section H: Counter-Arguments

**20. An ECT defender might argue: "The parallel structure of Matt 25:46 — same adjective (*aionios*) for both outcomes — proves the punishment must be as endless as the life." Respond using the following data.**

The same-adjective argument proves that the *aionios* applies to both outcomes — both are permanent/everlasting. But *aionios* modifies duration or permanence, not the nature of the noun. "Everlasting life" = life that is permanent. "Everlasting punishment" = punishment that is permanent. "Everlasting destruction" (*olethron aionion*, 2 Thess 1:9) = destruction that is permanent — not destruction that is an ongoing process. The adjective ensures permanence; it does not transform *kolasis* (penalty) into *basanismos* (torment). The word is *kolasis*, and Paul's didactic description of the same event uses *olethros* (destruction). The punishment is permanent destruction — everlasting in result, not in process.

**21. An ECT defender might argue: "2 Thess 1:9 means eternal separation from God's presence — ongoing conscious existence apart from God." Respond using the Isaiah 2 allusion, the lexical meaning of *olethros*, and the four NT uses of *olethros*.**

Three lines of evidence counter this argument. First, the Isaiah 2 allusion: Paul's language echoes the LXX of Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, where people flee FROM God's manifested glory — God's presence is the source of the destruction, not something they are separated from while continuing to exist. Second, lexical meaning: *olethros* means destruction, not separation. No lexicon defines *olethros* as "conscious existence apart from God." Redefining the word to mean its opposite (not destruction but ongoing existence) is an I-D inference — it overrides the lexical meaning. Third, the four NT uses (1 Cor 5:5; 1 Thess 5:3; 2 Thess 1:9; 1 Tim 6:9) all mean destruction/ruin. The ECT reading requires that one of the four uses means something fundamentally different from the other three without any contextual marker indicating the shift.

**22. An ECT defender might argue: "Heb 10:29 'sorer punishment' requires something worse than death — only eternal conscious torment qualifies." Respond using the passage's own vocabulary (devour, perdition), the paradigm of v.28 (death), and Jesus' statement in Matt 10:28 (destroy both soul and body in gehenna).**

The passage's own vocabulary defines the "sorer punishment" as destruction, not torment: fire devours (v.27), and the alternative to salvation is perdition/destruction (v.39). The claim that "only eternal conscious torment qualifies as worse than death" ignores the possibility that the destruction of both soul and body (Matt 10:28) is worse than the death of the body alone (Heb 10:28). Under the Mosaic system, the body could be killed. Under the new covenant, the soul itself faces destruction — a total annihilation that surpasses physical death in scope. The "sorer punishment" is sorer in comprehensiveness (body and soul), not necessarily in duration of consciousness. And the passage's own vocabulary confirms this reading: devour, perdition, destruction.

## Section I: Evidence Classification Exercise — Answers

**1. "Rev 20:14 identifies the lake of fire as 'the second death' using *thanatos* vocabulary, not *basanizo* vocabulary."**
- **Classification: E (Explicit)**
- The verse directly states "This is the second death." The vocabulary (*thanatos* vs. *basanizo*) is an observable textual fact.

**2. "Matt 25:46 uses *kolasis* (punishment), not *basanismos* (torment) — these are different Greek words."**
- **Classification: E (Explicit)**
- Observable lexical fact. The word in the text is *kolasis*. *Basanismos* does not appear.

**3. "The 'everlasting punishment' of Matt 25:46 is eternal conscious torment."**
- **Classification: I-B (Competing-Evidence Inference)**
- The text says *kolasis* (punishment), not *basanismos* (torment). The claim adds "conscious torment" as the meaning of *kolasis*. FOR: E7/E126 (same adjective *aionios* for both outcomes — Ambiguous). AGAINST: E8/E207 (kolasis = punishment, not torment — Plain), E9/E404b (kolasis, not basanismos — Plain), E19/E090 (2 Thess 1:9: olethros — Plain), E30/E418 (no basanizo in 8 passages — Plain). Resolution: Strong — 5 Plain items govern 1 Ambiguous item.

**4. "Isaiah 66:24 describes worm and fire acting on *peger* (dead bodies). *Peger* never means a living person in its 22 OT uses."**
- **Classification: E (Explicit)**
- The text uses *peger*. The lexical data (22 occurrences, all = dead body/corpse) is an observable fact.

**5. "The 'everlasting contempt' of Dan 12:2 means the wicked consciously experience shame forever."**
- **Classification: I-B (Competing-Evidence Inference)**
- The text says "everlasting contempt" (*dera'on*). The claim adds consciousness. FOR: E11/E288 (*olam* modifies both outcomes — Ambiguous). AGAINST: E12/E267 (*dera'on* = onlookers' reaction, 2 uses — Plain), E13/E192 (Isa 66:24 subjects are peger — Plain), N2/N060 (shared dera'on links both passages — Plain). Resolution: Strong — 3 Plain items govern 1 Ambiguous item.

**6. "*Olethros* (2 Thess 1:9) means 'ongoing conscious ruin,' not actual destruction."**
- **Classification: I-D (Counter-Evidence External Inference)**
- This redefines *olethros* from its lexical meaning (destruction) to mean its opposite (ongoing existence in ruin). All 4 NT uses mean destruction. No lexical authority supports the redefinition. The inference overrides the word's meaning, placing it at the lowest tier of evidence classification.

**7. "None of the 8 major judgment passages uses *basanizo* or *basanismos* for human wicked."**
- **Classification: E (Explicit)**
- Observable word-count fact. Anyone can verify which words appear in these passages. *Basanizo*/*basanismos* do not appear for human subjects in any of the eight.

**8. "The 8 judgment passages collectively describe permanent destruction/death as the fate of the wicked."**
- **Classification: I-A (Evidence-Extending Inference)**
- This systematizes the vocabulary pattern (E-items: thanatos, olethros, katesthio, apoleia, apollymi; N-items: vocabulary pattern, immortality sought/sinners perish) into a comprehensive conclusion. Every component comes from the E/N tables. It is an inference because it extends the pattern to a doctrinal claim. Subtype I-A: all components text-derived, conclusion aligns with the direction of the evidence.

## Section J: What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said — Answers

**What CAN be said (the Bible explicitly states or necessarily implies):**

1. The 8 major judgment passages collectively use destruction/death vocabulary (thanatos, olethros, katesthio, apoleia, apollymi) for the fate of the human wicked. None of the 8 uses basanizo (torment) for human subjects.

2. Rev 20:11-15 describes the Great White Throne judgment where humans are judged "according to their works." Those not in the book of life are "cast into the lake of fire," which the text identifies as "the second death" (thanatos). No torment vocabulary is used for human subjects.

3. Matt 25:46 states the wicked go to "everlasting punishment" (kolasin aionion) and the righteous to "life eternal" (zoen aionion). The word kolasis (punishment/penalty) appears only twice in the NT (Matt 25:46, 1 John 4:18). The text uses kolasis, not basanismos (torment).

4. Dan 12:2 states the dead awake to either "everlasting life" or "shame and everlasting contempt" (dera'on). Dera'on occurs only twice in the OT (Dan 12:2 and Isa 66:24). In Isa 66:24, the objects of dera'on are peger (dead bodies/carcasses).

5. Isa 66:24 describes the living going forth to look upon "carcases" (peger — dead bodies, 22 OT occurrences, always = corpse) of transgressors. The worm and fire act upon dead bodies. The context (vv.16-17) states the transgressors were "slain" and "consumed."

6. John 5:29 describes the "resurrection of life" and the "resurrection of damnation" (krisis — judgment/decision). The word krisis appears in vv.22, 24, 27, 29, 30 — all meaning judgment/decision, not torment.

7. 2 Thess 1:9 states the punishment is "everlasting destruction" (olethron aionion). Olethros appears 4 times in the NT, always meaning destruction/ruin. Paul's language echoes Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, where destruction proceeds FROM God's manifested glory.

8. Heb 10:27 states fire will "devour" the adversaries. Heb 10:28 uses death under Moses' law as the paradigm. Heb 10:29 asks about "sorer punishment" (timoria). Heb 10:39 contrasts "perdition" (apoleia — destruction) with "saving of the soul."

9. Rom 2:7 states those who seek "immortality" (aphtharsia) receive "eternal life." Rom 2:8-9 states the disobedient face "indignation and wrath, tribulation and anguish." Rom 2:12 states sinners "perish" (apollymi).

**What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied — requires adding something):**

1. **It cannot be said that any of the 8 judgment passages uses basanizo (torment) for the fate of human wicked.** The word does not appear in any of these passages in reference to humans. Claims of torment in these passages require adding vocabulary the text does not use.

2. **It cannot be said that kolasis (Matt 25:46) means "torment."** Kolasis means punishment/penalty (penal infliction). Its lexical meaning is not equivalent to basanismos (torment). The text says "punishment," not "torment."

3. **It cannot be said that dera'on (Dan 12:2) describes what the wicked experience.** The only other use of dera'on (Isa 66:24) describes the reaction of the living to dead bodies (peger). The word describes how the dead are viewed, not what they experience.

4. **It cannot be said that Isa 66:24 describes conscious beings.** The subjects are peger (carcasses/dead bodies). The word peger never refers to living persons in its 22 OT occurrences.

5. **It cannot be said that olethros (2 Thess 1:9) means "ongoing conscious ruin."** Olethros means destruction. In all 4 NT uses, it means destruction/ruin. Redefining the word to mean "conscious ongoing existence in ruined state" overrides its lexical meaning.

6. **It cannot be said that "sorer punishment" (Heb 10:29) specifically means eternal conscious torment.** The passage does not define the nature of the "sorer punishment." The passage's own vocabulary uses "devour" (v.27) and "perdition" (v.39) — destruction terms.

7. **It cannot be said that "tribulation and anguish" (Rom 2:9) are eternal.** The text does not specify the duration. The same passage uses "perish" (apollymi, v.12) as the concluding outcome vocabulary.

8. **It cannot be said that "weeping and gnashing of teeth" proves ongoing conscious torment.** The OT background for "gnashing of teeth" (brygmos ton odonton) consistently expresses anger and rage, not physical pain (Ps 35:16; 37:12; 112:10; Job 16:9; Lam 2:16; Acts 7:54). The phrase describes anger and grief at exclusion from the kingdom (Luke 13:28), not the experience of ongoing torment.
