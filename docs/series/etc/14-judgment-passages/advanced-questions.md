# Study 14: Judgment Passages — Advanced Study

*Rev 20:11-15, Matt 25:31-46, Dan 12:2, Isa 66:22-24, John 5:28-29, 2 Thess 1:7-10, Heb 10:26-31, Rom 2:5-11*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** — The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** — No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed — every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** — Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: Revelation 20:11-15 — Vocabulary Analysis

1. Revelation 20:14 uses a predicate nominative construction: "This is the second death" (*houtos ho thanatos ho deuteros*). The text identifies the lake of fire as *thanatos* (death). Revelation 20:10 uses *basanizo* (torment) for the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. When the text transitions to human subjects (20:14-15), what vocabulary shift occurs? What is the significance of the text choosing *thanatos* over *basanizo* for humans?

2. The beast and the false prophet (Rev 19:20) are symbolic entities within Revelation's apocalyptic framework — they represent systems, not individual human beings. The devil is a supernatural being. If the three subjects of Rev 20:10 include one supernatural being and two symbolic entities, what hermeneutical step is required to extend their fate (*basanizo*) to all individual human beings in Rev 20:15?

3. Rev 20:12-13 repeat the phrase "judged according to their works" twice. This judgment-by-works formula is consistent across the NT (Matt 16:27; 2 Cor 5:10; Rom 2:6). What does the emphasis on works-based judgment suggest about the nature of the outcome — is it a uniform fate for all the unsaved, or a proportional response?

## Section B: Matthew 25:46 — *Kolasis* Word Study

4. *Kolasis* (G2851) occurs only twice in the NT: Matt 25:46 ("punishment") and 1 John 4:18 ("fear hath torment/punishment"). It derives from *kolazo* (G2849), meaning "to curtail, prune, restrain." In classical Greek, Aristotle distinguished *kolasis* (corrective/restraining punishment) from *timoria* (retributive vindication). In the LXX, *kolasis* appears in contexts of capital punishment (Wisdom 19:4; 2 Macc 4:38). Given the full lexical range, does *kolasis* inherently mean "ongoing conscious suffering"?

5. Matt 25:46 uses *aionios* to modify both *kolasis* (punishment) and *zoe* (life): *kolasin aionion* and *zoen aionion*. Some argue the same adjective requires identical duration and nature for both outcomes. Evaluate this argument: does *aionios* determine the nature of what it modifies, or only the duration/permanence? If "everlasting destruction" (*olethron aionion*, 2 Thess 1:9) is Paul's vocabulary for the same eschatological event, what does the didactic passage contribute to interpreting the parabolic discourse?

6. Matt 25:31-46 is a parable (or parabolic discourse). 2 Thess 1:7-10 is a didactic epistle — Paul directly teaching a church about the same eschatological event (the Lord's return, the fate of the wicked). When a didactic passage and a parabolic passage describe the same event, which genre has greater interpretive clarity? How does this affect the relative weight of *kolasis* (Matt 25:46) versus *olethros* (2 Thess 1:9)?

## Section C: Daniel 12:2 and Isaiah 66:24 — The *Dera'on* Connection

7. *Dera'on* (H1860) occurs only twice in the entire OT: Dan 12:2 ("everlasting contempt") and Isa 66:24 ("abhorring"). In Isaiah 66:24, the objects of *dera'on* are *peger* (dead bodies/carcasses — 22 OT occurrences, always meaning corpse). The living "go forth and look upon" the carcasses. Does *dera'on* describe the experience of the dead, or the reaction of the living who view the dead?

8. Dan 12:2 uses *olam* for both outcomes: "everlasting life" (*chayyei olam*) and "everlasting contempt" (*dera'on olam*). Some argue that because *olam* modifies both, both outcomes must have identical duration and character. Evaluate this claim using the data from Study 7: In Psalm 90:2, *olam* describes God (genuinely eternal). In Exodus 21:6, *olam* describes slave service (one lifetime). In Jonah 2:6, *olam* describes three days. Does the "same adjective = same nature" argument hold across documented *olam* usage?

9. Isaiah 66:16-17 states the transgressors are "slain" (*chalal*) and "consumed" (*suph*) before verse 24 describes their carcasses. The sequence is: (1) judgment by fire and sword, (2) slain, (3) consumed, (4) carcasses viewed with abhorrence. Does this sequence support reading Isaiah 66:24 as describing conscious beings, or as describing dead bodies that result from the judgment?

## Section D: John 5 — *Krisis* in Context

10. *Krisis* (G2920) appears in John 5:22, 24, 27, 29, and 30. In verse 22, the Father has committed "all judgment" (*krisis*) to the Son. In verse 27, the Son has "authority to execute judgment" (*krisis*). In verse 24, the believer "shall not come into condemnation" (*krisis*). In verse 29, the wicked face "the resurrection of damnation" (*krisis*). Across all five uses in this chapter, does *krisis* describe a judgment process/authority, or does it specify the nature of the outcome (torment vs. destruction)?

11. John 5:24 states the believer "is passed from death (*thanatos*) unto life (*zoe*)." The binary is death versus life — not torment versus life. What does this binary tell you about what *krisis* (judgment, v.29) leads to for those who fail the judgment?

## Section E: 2 Thessalonians 1:9 — *Olethros* and the *Apo* Question

12. *Olethros* (G3639) appears four times in the NT: 1 Cor 5:5 ("destruction of the flesh"), 1 Thess 5:3 ("sudden destruction"), 2 Thess 1:9 ("everlasting destruction"), 1 Tim 6:9 ("destruction and perdition"). In all four, the word means destruction or ruin. An ECT argument redefines *olethros* as "ongoing conscious existence in a state of ruin" rather than actual destruction. What lexical evidence would be required to sustain this redefinition, and does such evidence exist?

13. 2 Thess 1:9 reads "everlasting destruction *from* (*apo*) the presence of the Lord." The preposition *apo* can mean "away from" (separation) or "from" (source/proceeding from). Paul's language (*apo prosopou... tes doxes tes ischuos*) closely echoes the LXX of Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, where people flee FROM God's terrifying manifested glory. In Isaiah 2, does destruction come from being separated from God's presence, or from God's presence as the source of the judgment? How does this OT allusion affect the reading of *apo* in 2 Thess 1:9?

14. 2 Thess 1:8 describes the Lord "in flaming fire taking vengeance." The fire is an instrument of judgment — vengeance vocabulary. Compare this with Heb 10:27 (fire "shall devour") and Isa 66:16 (judgment by fire and sword → slain). Across these three passages, what does fire consistently do to its subjects — preserve them in torment, or destroy/consume them?

## Section F: Hebrews 10:26-31 — The "Sorer Punishment" Question

15. Heb 10:28 establishes the paradigm: "He that despised Moses' law died without mercy under two or three witnesses." The punishment under the old covenant was death. Heb 10:29 asks: "Of how much sorer punishment (*timoria*, G5098) shall he be thought worthy?" *Timoria* occurs only once in the NT. If the baseline punishment (v.28) is death, and the new-covenant punishment is "sorer," does the text specify that the specific way it is sorer is duration of consciousness? Or could it be sorer in scope (body and soul rather than body alone)?

16. Heb 10:27 says fire will "devour" (*katesthio* — consume, eat up) the adversaries. Heb 10:39 contrasts "perdition" (*apoleia* — destruction) with "saving of the soul." The passage's own vocabulary — devour, perdition — is destruction terminology. If you read Heb 10:26-39 without any prior theological framework, what kind of punishment does the passage's own vocabulary describe?

## Section G: Romans 2:5-12 — Immortality and Perishing

17. Rom 2:7 states those who seek "immortality" (*aphtharsia*, G861) receive "eternal life." Rom 2:8-9 states the disobedient face "indignation and wrath, tribulation and anguish." Rom 2:12 states sinners "perish" (*apollymi*). Chart the sequence: what do the righteous seek and receive? What do the wicked face, and what is the final outcome word?

18. If immortality must be sought and received (Rom 2:7), what does this imply about whether the wicked possess immortality? If they do not possess it, what is the logical consequence of "tribulation and anguish" (v.9) — does it continue forever (requiring immortality) or culminate in perishing (v.12)?

19. An ECT defender might argue that "tribulation and anguish" (Rom 2:9) describe eternal conscious suffering. But verse 12 uses *apollymi* (perish/destroy) as the outcome vocabulary. What is the relationship between the tribulation/anguish (v.9) and the perishing (v.12) — does the tribulation describe the eternal state, or the judgment process that results in perishing?

## Section H: Counter-Arguments

20. An ECT defender might argue: "The parallel structure of Matt 25:46 — same adjective (*aionios*) for both outcomes — proves the punishment must be as endless as the life." Respond using the following data: (a) *Aionios* modifies the duration/permanence but does not determine the nature of what it modifies; (b) "everlasting destruction" (2 Thess 1:9) uses the same adjective for destruction — is the destruction ongoing or permanent-in-result?; (c) the word is *kolasis* (penalty), not *basanismos* (torment).

21. An ECT defender might argue: "2 Thess 1:9 means eternal separation from God's presence — ongoing conscious existence apart from God." Respond using the Isaiah 2 allusion (people fleeing FROM God's terrifying glory), the lexical meaning of *olethros* (destruction, not separation), and the four NT uses of *olethros*.

22. An ECT defender might argue: "Heb 10:29 'sorer punishment' requires something worse than death — only eternal conscious torment qualifies." Respond using the passage's own vocabulary (devour, perdition), the paradigm of v.28 (death), and Jesus' statement in Matt 10:28 (destroy both soul and body in gehenna).
