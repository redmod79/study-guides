# Study 1: What Is Man? — Advanced Study Answer Key

*Biblical Anthropology: Hebrew and Greek Word Studies*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** — The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** — No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed — every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** — Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: The Hebrew Word for "Soul" — *Nephesh* (H5315)

**1. *Nephesh* appears approximately 780 times in the Old Testament and is translated over 40 different ways. Using a concordance, find at least five different English words used to translate *nephesh*. What does this range tell you about whether *nephesh* simply means "immortal soul"?**

*Nephesh* is translated: soul, life, creature, person, body, dead body, self, desire, appetite, heart, mind, among many others. With 780 occurrences and 214 distinct translations in the KJV, the word covers a vast semantic range. It clearly does not mean "immortal soul" — it refers to the whole living being, life itself, or even a dead body. The assumption that *nephesh* = "immortal soul" imports a concept the word itself does not carry.

**2. In Genesis 2:7, the Hebrew verb is *hayah* (Qal Wayyiqtol 3ms) — "became." Does the grammar support "man received a soul" or "man became a soul"?**

The grammar supports "man *became* a living soul." The verb *hayah* means "to become" or "to be." The construction is: God formed man, breathed into him, and man *became* (*hayah*) a *nephesh chayyah*. There is no grammatical basis for reading this as receiving a soul as a separate component placed into a body. The soul is what the whole person *is*.

**3. In Genesis 1:20-21, 24, 30, and 2:19, animals are called *nephesh chayyah*. In Genesis 2:7, man is called *nephesh chayyah*. The KJV translates the animal occurrences as "living creature" and the human occurrence as "living soul." What does the identical Hebrew tell you?**

The Hebrew is identical in both cases: *nephesh chayyah*. The difference in English translation obscures this fact. Both animals and humans are *nephesh chayyah* — living beings animated by the breath of life. The term *nephesh* does not distinguish humans from animals. What distinguishes humans is the *imago Dei* (image of God) — Genesis 1:26-27, 9:6.

**4. In Leviticus 21:11 and Numbers 6:6, the Hebrew says *nephesh meth* — literally "dead soul." How does this usage affect the claim that the soul is immortal?**

Scripture itself uses the phrase "dead soul" (*nephesh meth*), translated "dead body" in English. If the soul were inherently immortal and could not die, the phrase "dead soul" would be self-contradictory. The Bible does not treat it as contradictory — it uses the phrase naturally because *nephesh* refers to the whole being, which can die.

**5. In Leviticus 17:11, 14, Scripture says "the *nephesh* of the flesh is in the blood." What does this tell you about whether *nephesh* refers to an immaterial, immortal entity?**

The *nephesh* is identified with the blood — a physical substance. This is a biological, material identification. An immaterial, immortal entity cannot be "in the blood." This confirms that *nephesh* refers to the life or vitality of the creature, not to a separable immortal component.

## Section B: The Hebrew Words for "Spirit" and "Breath" — *Ruach* (H7307) and *Neshamah* (H5397)

**6. *Ruach* appears 434 times and is translated "spirit," "wind," and "breath" among other ways. In Ecclesiastes 3:19, the text says humans and animals have "one *ruach*" (breath/spirit). If *ruach* meant an immortal conscious entity unique to humans, what problem would this create?**

If *ruach* meant an immortal conscious entity, then animals would also possess one, since Ecclesiastes 3:19 says humans and animals have "one *ruach*" (*ruach echad*). The text explicitly states man "has no preeminence above a beast" regarding this *ruach*. Either *ruach* is an immortal soul (in which case animals also have one), or it is the breath of life shared by all creatures. The latter reading is consistent with the text.

**7. *Neshamah* appears 25 times. Compare Genesis 2:7 (God breathed *neshamah* into man) with Genesis 7:22 (all creatures with *neshamah* in their nostrils). Do animals also have *neshamah*?**

Yes. Genesis 7:22 says "All in whose nostrils was the breath of life (*neshamah*)" — referring to all air-breathing creatures that died in the flood. The *neshamah* that God breathed into man is also attributed to animals. It is the breath of life, not a uniquely human immortal component.

**8. Read Isaiah 42:5, Acts 17:25, and Zechariah 12:1. Who is the source of breath and spirit? Is it inherent to humans, or given by God?**

Every passage identifies God as the source:
- Isaiah 42:5: God "giveth breath to the people upon [the earth], and spirit to them that walk therein."
- Acts 17:25: God "giveth to all life, and breath, and all things."
- Zechariah 12:1: The LORD "formeth the spirit of man within him."

Breath and spirit are *given* by God, not inherent to humans. At death, they *return* to God who gave them (Ecc 12:7). They were never "ours" — they are on loan.

**9. In the Septuagint (Greek OT), *neshamah* is typically translated *pnoe* (G4157) and *ruach* is translated *pneuma* (G4151). Both are breath/wind words. What does this mapping suggest about whether the translators understood these as conscious, immortal entities?**

The LXX translators chose breath/wind words to render both *neshamah* and *ruach*. *Pnoe* means "a blowing, breath." *Pneuma* means "wind, breath, spirit." The Greek-speaking translators did not use vocabulary suggesting a conscious, immortal soul — they used vocabulary consistent with the breath-of-life reading. This is significant because the LXX translators consistently used breath/wind vocabulary rather than terminology suggesting a conscious, immortal entity.

## Section C: Death as Reversal of Creation

**10. Construct the creation/death reversal from the text:**

- Creation (Gen 2:7): **dust** + **breath of life** = **living soul**
- Death (Ecc 12:7): **dust** returns to **earth** + **spirit** returns to **God**
- Result: The **living soul** ceases.

The formula works in one direction (creation) and reverses in the other (death). No third component — an immortal soul that continues — is mentioned in either process.

**11. Read Psalm 146:4 carefully. The Hebrew word for "thoughts" is *eshtonot* and the verb is *abad* (perish). What is the timing? Does the text say thoughts perish gradually, or "in that very day"?**

"In that very day" (*bayom hahu*) — the cessation is immediate. When the breath departs, thoughts perish *that same day*. The text does not describe a gradual fading or a transition to a different mode of thinking. It describes the end of all cognitive activity at the point of death.

**12. Read Job 34:14-15. What happens if God withdraws his *ruach* and *neshamah*? Does this apply only to humans or to "all flesh"?**

"All flesh shall perish together, and man shall turn again unto dust." Both *ruach* and *neshamah* are mentioned, and the effect of their withdrawal is universal — "all flesh" perishes. Elihu describes the same process as Ecclesiastes 12:7 and Psalm 104:29.

**13. Read Psalm 104:29. Compare with Job 34:14-15 and Ecclesiastes 12:7. How many authors describe the same death process?**

At least three authors — the Psalmist (Ps 104:29), Elihu/Job (Job 34:14-15), and Solomon (Ecc 12:7) — describe the identical process: God withdraws breath/spirit, the creature dies and returns to dust. This consistent testimony across multiple authors and books strengthens the classification of these as reliable descriptions of what death is.

## Section D: Immortality — Three Greek Words

**14. Look up the following Greek words and their occurrences:**

- **Athanasia** (G110) — "deathlessness." 3 NT occurrences. 1 Timothy 6:16: God "only [*monos*] hath immortality [*athanasia*]." 1 Corinthians 15:53-54: mortals "must put on" *athanasia* at the resurrection. *Athanasia* belongs to God alone and is given to believers at the resurrection — not at birth and not at death.

- **Aphtharsia** (G861) — "incorruptibility." 8 NT occurrences. Romans 2:7: believers "seek for" *aphtharsia*. 2 Timothy 1:10: Christ "brought life and immortality [*aphtharsia*] to light through the gospel." Something that must be sought and was brought to light by Christ is not an inherent human possession.

- **Thnetos** (G2349) — "mortal." 6 NT occurrences. Romans 6:12: "your mortal [*thnetos*] body." 1 Corinthians 15:53: "this mortal [*thnetos*] must put on immortality." The human body is explicitly called *mortal*. The transformation to immortality is future, not present.

**15. Read Genesis 3:22-24. If man already possessed an immortal soul, why would God need to bar him from the tree of life to prevent him from "living forever"?**

The action would be pointless. If man already had an immortal component that could not die, barring him from the tree of life to prevent him from "living forever" (*chay le-olam*) accomplishes nothing — he would already live forever regardless. God's action only makes sense if man is wholly mortal and the tree of life was the means by which he could have sustained life indefinitely.

## Section E: The Intermediate State — Competing Passages

**16. Read 2 Corinthians 5:1-8.**

Paul's desire is to be "clothed upon" (*ependysasthai*) — to receive the new body *over* the old — not to be "unclothed" (*ekdysasthai*) or disembodied (v.4). He wants "mortality swallowed up of life." This exact phrase appears in 1 Corinthians 15:54, where it describes the resurrection. Paul is not describing an intermediate disembodied state — he is describing his preference to be alive at the Second Coming and transformed without dying.

**17. Read Philippians 1:23.**

Paul desires "to depart and be with Christ." But in the same letter (Phil 3:11), his stated hope is "if by any means I might attain unto the resurrection of the dead." And in Phil 3:20-21, he awaits a Savior who "shall change our vile body." Paul's own writings consistently tie being with Christ to the resurrection. From the perspective of the sleeper, departing (death) and being with Christ (resurrection) are experienced as immediate — with no subjective awareness of the time between.

**18. Read Luke 23:43.**

Greek manuscripts had no punctuation. "I say to you today, you will be with me in paradise" is as grammatically valid as "I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise." John 20:17 states that on Sunday morning — two days after the crucifixion — Jesus said "I am not yet ascended to my Father." If Jesus was not in paradise on Friday, neither was the thief. The reading "I say to you today" is consistent with all other explicit statements; the other reading creates a contradiction with John 20:17.

**19. Read 1 Thessalonians 4:16-17.**

Paul explicitly describes the sequence: "The dead in Christ shall rise first: then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them... and so shall we ever be with the Lord." Being with the Lord happens *after* the resurrection and *at* the Second Coming — not at death. This is Paul's own didactic teaching on the subject.

## Section F: Anthropological Models

**20. Read 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Mark 12:30.**

1 Thessalonians 5:23 lists three terms (spirit, soul, body). Mark 12:30 lists four (heart, soul, mind, strength). Deuteronomy 6:5 lists three different ones (heart, soul, might). No passage prescribes a specific number of human components as a doctrinal teaching. These passages use overlapping terms to describe the whole person comprehensively. Reading any of them as a technical anatomy lesson requires importing a framework the text does not provide.

**21. Read Isaiah 26:9 and Luke 1:46-47.**

In both passages, "soul" and "spirit" are used in synonymous parallelism — a Hebrew poetic device where two lines say the same thing in different words. "My soul doth magnify the Lord, and my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour" (Luke 1:46-47). Mary is not describing two separate components of herself; she is expressing one reality in two parallel phrases.

## Evidence Classification Exercise — Answers

**1. "Man became a living soul" (Gen 2:7)**
- **Explicit (E).** Genesis 2:7 directly states this in the text.

**2. "The same term (*nephesh chayyah*) is used for both humans and animals, so the term does not distinguish humans from animals."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** The fact that both humans (Gen 2:7) and animals (Gen 1:20-21,24) receive the same designation is explicitly stated. The conclusion that a shared term cannot serve as a distinguishing marker follows unavoidably — no interpretation or framework is needed.

**3. "The human soul is inherently immortal and survives death as a conscious entity."**
- **Inference (I) — specifically I-D (Counter-Evidence External).** No verse states this. It requires:
  - *Adding* the concept "immortal soul" (no verse uses this phrase)
  - *Redefining* "only" in 1 Tim 6:16 to exclude a type of immortality
  - *Redefining* "die" in Ezek 18:4 to mean something other than death
  - *Redefining* "destroy" in Matt 10:28 to mean something other than destruction
  - *Applying* a Platonic philosophical framework not found in Scripture
  - This is the weakest type of inference because it requires overriding what the text explicitly states.

**4. "Man does not inherently possess immortality."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** God *only* has immortality (1 Tim 6:16 — explicit). Immortality must be *sought* (Rom 2:7 — explicit). It must be *put on* at resurrection (1 Cor 15:53 — explicit). Together, these leave no room for humans already possessing immortality. Every reader, regardless of theological position, must acknowledge what "only," "seek," and "must put on" mean.

**5. "Death involves the cessation of cognitive activity."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** Seven explicit statements from five authors state the dead lack thoughts (Ps 146:4), knowledge (Ecc 9:5), remembrance (Ps 6:5), work or wisdom (Ecc 9:10), perception (Job 14:21), and praise (Ps 115:17). The cumulative, unanimous testimony leaves no alternative reading of what these texts say. (Note: one could *infer* these are limited in scope — but the limitation itself would be an inference, since no verse states it.)

**6. "Paul's desire to 'depart and be with Christ' (Phil 1:23) proves a conscious intermediate state between death and resurrection."**
- **Inference (I) — specifically I-B (Competing Evidence).** Phil 1:23 expresses Paul's desire (explicit). But Paul's own teaching in 1 Thess 4:16-17 ties being with the Lord to the resurrection (explicit). The claim that Phil 1:23 proves a conscious intermediate state must be *chosen* over Paul's didactic teaching in 1 Thessalonians. It is an inference with competing textual evidence on both sides, and the clearer passage (didactic instruction) interprets the less clear (personal expression of desire).

**7. "The spirit (*ruach*) that returns to God at death (Ecc 12:7) carries the person's consciousness."**
- **Inference (I) — specifically I-C (Compatible External).** Ecclesiastes 12:7 says the *ruach* returns to God (explicit). The text does not say it carries consciousness, identity, or personality. Psalm 146:4 says thoughts perish when the *ruach* departs. The claim that the returning *ruach* is conscious *adds* a concept the text does not contain. It does not directly contradict Ecc 12:7 (it's compatible with it), but it requires importing information from outside the text.

## What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said — Answers

**What CAN be said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies):**

1. Man was formed from dust and God breathed the breath of life into him; man *became* a living soul (Gen 2:7).
2. The same term *nephesh chayyah* applies to both humans and animals; it does not distinguish them.
3. Animals and humans share the same *ruach*/breath (Ecc 3:19; Gen 7:22).
4. At death, dust returns to earth and *ruach* returns to God (Ecc 12:7). Thoughts perish that same day (Ps 146:4).
5. The dead know nothing, have no remembrance, no work, no knowledge, no wisdom (Ecc 9:5,10; Ps 6:5; 115:17).
6. The soul (*nephesh*) can die (Ezek 18:4) and be destroyed (Matt 10:28).
7. God alone has immortality (1 Tim 6:16). Humans must seek it (Rom 2:7) and put it on at resurrection (1 Cor 15:53).
8. Man was barred from the tree of life to prevent him from living forever (Gen 3:22-24).
9. Death is called "sleep" by seven or more biblical authors.
10. Being with the Lord is tied to the resurrection (1 Thess 4:16-17).

**What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied — requires adding something):**

1. That humans have an "immortal soul." The phrase never appears in Scripture. The claim requires overriding multiple explicit statements.
2. That the *ruach*/spirit carries consciousness after death. Psalm 146:4 says thoughts perish when it departs.
3. That a conscious intermediate state exists between death and resurrection. Paul ties being with the Lord to the resurrection, not to death.
4. That *nephesh* distinguishes humans from animals. The same term applies to both.
5. That Scripture prescribes a specific anthropological model (dichotomy or trichotomy). Terms are used flexibly, not technically.
6. That Ecclesiastes' death statements are limited to an "earthly perspective." No verse states this limitation, and the "under the sun" phrase appears in Ecc 9:6, not in 9:5, 9:10, Ps 146:4, or Ps 6:5.
7. That 2 Corinthians 5:8 describes a disembodied intermediate existence. Paul expressly says he does *not* desire to be "unclothed" (v.4).
