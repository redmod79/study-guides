# Study 17: God's Character, Justice, and the Fate of the Wicked — Advanced Study Answer Key

*Is Infinite Torment Consistent with Biblical Justice?*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** — The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** — No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed — every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** — Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: God's Self-Revelation Formula

**1. Read Exodus 34:6-7. Catalog the mercy-attributes and judgment-attributes separately. How many attributes fall on each side, and what is the proportion of mercy-language to judgment-language in God's own self-proclamation?**

Mercy-attributes: (1) merciful, (2) gracious, (3) longsuffering, (4) abundant in goodness and truth, (5) keeping mercy for thousands, (6) forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin. Judgment-attributes: (1) will by no means clear the guilty, (2) visiting iniquity unto the third and fourth generation. The proportion is approximately 6:2 — mercy dominates God's self-proclamation by a ratio of 3:1. This is not an inference about God's character; it is the observable structure of the text itself.

> "The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, Keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, and that will by no means clear the guilty; visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children, and upon the children's children, unto the third and to the fourth generation." — Exodus 34:6-7

**2. The Exodus 34:6-7 formula is echoed in at least five other OT passages: Psalm 86:15, Psalm 103:8, Psalm 145:8, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2. Do any of these echoes add judgment-language that Exodus 34 omits, or do they consistently emphasize the mercy-attributes?**

The echoes consistently emphasize the mercy-attributes while omitting or shortening the judgment-language:

- **Psalm 86:15** — "Full of compassion, and gracious, longsuffering, and plenteous in mercy and truth." No judgment-language.
- **Psalm 103:8** — "Merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and plenteous in mercy." No judgment-language.
- **Psalm 145:8** — "Gracious, and full of compassion; slow to anger, and of great mercy." No judgment-language.
- **Joel 2:13** — "Gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil." No judgment-language.
- **Jonah 4:2** — "Gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil." No judgment-language.

None adds judgment-language. All selectively repeat the mercy-attributes. This is not an accident of one author but a pattern across five authors in at least three centuries of composition. Scripture's own repetition tells us which attributes define God's character most fundamentally.

**3. Read Psalm 103:10,14. "He hath not dealt with us after our sins; nor rewarded us according to our iniquities... For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." How does God's awareness that humans are "dust" affect His administration of justice?**

God's awareness of human frailty — "he remembereth that we are dust" — results in restrained judgment: He has "not dealt with us after our sins." The text explicitly connects God's awareness of human limitation to His merciful treatment. God does not apply the full weight of what human sin deserves precisely because He knows what humans are: mortal, fragile, dust. This is relevant to the ECT question because infinite torment on finite, dust-derived beings would represent the maximum possible divergence between what the offender is and what the penalty demands.

> "He hath not dealt with us after our sins; nor rewarded us according to our iniquities... For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." — Psalm 103:10,14

**4. Read Deuteronomy 32:4. "All his ways are judgment: a God of truth and without iniquity, just and right is he." Does this verse define what "just and right" looks like, or does it simply declare God to be just?**

This verse declares God to be "just and right" without defining what that justice entails regarding final punishment. It is a statement about God's character (He is just), not a specification of His methods (what form justice takes). Both ECT and conditionalist positions agree that God is just. The question is what "just" looks like when applied to the fate of the wicked — and this verse does not answer that question. The content of God's justice must be determined from passages that describe His justice in action, such as the "according to deeds" passages and the historical judgment pattern.

## Section B: Biblical Justice Vocabulary

**5. The Greek word *dikaiokrasia* (G1341) appears in Romans 2:5 — "the righteous judgment of God." Based on this compound, what does Paul assert about the character of God's judgment?**

*Dikaiokrasia* combines *dikaios* ("righteous, just") and *krisis* ("judgment, decision, verdict"). Paul asserts that God's judgment is inherently righteous — it is not arbitrary, capricious, or disproportionate. The compound word itself embeds the concept of justice into the act of judging. Whatever God's judgment looks like, it must be recognizably righteous. The verse immediately following (Rom 2:6) defines what this righteous judgment entails: "Who will render to every man according to his deeds." The definition of divine "righteous judgment" is proportional rendering.

> "The day of wrath and revelation of the righteous judgment [*dikaiokrasia*] of God; Who will render to every man according to his deeds." — Romans 2:5-6

**6. The Greek word *misthapodosia* (G3405) appears in Hebrews 2:2 — "a just recompense of reward." What does this word's construction tell you about the nature of divine punishment?**

*Misthapodosia* is constructed from *misthos* ("wages, pay, reward") and *apodidomi* ("to give back, to pay back, to return"). The word's semantic structure is "a giving-back of wages" — a recompense that corresponds to the work done. This is transactional language: the penalty paid back corresponds to the offense committed. It is inherently proportional. If punishment is a "paying back" of what is owed, then infinite payment for finite debt exceeds what is owed and is therefore not a "just recompense."

> "For if the word spoken by angels was stedfast, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompence of reward [*misthapodosia*]..." — Hebrews 2:2

**7. Read Colossians 3:25. "He that doeth wrong shall receive for the wrong which he hath done: and there is no respect of persons." How does this verse bear on proportionality?**

The phrase "receive for the wrong which he hath done" establishes direct correspondence between the offense ("the wrong done") and the penalty ("receive for the wrong"). The penalty is measured by the wrong committed. The addition of "no respect of persons" reinforces that the standard is objective — based on deeds, not on status. This is proportional, deed-based justice explicitly stated.

> "But he that doeth wrong shall receive for the wrong which he hath done: and there is no respect of persons." — Colossians 3:25

**8. Compile a list of passages that state punishment is "according to deeds" or proportional to the offense. How many passages, authors, and testaments are represented?**

| # | Passage | Key Phrase | Author |

At least 11 passages across 9 authors (Paul, Elihu, David, Jeremiah, Jesus, John, the author of Hebrews, plus Proverbs 24:12 and others) in both testaments. This is one of the most broadly attested principles in Scripture. Proportional, deed-based justice is not a minor theme — it is the consistently stated measure of divine judgment.

## Section C: Proportionality Analysis

**9. Jesus teaches "many stripes" vs. "few stripes" and "more tolerable" for some than others. Does infinite conscious torment for all the unsaved accommodate these degrees, or does infinity absorb all differences?**

Infinity absorbs all differences. If every unsaved person experiences conscious torment without end, the distinction between "many stripes" and "few stripes" becomes meaningless in practice. After the first trillion years, both the servant who knew and the servant who did not know are still being tormented. After the first trillion trillion years, they are still being tormented. The mathematical reality of infinity is that no finite difference in initial intensity can be meaningfully distinguished against an infinite duration. The "more tolerable" distinction requires finite punishment — only if punishment ends can one person's total punishment be measurably less than another's.

**10. The stated penalty for sin is "death." No passage states that the penalty is eternal conscious torment. What must be added to arrive at ECT?**

The stated penalty is consistently "death" (*thanatos*/*mut*):
- **Romans 6:23**: "The wages of sin is death"
- **Ezekiel 18:4,20**: "The soul that sinneth, it shall die"
- **Genesis 2:17**: "In the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die"

To arrive at eternal conscious torment, one must add: (1) the redefinition of "death" to mean something other than death — i.e., continued conscious existence in suffering; (2) the claim that the biblical word "death" does not mean what it normally means; (3) the assumption that "die" in these passages is metaphorical for "live forever in torment." None of these additions is stated in any biblical text.

**11. Read Genesis 4:11-15. Cain's punishment for the first murder was exile and wandering — not death. God specifically set a mark on Cain to protect him from being killed. What does this tell you about proportionality?**

The first recorded divine judgment on a human being for murder was exile and divine protection from retaliatory death — not infinite punishment, not even capital punishment. God's earliest recorded administration of justice was demonstrably proportional and restrained. This does not prove what the final judgment will look like, but it establishes a pattern: God's justice, when observed in action, is measured and proportional rather than maximal.

> "And the LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him." — Genesis 4:15

**12. Read Nahum 1:8-9. God declares of Nineveh: "He will make an utter end." The word *kalah* means complete annihilation/consumption. Does this language describe ongoing torment or completed destruction?**

*Kalah* describes completed consumption — "an utter end." The word denotes something brought to its conclusion, used up, finished. This is termination language, not preservation-in-torment language. Nahum adds the rhetorical question: "What do ye imagine against the LORD? he will make an utter end: affliction shall not rise up the second time." The affliction does not continue indefinitely; it is so thorough that a second instance is unnecessary.

> "He will make an utter end: affliction shall not rise up the second time." — Nahum 1:9

## Section D: The Anselmian Argument

**13. Does any verse in Scripture state that the gravity of sin is measured by the dignity or nature of the offended party?**

No. No verse in Scripture states this principle. Every passage that addresses the measure of punishment uses the offender's deeds as the standard: "according to his deeds" (Rom 2:6), "according to his ways" (Job 34:11), "according to the fruit of his doings" (Jer 17:10), "receive for the wrong which he hath done" (Col 3:25). The measure is always the offender's actions, never the offended party's nature. The Anselmian principle — that offense-gravity is determined by the rank of the one offended — must be imported from outside the text. It is an I-C inference requiring criterion #3 (external framework).

**14. The Anselmian framework derives from medieval feudal jurisprudence. Compare these two frameworks: Which one is found in Scripture?**

Biblical proportional justice: punishment measured by the harm done, the deeds committed, the knowledge possessed ("many stripes" for the knowledgeable, "few stripes" for the ignorant — Luke 12:47-48). This is explicitly stated in 11+ passages.

Anselmian-feudal justice: punishment measured by the rank of the offended party. An offense against a duke is punished more severely than the same offense against a peasant. Transferred to theology: because God is infinite in rank/dignity, any offense against Him is infinite in gravity, requiring infinite punishment.

The first framework is in the text. The second is not. The Anselmian framework was articulated in *Cur Deus Homo* (c. 1098 AD) and entered mainstream theology through Aquinas (*Summa Theologica*, 13th century) and Edwards (*The Justice of God in the Damnation of Sinners*, 18th century). Its absence from Scripture for 1,098 years after the New Testament's completion is itself significant.

**15. Read John 19:11. "He that delivered me unto thee hath the greater sin." What does Jesus' statement do to the claim that all sin is equally infinite because God is infinite?**

If all sin against God is equally infinite (the Anselmian premise), then no sin can be "greater" than another — infinity equals infinity. But Jesus explicitly says one sin is "greater" than another. This is a direct statement from Jesus that sin has degrees, which is incompatible with the premise that all sin is equally infinite in magnitude. The Anselmian framework requires that sin be undifferentiated in gravity (all infinite); Jesus states that sin is differentiated ("greater sin"). The text contradicts the framework.

> "Jesus answered, Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above: therefore he that delivered me unto thee hath the greater sin." — John 19:11

**16. Read Isaiah 57:16. "I will not contend for ever, neither will I be always wroth: for the spirit should fail before me, and the souls which I have made." What reason does God give for not contending forever?**

God states He will not contend forever because "the spirit should fail before me, and the souls which I have made." God limits His contention out of regard for the frailty of the beings He created. This is remarkable: God restrains His judgment because the objects of His judgment cannot endure it indefinitely. The text portrays God as moderating His anger precisely because infinite contention would obliterate the finite beings He made — which is the opposite of sustaining them in torment forever. This passage directly contradicts the Anselmian premise that God's infinite nature requires infinite punishment; here, God's awareness of human finitude is the reason He limits punishment.

> "For I will not contend for ever, neither will I be always wroth: for the spirit should fail before me, and the souls which I have made." — Isaiah 57:16

## Section E: God's Stated Desires vs. ECT

**17. Evaluate whether ECT or destruction (cessation) is more consistent with five passages about God's desires.**

The five passages:
- **Ezekiel 18:23**: God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked
- **Ezekiel 33:11**: God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked (with oath)
- **2 Peter 3:9**: God is not willing that any should perish
- **1 Timothy 2:4**: God will have all men to be saved
- **Lamentations 3:33**: God does not afflict from His heart

Under ECT: God perpetually sustains the conscious torment of beings He stated He does not want to see perish, does not take pleasure in seeing die, wills to be saved, and does not afflict from His heart. He actively maintains — by His own power, forever — the very condition He declared contrary to His desires. This creates an irreconcilable tension between God's stated desires and His alleged actions.

Under conditionalism (destruction): The wicked perish — God does not desire this, takes no pleasure in it, but permits it as the just consequence of sin. The affliction reaches its conclusion (death). God's stated desires and His actions are consistent: He does not desire their death, and He does not sustain their suffering beyond death. The penalty is severe (permanent loss of existence) but finite, and the affliction ends.

**18. Read Psalm 145:17,20. If God is "righteous in all his ways" and His way with the wicked is to destroy them, what does this imply?**

The text places two statements in direct conjunction: (1) God is "righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works" and (2) "all the wicked will he destroy [*shamad*]." The text itself identifies destruction as the righteous, holy act of God toward the wicked. If destruction is what God does, and everything God does is righteous and holy, then destruction IS the righteous, holy outcome. The text does not say God preserves the wicked in suffering; it says He destroys them. The contrast in verse 20 is between preservation (for those who love Him) and destruction (for the wicked) — two different fates, not two modes of eternal existence.

> "The LORD is righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works... The LORD preserveth all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy." — Psalm 145:17,20

## Section F: Counter-Arguments

**19. ECT counter-argument: "Psalm 103:9 and Micah 7:18 apply only to God's covenant people, not to the eschatological fate of the wicked."**

Psalm 103:9 and Micah 7:18 are addressed in contexts dealing with God's people. An ECT reader could argue these are contextually limited. However, these texts describe God's character — "he delighteth in mercy" (Mic 7:18) — not a situational policy. The Exodus 34:6-7 formula on which they are based is a proclamation of who God IS, not a promise limited to one audience. No verse states that God's character changes depending on the subject. To argue that God retains not anger forever toward His people but does retain anger forever toward the wicked requires the claim that a character attribute operates differently across audiences — a restriction the text does not make. This is an inference (I-C): it adds a scope-limitation not present in the text.

**20. ECT counter-argument: "God's justice requires infinite punishment because God's holiness is absolute and cannot tolerate any sin."**

Habakkuk 1:13 ("Thou art of purer eyes than to behold evil") addresses God's incompatibility with sin, not the duration of punishment. God's holiness explains why sin must be dealt with — it does not specify how. Holiness is equally satisfied by destruction (the permanent removal of sin and the sinner) as by eternal torment (the permanent preservation of sin and the sinner in suffering). In fact, destruction more completely removes sin from God's creation than ECT, which requires the permanent existence of sinful beings. The holiness argument does not favor ECT over conditionalism.

**21. ECT counter-argument: "If destruction is the penalty, the wicked 'get off easy.'"**

This objection assumes that ceasing to exist is trivial. Consider what is lost: existence itself, consciousness, all relationships, all future, all possibility. Romans 6:23 contrasts death with "eternal life" — the greatest gift God offers. If eternal life is the supreme good, then the permanent loss of life is the supreme deprivation. The wicked lose everything — permanently, irreversibly. This is not "getting off easy"; it is the total forfeiture of all that makes existence valuable.

Additionally, the objection reveals a problematic assumption: that justice is about maximizing suffering rather than rendering proportional recompense. The biblical standard is "according to deeds" and "just recompense" — not "maximum possible suffering." The text never defines justice as the infliction of the worst conceivable penalty.

**22. Conditionalist counter-argument: "Every historical divine judgment results in death/destruction, never ongoing torment." Can an ECT defender identify a counterexample?**

No recorded divine judgment in Scripture — outside of apocalyptic symbolism — results in ongoing conscious torment. The flood: death (Gen 7:21-23). Sodom: overthrow/destruction (Gen 19:24-25). Korah: perished/consumed (Num 16:31-35). Nadab and Abihu: devoured/died (Lev 10:1-2). Sennacherib's army: all dead corpses (2 Ki 19:35). Uzzah: died (2 Sam 6:7). Ananias and Sapphira: fell down and died (Acts 5:5,10). In every case the result is death. No ECT defender has identified a historical (non-symbolic) divine judgment in Scripture resulting in ongoing conscious torment.

## Section G: Evidence Classification Exercise — Answers

**1. "God proclaims His character as merciful, gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth (Exo 34:6-7)."**
- **Explicit (E).** This is a direct quotation of God's self-proclamation. The text says exactly this.

**2. "God retaineth not his anger for ever (Mic 7:18) and will not always chide (Ps 103:9)."**
- **Explicit (E).** Both are direct quotations from the text. Both use "for ever" (*olam*/*la'ad*) with negation to state that God's anger is not permanent.

**3. "Infinite conscious torment for finite sin is inconsistent with the biblical principle of proportional justice."**
- **Inference — I-A (Evidence-Extending).** All components are in the explicit evidence: punishment is "according to deeds" (E), human deeds are finite (observable), and "according to" finite deeds produces finite punishment (logical extension). This inference systematizes multiple E-level items into a collective claim. Only criterion #5 (systematizing textual evidence) is required. No external framework is added.

**4. "Sin against an infinite God warrants infinite punishment."**
- **Inference — I-C (Compatible External).** No biblical text states that the gravity of sin is measured by the nature of the offended party. This reasoning derives from Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1098 AD), rooted in feudal jurisprudence. The Bible consistently measures punishment "according to deeds" (the offender's actions), never "according to the nature of the one offended." Criterion #3 (external framework) is required. Jesus' statement that one sin can be "greater" than another (John 19:11) directly contradicts the premise that all sin is equally infinite.

**5. "Biblical punishment is proportional — 'according to deeds' (Rom 2:6), 'many stripes' vs. 'few stripes' (Luke 12:47-48), 'more tolerable' (Matt 10:15), 'just recompense' (Heb 2:2)."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** Each individual passage is Explicit. The collective observation — that 11 passages across 9 authors in both testaments all state proportional, deed-based punishment — is a necessary implication that no reader can deny. The texts themselves state proportionality; the N-tier claim merely observes the cumulative pattern.

**6. "God's statements about not retaining anger 'for ever' apply only to His covenant people, not to the final fate of the wicked."**
- **Inference — I-C (Compatible External).** The texts (Ps 103:9; Mic 7:18) are addressed in the context of God's people. But they describe God's character ("he delighteth in mercy"), not a situational policy. The scope-restriction is not in the text — it must be added. This requires criterion #3: an external contextual limitation the text does not state.

**7. "Destruction (cessation) as the final fate of the wicked is consistent with God's self-revealed character of mercy, the temporary nature of His anger, and the stated penalty of death."**
- **Inference — I-A (Evidence-Extending).** All components are Explicit or Necessary Implication: God is merciful (E1/Exo 34:6-7), His anger is temporary (E3/Ps 103:9; E6/Mic 7:18), the penalty for sin is death (E22/Rom 6:23; E23/Ezek 18:4). This claim systematizes those E-level items into a coherent conclusion. Only criterion #5. No external framework is added.

## Section H: What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said — Answers

**What CAN be said (the Bible explicitly states or necessarily implies):**

1. God's self-revealed character (Exo 34:6-7) places mercy-language (6-7 attributes) before and above judgment-language (2 attributes). This formula is repeated in at least 5 other OT passages, consistently emphasizing mercy and omitting or shortening the judgment-language.

2. God "retaineth not his anger for ever" (Mic 7:18) and "will not always chide: neither will he keep his anger for ever" (Ps 103:9). These are explicit, didactic statements about God's character — not situational promises. Isaiah 57:16 adds: "I will not contend for ever, neither will I be always wroth."

3. God has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezek 18:23,32; 33:11) and is "not willing that any should perish" (2 Pet 3:9). God does not afflict "from his heart" (Lam 3:33). God "will have all men to be saved" (1 Tim 2:4).

4. Biblical punishment is explicitly proportional: "according to deeds" (Rom 2:6), "many stripes" vs. "few stripes" (Luke 12:47-48), "more tolerable" (Matt 10:15; 11:22), "just recompense" (Heb 2:2), "sorer punishment" (Heb 10:29), "receive for the wrong done" (Col 3:25). At least 11 passages across 9 authors in both testaments state this principle.

5. Every recorded divine judgment in Scripture results in death/destruction, never ongoing torment: the flood (Gen 7:21-23), Sodom (Gen 19:24-25), Korah (Num 16:31-35), Nadab/Abihu (Lev 10:1-2), Sennacherib's army (2 Ki 19:35), Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5:5,10).

6. The stated penalty for sin is death (Rom 6:23; Ezek 18:4,20; Gen 2:17), using the same words (*thanatos*/*mut*) consistently.

7. The "sin against infinite God requires infinite punishment" argument is not found in any biblical text. It is a philosophical framework originating with Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1098 AD). No verse measures punishment by the dignity of the offended party. Every verse that addresses the measure of punishment uses "according to deeds" (the offender's actions). Jesus' statement that one sin is "greater" than another (John 19:11) contradicts the Anselmian premise that all sin is equally infinite.

8. Destruction (cessation) is consistent with all E-level and N-level findings: proportional justice, temporary divine anger, the stated penalty of death, God's self-revealed character of mercy, God's stated reluctance regarding the death of the wicked, and the observed pattern of every historical divine judgment.

**What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied — requires adding something):**

1. **It cannot be said that infinite torment is taught by any explicit statement in Scripture.** No verse says "the wicked will be tormented forever" with human subjects in didactic genre. This has been confirmed across studies etc6-12, etc6-14, and etc6-15.

2. **It cannot be said that infinite torment is consistent with the "according to deeds" principle.** Infinite punishment for finite deeds violates the explicitly stated proportionality principle. If punishment is "according to deeds" and human deeds are finite, proportional punishment is finite.

3. **It cannot be said that God retains anger forever.** Two explicit passages state the opposite (Ps 103:9; Mic 7:18). Isaiah 57:16 adds a third. Psalm 30:5 states His anger "endureth but a moment."

4. **It cannot be said that God takes pleasure in the death of the wicked.** Three explicit passages state the opposite (Ezek 18:23, 18:32, 33:11), and God confirms it with an oath in Ezekiel 33:11.

5. **It cannot be said that any recorded divine judgment in Scripture resulted in ongoing torment.** All historical (non-symbolic) divine judgments result in death/destruction.

6. **It cannot be said that the "infinite God / infinite punishment" argument is biblical.** It is a philosophical inference (I-C) not derived from any biblical text, requiring an external framework (Anselmian feudal jurisprudence) absent from Scripture for 1,098 years after the New Testament's completion. It is contradicted by Jesus' statement that sin has degrees (John 19:11) and by every "according to deeds" passage.
