# Study 17: God's Character, Justice, and the Fate of the Wicked — Advanced Study

*Is Infinite Torment Consistent with Biblical Justice?*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** — The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** — No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed — every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** — Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: God's Self-Revelation Formula

1. Read **Exodus 34:6-7**. This is the foundational divine self-description in Scripture. Catalog the mercy-attributes and judgment-attributes separately. How many attributes fall on each side, and what is the proportion of mercy-language to judgment-language in God's own self-proclamation?

2. The Exodus 34:6-7 formula is echoed in at least five other OT passages: **Psalm 86:15**, **Psalm 103:8**, **Psalm 145:8**, **Joel 2:13**, **Jonah 4:2**. Read each. Do any of these echoes add judgment-language that Exodus 34 omits, or do they consistently emphasize the mercy-attributes? What does this pattern of selective repetition tell you about which aspects of God's character Scripture prioritizes?

3. Read **Psalm 103:10,14**. "He hath not dealt with us after our sins; nor rewarded us according to our iniquities... For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." How does God's awareness that humans are "dust" affect His administration of justice?

4. Read **Deuteronomy 32:4**. "All his ways are judgment: a God of truth and without iniquity, just and right is he." This text declares God's justice. Does this verse define what "just and right" looks like, or does it simply declare God to be just? What is the difference between asserting that God is just and defining what His justice entails?

## Section B: Biblical Justice Vocabulary

5. The Greek word *dikaiokrasia* (G1341) appears in **Romans 2:5** — "the righteous judgment of God." The word combines *dikaios* ("righteous/just") and *krisis* ("judgment/decision"). Based on this compound, what does Paul assert about the character of God's judgment?

6. The Greek word *misthapodosia* (G3405) appears in **Hebrews 2:2** — "a just recompense of reward." It is built from *misthos* ("wages/pay") and *apodidomi* ("to give back/pay back"). What does this word's construction tell you about the nature of divine punishment — is it arbitrary or proportional?

7. Read **Colossians 3:25**. "He that doeth wrong shall receive for the wrong which he hath done: and there is no respect of persons." The phrase "receive for the wrong" implies correspondence between offense and penalty. How does this verse bear on the question of proportionality?

8. Compile a list of passages that state punishment is "according to deeds" or proportional to the offense: **Romans 2:6**, **Job 34:11**, **Psalm 62:12**, **Jeremiah 17:10**, **Matthew 16:27**, **Revelation 20:12-13**, **Colossians 3:25**, **Luke 12:47-48**, **Matthew 10:15**, **Hebrews 2:2**, **Hebrews 10:29**. How many passages, authors, and testaments are represented? What is the strength of this evidence base?

## Section C: Proportionality Analysis

9. Jesus teaches "many stripes" versus "few stripes" (**Luke 12:47-48**) and "more tolerable" for some than others at the judgment (**Matt 10:15; 11:22,24**). **Hebrews 10:29** asks "of how much sorer punishment" one deserves for trampling the Son of God. These texts explicitly establish degrees of punishment. Does infinite conscious torment for all the unsaved accommodate these degrees, or does infinity absorb all differences?

10. The stated penalty for sin is "death" (**Romans 6:23**; **Ezekiel 18:4,20**; **Genesis 2:17**). The same Greek word *thanatos* and Hebrew word *mut* are used consistently across these passages. No passage states that the penalty for sin is eternal conscious torment. Evaluate: If the wages of sin is death, and death is the consistently stated penalty, what must be added to arrive at the conclusion that the penalty is actually eternal conscious torment?

11. Read **Genesis 4:11-15**. Cain's punishment for the first murder was exile and wandering — not death. God specifically set a mark on Cain to protect him from being killed. What does this tell you about proportionality in God's earliest recorded judgment on a human being?

12. Read **Nahum 1:8-9**. God declares of Nineveh: "He will make an utter end" (*kalah*). The word *kalah* means complete annihilation/consumption. Does this judgment language describe ongoing torment or completed destruction?

## Section D: The Anselmian Argument

13. The traditional ECT argument holds that because God is infinite, any sin against Him is an infinite offense requiring infinite punishment. This reasoning is attributed to Anselm of Canterbury (*Cur Deus Homo*, c. 1098 AD) and developed by Thomas Aquinas and Jonathan Edwards. Identify: Does any verse in Scripture state that the gravity of sin is measured by the dignity or nature of the offended party? Or does every passage that addresses the measure of punishment use "according to deeds" (the offender's actions)?

14. The Anselmian framework derives from medieval feudal jurisprudence, where an offense against a king was punished more severely than the same offense against a commoner — the offense's gravity was measured by the rank of the offended party. Biblical proportional justice measures the offense by the harm done ("according to deeds"). Compare these two frameworks: Which one is found in Scripture, and which must be imported from outside the text?

15. Read **John 19:11**. Jesus says to Pilate: "He that delivered me unto thee hath the greater sin." If all sin against God is equally infinite (the Anselmian premise), can one sin be "greater" than another? What does Jesus' statement do to the claim that all sin is equally infinite because God is infinite?

16. Read **Isaiah 57:16**. "I will not contend for ever, neither will I be always wroth: for the spirit should fail before me, and the souls which I have made." What reason does God give for not contending forever? How does this bear on the Anselmian argument?

## Section E: God's Stated Desires vs. ECT

17. Three passages state God's desire regarding the wicked: **Ezekiel 18:23** ("Have I any pleasure at all that the wicked should die?"), **Ezekiel 33:11** ("I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked"), and **2 Peter 3:9** ("not willing that any should perish"). **1 Timothy 2:4** adds that God "will have all men to be saved." **Lamentations 3:33** says God does not afflict "from his heart." If ECT is true, God would be perpetually sustaining the torment of beings He expressly stated He does not desire to see perish. Evaluate whether ECT or destruction (cessation) is more consistent with these five statements.

18. Read **Psalm 145:17,20**. Verse 17 declares God "righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works." Verse 20 states: "The LORD preserveth all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy." The verb is *shamad* — annihilate, exterminate. If God is "righteous in all his ways" and His way with the wicked is to destroy (*shamad*) them, what does this imply about whether destruction is consistent with God's righteousness?

## Section F: Counter-Arguments

19. **ECT counter-argument:** "Psalm 103:9 and Micah 7:18 ('retaineth not his anger for ever') apply only to God's covenant people, not to the eschatological fate of the wicked." Evaluate this claim. Do these texts describe a situational policy limited to Israel, or a characteristic of God's nature? Does the text itself place any scope-restriction on these statements?

20. **ECT counter-argument:** "God's justice requires that sin be punished infinitely because God's holiness is absolute and cannot tolerate any sin." Read **Habakkuk 1:13** ("Thou art of purer eyes than to behold evil"). Does this verse address the duration of punishment, or the incompatibility of God and sin? Does it state anything about infinite punishment?

21. **ECT counter-argument:** "If destruction is the penalty, then the wicked 'get off easy' — they simply cease to exist, which is not a real punishment." Evaluate: Is the loss of existence — the permanent, irreversible forfeiture of life, consciousness, relationships, and all future — trivial? What does Romans 6:23 contrast death with? ("The gift of God is eternal life.") If eternal life is the greatest possible gift, what does the loss of it represent?

22. **Conditionalist counter-argument:** "Every historical divine judgment in Scripture results in death/destruction, never ongoing torment. This establishes the consistent pattern of how God judges." Can an ECT defender identify any recorded divine judgment in Scripture (not apocalyptic symbolism) where the result is ongoing conscious torment rather than death or destruction?
