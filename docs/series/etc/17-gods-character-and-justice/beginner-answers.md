# Study 17: God's Character, Justice, and the Fate of the Wicked — Answer Key

*Is Infinite Torment Consistent with Biblical Justice?*

---

> **A Note on How to Study**
>
> Throughout this series, we distinguish between what the Bible **explicitly says** and what people **infer** from the Bible. These are not the same thing.
>
> - **Explicit:** You can point to a verse and quote it. The text directly says this.
> - **Inference:** Someone claims the Bible teaches something, but no single verse says it. They have drawn a conclusion — possibly a good one, possibly not — but the conclusion itself is not in the text.
>
> This distinction is especially important in this study. Many people assume they know what God's character requires regarding the fate of the wicked. But what does God Himself say about His own character — and what does that character tell us about the nature of His judgments?

---

## Section A: God's Self-Revealed Character

**1. Read Exodus 34:6-7. God proclaims His own name and character: "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, and that will by no means clear the guilty." How many attributes describe mercy in this formula, and how many describe judgment?**

Six attributes describe mercy (merciful, gracious, longsuffering, abundant in goodness, abundant in truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity/transgression/sin), while only two describe judgment (will not clear the guilty, visiting iniquity to the third and fourth generation). God's own self-description proportions mercy-language to judgment-language roughly 6:2.

> "The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, Keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, and that will by no means clear the guilty; visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children... unto the third and to the fourth generation." — Exodus 34:6-7

**2. Read Exodus 34:7 again. God's mercy extends to "thousands" while His judgment of iniquity extends "unto the third and to the fourth generation." What is the ratio of mercy to judgment in God's own self-description?**

Mercy extends to "thousands" of generations; judgment extends to "the third and fourth generation." The ratio is thousands to three or four — an overwhelming preponderance of mercy over judgment. This is not a theological inference; it is what the text itself states in God's own words.

**3. This self-description formula is repeated throughout the Old Testament: Psalm 86:15, Psalm 103:8, Psalm 145:8, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2. What does the fact that Scripture repeats this formula at least five additional times tell you about its importance?**

Scripture's repetition of a formula across multiple authors, genres, and centuries signals that this is not a passing comment but a foundational declaration of God's character. At least six passages echo Exodus 34:6-7, making it the most repeated character description of God in the Old Testament. This is how God wants to be known.

**4. Read Lamentations 3:33. "He doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men." The Hebrew word translated "willingly" is *milibo* — literally "from his heart." According to this text, does God's heart desire to afflict people?**

No. Affliction does not come "from his heart." The text states that when God afflicts, it is not because He desires it. This is consistent with the Ezekiel passages where God explicitly states He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked.

> "For he doth not afflict willingly [*milibo*, 'from his heart'] nor grieve the children of men." — Lamentations 3:33

## Section B: God's Anger — Temporary or Permanent?

**5. Read Psalm 103:9. "He will not always chide: neither will he keep his anger for ever." According to this text, is God's anger permanent or temporary?**

Temporary. The text explicitly says God will NOT keep His anger "for ever." Both clauses reinforce the same point: God's chiding has an endpoint, and His anger is not permanent. This is a direct, didactic statement about God's character.

> "He will not always chide: neither will he keep his anger for ever." — Psalm 103:9

**6. Read Micah 7:18. "He retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy." What reason does the text give for why God does not retain anger forever?**

Because He "delighteth in mercy." This is not merely a statement about what God does — it is a statement about what God enjoys, what He takes pleasure in. God delights in mercy, not in anger. His anger has a terminus; His mercy does not.

> "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity, and passeth by the transgression of the remnant of his heritage? he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy." — Micah 7:18

**7. Read Psalm 30:5. "His anger endureth but a moment; in his favour is life: weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning." How does this verse compare the duration of anger to the duration of favor?**

Anger is "a moment"; favor is "life." The contrast is stark: God's anger is brief and temporary, while His favor is enduring. The imagery of weeping for "a night" versus joy "in the morning" reinforces that anger is the exception, not the rule, in God's dealings.

> "For his anger endureth but a moment; in his favour is life: weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning." — Psalm 30:5

**8. If God "retaineth not his anger for ever" (Mic 7:18) and "will not always chide" (Ps 103:9), what would it mean for God to sustain conscious torment for eternity? Would that be consistent with these statements?**

Eternal conscious torment would require God to sustain anger and punishment without end — precisely what these texts say He does NOT do. If God "retaineth not his anger for ever," then a punishment sustained by divine power for infinite duration contradicts what the text explicitly states about God's character.

## Section C: God's Attitude Toward the Death of the Wicked

**9. Read Ezekiel 18:23. "Have I any pleasure at all that the wicked should die? saith the Lord GOD: and not that he should return from his ways, and live?" What answer does this rhetorical question expect?**

The expected answer is "No." God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. The rhetorical question, coming from God Himself, reveals His preference: He desires that the wicked turn and live, not that they die.

> "Have I any pleasure at all that the wicked should die? saith the Lord GOD: and not that he should return from his ways, and live?" — Ezekiel 18:23

**10. Read Ezekiel 33:11. "As I live, saith the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live." God swears by His own life. What does He say He desires for the wicked?**

God desires their repentance and life, not their death. This is stated three times in Ezekiel (18:23, 18:32, 33:11), and in chapter 33 God swears it with an oath — "As I live" — the strongest form of divine assurance. The repetition and the oath underscore how important this declaration is.

> "As I live, saith the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live: turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel?" — Ezekiel 33:11

**11. Read 2 Peter 3:9. "Not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance." Read also 1 Timothy 2:4. "Who will have all men to be saved." According to these texts, what is God's stated desire for all people?**

God's desire is that ALL people be saved and that NONE perish. These are universal statements — "any," "all men" — not limited to a select group. God's will is for salvation, not destruction.

> "The Lord is not slack concerning his promise... but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance." — 2 Peter 3:9

> "Who will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth." — 1 Timothy 2:4

**12. If God has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked" and is "not willing that any should perish," what would eternal conscious torment — sustained by God's power, without end — say about God's character?**

Eternal conscious torment would require God to perpetually sustain the suffering of beings whose death He takes no pleasure in, whose perishing He does not will. He would be actively maintaining — forever — the very condition He declares He does not desire. Destruction (cessation) aligns with these statements: the wicked perish (God does not desire this but permits it), and the affliction reaches its conclusion.

## Section D: Proportional Justice

**13. Read Romans 2:5-6. God "will render to every man according to his deeds." Read also Job 34:11. "The work of a man shall he render unto him, and cause every man to find according to his ways." What standard does God use to determine punishment — the nature of the offended party, or the deeds of the offender?**

According to both texts, the standard is the offender's deeds — "according to his deeds," "according to his ways." The measure of punishment is the actions of the person, not the nature of the one offended. This principle appears in at least 11 passages across 9 authors in both testaments (Rom 2:6; Job 34:11; Ps 62:12; Jer 17:10; Matt 16:27; Rev 20:12-13; Col 3:25; and others).

> "The righteous judgment of God; Who will render to every man according to his deeds." — Romans 2:5-6

> "The work of a man shall he render unto him, and cause every man to find according to his ways. Yea, surely God will not do wickedly, neither will the Almighty pervert judgment." — Job 34:11-12

**14. Read Luke 12:47-48. The servant who knew his lord's will receives "many stripes"; the servant who did not know receives "few stripes." What does this parable teach about whether punishment is uniform or proportional?**

Punishment is proportional, not uniform. Jesus explicitly teaches degrees of judgment based on knowledge and responsibility. "Many stripes" versus "few stripes" means different offenders receive different levels of punishment. If all punishment were infinite and identical (eternal conscious torment for all the lost), the distinction between "many" and "few" would be meaningless.

> "And that servant, which knew his lord's will, and prepared not himself, neither did according to his will, shall be beaten with many stripes. But he that knew not, and did commit things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with few stripes." — Luke 12:47-48

**15. Read Matthew 10:15. "It shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city." If judgment is "more tolerable" for some than others, what does that require about the nature of punishment?**

It requires that punishment varies in degree. If punishment is "more tolerable" for Sodom than for the cities that rejected the apostles, then judgment is graduated — some experience more, others less. This is incompatible with a single uniform fate of infinite conscious torment for all the unsaved.

> "Verily I say unto you, It shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city." — Matthew 10:15

**16. Read Hebrews 2:2. "Every transgression and disobedience received a just recompence of reward." The Greek word *misthapodosia* means "a paying back." If punishment is a "just recompense" — a proportional paying back — can finite sins produce an infinite penalty?**

A "just recompense" — a proportional paying back — requires correspondence between the offense and the penalty. Human deeds are finite (committed by finite beings in finite time). A just recompense for finite deeds is a finite punishment. An infinite penalty for finite offenses is not a "paying back" proportional to what was done; it exceeds the offense by an infinite degree.

> "Every transgression and disobedience received a just recompence of reward." — Hebrews 2:2

## Section E: The Pattern of Historical Judgments

**17. In all of these divine judgments, what is the result — death/destruction or ongoing torment?**

Every recorded divine judgment in Scripture results in death and destruction, never ongoing torment:

- **The flood** (Gen 7:21-23): "All flesh died... every man... all... were destroyed." The result is death.
- **Sodom** (Gen 19:24-25): "The LORD overthrew those cities." The result is destruction.
- **Korah** (Num 16:31-35): "They perished... the fire consumed them." The result is death and consumption.
- **Nadab and Abihu** (Lev 10:1-2): "There went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them, and they died." The result is death.

In every case, the divine judgment ends in death or destruction. None results in ongoing conscious torment. This is the consistent pattern across the entire Old Testament.

**18. Read Psalm 145:17,20. "The LORD is righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works... The LORD preserveth all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy." The Hebrew word for "destroy" is *shamad* — meaning to annihilate, exterminate. According to this text, what does God do with the wicked?**

God destroys (*shamad*) them. The text does not say God preserves the wicked in torment; it says He preserves those who love Him and destroys the wicked. The contrast is between preservation (for the righteous) and destruction (for the wicked) — not between two kinds of preservation (blissful vs. tormenting).

> "The LORD is righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works... The LORD preserveth all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy." — Psalm 145:17,20

## Explicit or Inference? — Answers

**A. "God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezek 18:23; 33:11).**
- **Explicit.** God says this directly, three times (Ezek 18:23, 18:32, 33:11). In chapter 33 He swears it with an oath. This is a direct divine statement.

**B. "Sin against an infinite God deserves infinite punishment."**
- **Inference.** No verse in the Bible states this. It is a philosophical argument originating with Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1098 AD), rooted in feudal concepts of honor (the gravity of an offense measured by the rank of the offended party). Every biblical passage that addresses the measure of punishment uses "according to deeds" (the offender's actions), not "according to the nature of the one offended."

**C. "God will render to every man according to his deeds" (Rom 2:6).**
- **Explicit.** This is a direct quotation from Romans 2:6, and the same principle is stated in at least 10 other passages across both testaments (Job 34:11; Ps 62:12; Jer 17:10; Matt 16:27; Col 3:25; Rev 20:12-13; and others).

**D. "God retaineth not his anger for ever" (Mic 7:18).**
- **Explicit.** This is a direct quotation from Micah 7:18, and the same principle is stated in Psalm 103:9 ("neither will he keep his anger for ever"), Psalm 30:5 ("his anger endureth but a moment"), and Isaiah 57:16 ("I will not contend for ever").

**E. "Eternal conscious torment is the just penalty for sin."**
- **Inference.** No verse in the Bible states this. The stated penalty for sin is "death" (Rom 6:23; Ezek 18:4,20; Gen 2:17). The claim that the just penalty is eternal conscious torment requires substituting "torment" for "death" — a substitution no text makes.

## What the Bible Says vs. What People Say the Bible Teaches

| The Bible explicitly says... | People often claim the Bible teaches... |
|---|---|
| God's mercy extends to "thousands"; judgment to "the third and fourth generation" (Exo 34:7) | God's justice demands infinite punishment for any sin |
| God "retaineth not his anger for ever" (Mic 7:18; Ps 103:9) | God's anger against the wicked is sustained eternally |
| God has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezek 18:23; 33:11) | God's holiness requires the perpetual torment of the lost |
| Punishment is "according to deeds" — many stripes vs. few stripes (Rom 2:6; Luke 12:47-48) | All the unsaved receive the same infinite punishment |
| "The wages of sin is death" (Rom 6:23) | The wages of sin is eternal conscious torment |
| Every divine judgment results in death/destruction (Gen 7:21; 19:24; Num 16:35) | Divine judgment results in ongoing torment |
| "All the wicked will he destroy" (Ps 145:20) | God preserves the wicked alive in perpetual suffering |
