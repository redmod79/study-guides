# Study 12: Tormented Forever -- Advanced Study Answer Key

*Revelation 20:10 -- Subject Identification, Basanizo Semantic Range, and Vocabulary Differentiation*

---

> **Evidence Tiers**
>
> This series uses a three-tier evidence classification to distinguish what Scripture says from what people claim it teaches:
>
> 1. **Explicit (E)** -- The text directly says this. You can point to a verse and quote it.
> 2. **Necessary Implication (N)** -- No single verse says this word-for-word, but it unavoidably follows from what the text does say. No interpretation is needed -- every reader, regardless of theological position, must agree.
> 3. **Inference (I)** -- Someone claims the Bible teaches this, but no verse says it and no combination of verses forces it. Something must be *added* beyond what the text contains: a concept, a framework, or a choice between possible readings.
>
> The hierarchy matters: **Explicit > Necessary Implication > Inference.** An inference cannot override what the text explicitly states.

## Section A: Subject Gate Analysis -- Who Are the Three Entities?

**1. Document the nature of each subject from the text.**

**The devil:**
Rev 12:9 identifies him as "the great dragon... that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world." Rev 20:2 confirms: "the dragon, that old serpent, which is the Devil, and Satan." The devil is a non-human supernatural spirit being.

> "And the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world." -- Revelation 12:9

**The beast:**
Rev 13:1-2: rises from the sea, seven heads, ten horns, composite body (leopard, bear, lion). Rev 17:8: "was, and is not, and shall ascend out of the bottomless pit, and go into perdition." The beast is identified by a number (Rev 13:18). No literal human being rises from the sea with seven heads and ten horns, or "was, and is not." This is a symbolic apocalyptic entity.

> "The beast that thou sawest was, and is not; and shall ascend out of the bottomless pit, and go into perdition." -- Revelation 17:8

**The false prophet:**
Rev 19:20 identifies the false prophet as the entity that "wrought miracles before" the beast -- the second beast of Rev 13:11-17. The Greek *pseudoprophetes* appears 3 times in Revelation (16:13; 19:20; 20:10) as a singular specific apocalyptic figure. It appears 8 times elsewhere in the NT (Matt 7:15; 24:11,24; Mark 13:22; Luke 6:26; Acts 13:6; 2 Pet 2:1; 1 John 4:1) as a generic plural class of human beings. The Revelation usage is distinct: a specific symbolic entity, not a human individual.

**2. Subject Gate application.**

- The devil: non-human supernatural spirit being. Automatic FAIL: "A non-human spirit being."
- The beast: symbolic entity in an apocalyptic vision (seven heads, ten horns, rises from sea, "was, and is not"). Automatic FAIL: "A symbolic entity (a figure in an apocalyptic vision)."
- The false prophet: symbolic entity (the second beast of Rev 13:11-17). Automatic FAIL: "A symbolic entity."

All three subjects FAIL the Subject Gate. Rev 20:10 cannot serve as positional evidence about the fate of literal human beings. The torment formula is applied to entities that are not literal human beings.

**3. Genre classification.**

Rev 1:1 uses *semaino* (to signify, to communicate through signs): "The Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave unto him, to shew unto his servants things which must shortly come to pass; and he sent and signified it by his angel." Nave's Topical Bible classifies Revelation under VISION. The Genre Gate FAILS: Revelation is apocalyptic vision, not didactic teaching. Apocalyptic imagery is governed by, not governor of, didactic teaching from epistles and Gospels.

## Section B: Word Study -- Basanizo (G928) Full NT Usage

**4. All 12 NT occurrences of basanizo.**

**Non-judgment uses (7 of 12):**

| Passage | Subject | Context | KJV Translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matt 8:6 | Centurion's servant | Physical illness (palsy) | "Grievously tormented" |
| Matt 14:24 | Ship/boat | Waves tossing | "Tossed" |
| Mark 6:48 | Disciples | Difficulty rowing | "Toiling" |
| Mark 5:7 | Demon (Legion) | Demonic fear at Jesus' presence | "Torment" |
| Luke 8:28 | Demon (Legion) | Demonic fear at Jesus' presence | "Torment" |
| 2 Pet 2:8 | Lot | Emotional distress at Sodom's wickedness | "Vexed" |
| Rev 12:2 | Woman (symbolic) | Labor pains | "Pained" |

**Judgment uses (5 of 12):**

| Passage | Subject | Context | KJV Translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matt 8:29 | Demons | Demons anticipating their own future judgment | "Torment" |
| Rev 9:5 | Locusts' victims | Judgment with 5-month time limit | "Tormented" |
| Rev 11:10 | Two witnesses' effect | Prophets' testimony as "torment" to the earthly | "Tormented" |
| Rev 14:10 | Beast-worshippers | Apocalyptic judgment scene | "Tormented" |
| Rev 20:10 | Devil, beast, false prophet | Lake of fire | "Tormented" |

58% of *basanizo* uses (7 of 12) are non-judgment contexts. The word covers physical illness, waves tossing a boat, toiling at rowing, emotional distress, labor pains, and demonic fear. It is not a technical term for eschatological eternal punishment.

**5. Location of the five judgment uses.**

All five judgment uses are either in Revelation's apocalyptic framework (Rev 9:5; 11:10; 14:10; 20:10) or involve demons speaking of their own future (Matt 8:29). Zero are in epistles. Zero are in Gospel didactic teaching about human fate. Zero are in the OT. The judgment use of *basanizo* is confined to apocalyptic genre and demonic self-reference.

**6. Revelation 9:5 -- the five-month limit.**

> "And to them it was given that they should not kill them, but that they should be tormented five months." -- Revelation 9:5

*Basanizo* here carries an explicit time limit. This demonstrates that the word does not inherently encode duration. It describes the experience (pain, suffering, distress) but says nothing about how long. Duration is determined by context -- "five months" in Rev 9:5, "day and night for ever and ever" in Rev 20:10. The word itself is neutral regarding duration.

**7. Related word group.**

**Basanismos (G929):** 6 NT occurrences, ALL in Revelation (9:5; 14:11; 18:7,10,15). Three of six describe Babylon's *basanismos*. But Babylon's *basanismos* IS her completed destruction:
- Rev 18:8: "utterly burned with fire"
- Rev 18:10: "in one hour is thy judgment come"
- Rev 18:21: "shall be thrown down, and shall be found no more at all"

Babylon's "torment" (*basanismos*) results in her total annihilation. She is "found no more at all." The torment word group in Revelation does not require ongoing existence -- it can describe the process of destruction.

**Basanos (G931):** 3 NT occurrences: Matt 4:24 (physical diseases), Luke 16:23 (parabolic context -- rich man), Luke 16:28 (parabolic context -- rich man). *Basanos* is NOT used in any eschatological judgment passage.

## Section C: Vocabulary Differentiation in Revelation 20:7-15

**8. Vocabulary mapping.**

| Passage | Subject | Vocabulary | Greek Word |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rev 19:20 | Beast and false prophet | "Cast alive" | *zontes* (living) |
| Rev 19:21 | Human remnant | "Slain" | *apektanthesan* (killed) |
| Rev 20:9 | Human armies | "Devoured" | *katephagen* (consumed) |
| Rev 20:10 | Devil, beast, false prophet | "Tormented day and night for ever and ever" | *basanisthesontai* |
| Rev 20:14-15 | Humans not in book of life | "The second death" / "cast into the lake of fire" | *ho thanatos ho deuteros* |
| Rev 21:8 | Eight categories of human wicked | "The second death" | *ho thanatos ho deuteros* |

The text consistently distinguishes between the three non-human/symbolic entities and human beings. Symbolic entities receive torment vocabulary and are cast "alive." Human beings are "slain," "devoured," and consigned to "the second death." The vocabulary differentiation is not occasional -- it is systematic across every mention.

**9. The "tormented for ever and ever" formula.**

The specific formula *basanisthesontai eis tous aionas ton aionon* appears once in Scripture -- Revelation 20:10. It names three subjects: the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. It is never applied to human beings anywhere in the Bible. Rev 20:15 casts humans into the lake without the formula. Rev 21:8 names eight categories of human wicked and calls the lake "the second death." The text had the opportunity to extend the formula to humans and instead used death vocabulary.

**10. Revelation 20:6 -- "the second death has no power."**

> "Blessed and holy is he that hath part in the first resurrection: on such the second death hath no power." -- Revelation 20:6

The word "power" (*exousia*) is death language, not torment language. We speak of death having "power" or "dominion" (cf. Rom 6:9: "death hath no more dominion over him"). The second death is presented as something that exercises authority over a person -- consistent with death terminology. This is the text's own characterization of the second death: it has *exousia*, the kind of language that belongs to the domain of death, not the domain of conscious experience.

## Section D: The "Ages of Ages" Formula

**11. Distribution of *eis tous aionas ton aionon*.**

The formula appears approximately 22 times in the NT:
- Approximately 19 times doxologically: for God's glory, reign, and existence (Gal 1:5; Phil 4:20; 1 Tim 1:17; 2 Tim 4:18; Heb 1:8; 13:21; 1 Pet 4:11; 5:11; Rev 1:6,18; 4:9,10; 5:13; 7:12; 10:6; 11:15; 15:7; 22:5, etc.)
- 3 times in judgment contexts: Rev 14:11 (smoke of beast-worshippers), Rev 19:3 (smoke of Babylon), Rev 20:10 (torment of devil, beast, false prophet)

All three judgment uses are in Revelation's apocalyptic framework. None is in an epistle or Gospel. The formula's overwhelming use is doxological (for God). Its judgment uses are confined to one apocalyptic book.

**12. Revelation 19:3 and the "for ever and ever" formula for Babylon.**

> "And again they said, Alleluia. And her smoke rose up for ever and ever." -- Revelation 19:3

> "And a mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone, and cast it into the sea, saying, Thus with violence shall that great city Babylon be thrown down, and shall be found no more at all." -- Revelation 18:21

The identical "for ever and ever" formula describes the smoke of Babylon -- an entity that is "found no more at all," "utterly burned with fire" (18:8), destroyed "in one hour" (18:10). The "for ever and ever" language coexists with total annihilation in the same book, applied to the same entity. Within Revelation's apocalyptic framework, "smoke ascending for ever and ever" can describe the aftermath of completed destruction -- permanent results, not ongoing process.

## Section E: The Extension Argument

**13. Evaluation of the "same destination = same experience" argument.**

The argument requires three unstated premises:
1. That all inhabitants of the lake share the same experience -- the text does not state this.
2. That the torment formula extends beyond its named subjects -- the text names the devil, beast, and false prophet, not humans.
3. That the vocabulary difference is insignificant -- but the text uses "devoured" (v.9), "tormented" (v.10), and "second death" (vv.14-15) in the same passage, systematically distinguishing subjects.

The text itself provides different terminology for different subjects at the same destination. "Same destination = same experience" must be added from outside the text. This is an I-C inference (compatible external): it does not contradict any explicit statement (the text does not deny humans could experience torment), but it extends beyond what the text states. The text had the opportunity to append the torment formula to Rev 20:15 or 21:8 and instead used "the second death."

**14. The beast-as-representative argument.**

The argument that the beast's torment transfers to its human referents is undermined by Revelation's own vocabulary distinctions:
- The symbolic trio (devil, beast, false prophet) receives "torment" (*basanizo*) -- Rev 20:10.
- Human beings receive different vocabulary: "slain" (*apokteino* -- Rev 19:21), "devoured" (*katesthio* -- Rev 20:9), "second death" (Rev 20:14-15).

The text distinguishes the subjects and defines the lake of fire as "the second death" -- its own self-interpretation. If the beast's experience were meant to transfer to humans, the text's consistent vocabulary differentiation between the two groups would be inexplicable.

**15. Matthew 25:41 and 25:46 -- vocabulary difference.**

Matt 25:41: the everlasting fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels" -- its primary designation is for supernatural entities.

Matt 25:46: the human punishment vocabulary is *kolasis* (G2851, "punishment"), not *basanizo* (G928, "torment"). *Kolasis* occurs only twice in the NT: Matt 25:46 and 1 John 4:18 ("fear hath torment/punishment"). Even in the one passage where humans share a destination with the devil, the vocabulary shifts: the fire was *prepared* for the devil; the human term is *kolasis*, not *basanizo*. The text maintains the vocabulary distinction even here.

## Section F: What Vocabulary Does the NT Use for Human Fate Outside Revelation?

**16. NT vocabulary for the final fate of human wicked outside Revelation.**

| Passage | Author | Genre | Vocabulary | Greek |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matt 10:28 | Jesus | Didactic | "Destroy" soul and body | *apollymi* |
| John 3:16 | John | Didactic | "Perish" vs. everlasting life | *apollymi* |
| Rom 6:23 | Paul | Didactic | "Death" vs. eternal life | *thanatos* |
| 2 Thess 1:9 | Paul | Didactic | "Everlasting destruction" | *olethros aionios* |
| Phil 3:19 | Paul | Didactic | "Destruction" | *apoleia* |
| 2 Pet 3:7 | Peter | Didactic | "Perdition of ungodly men" | *apoleia* |
| Matt 7:13 | Jesus | Didactic | "Destruction" (the broad way) | *apoleia* |
| Gal 6:8 | Paul | Didactic | "Corruption" | *phthora* |
| Heb 10:27 | Author of Hebrews | Didactic | "Devour" the adversaries | *katesthio* |

All are didactic genre. All use destruction/death vocabulary. None uses *basanizo* or *basanismos*. The NT's consistent vocabulary for human fate outside Revelation is destruction, death, perdition, corruption, and perishing.

**17. Hebrews 2:14 -- destruction vocabulary for the devil.**

> "That through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil." -- Hebrews 2:14

Even the devil -- whose future judgment includes *basanizo* according to Rev 20:10 -- is described with destruction vocabulary (*katargeo*) in a didactic epistle. *Katargeo* has a wide semantic range (27 NT occurrences: "abolish," "render powerless," "nullify," "do away with"), and genuine lexical ambiguity exists for the Heb 2:14 application. But it is noteworthy that when an epistle writer describes the devil's fate, destruction vocabulary is used rather than torment vocabulary.

**18. Matthew 8:29 -- demons anticipate basanizo.**

> "And, behold, they cried out, saying, What have we to do with thee, Jesus, thou Son of God? art thou come hither to torment us before the time?" -- Matthew 8:29

The subjects anticipating *basanizo* are demons -- supernatural entities, not human beings. They recognize torment as their own appointed future judgment. This is one of only two *basanizo* uses outside Revelation, and it involves supernatural entities referring to their own fate. It reinforces the pattern: *basanizo* as judgment vocabulary is associated with non-human/supernatural entities, not with generic human wicked.

## Section G: Evidence Classification Exercise -- Answers

**1. "Rev 20:10 names three subjects for 'tormented day and night for ever and ever': the devil, the beast, and the false prophet."**
- **Explicit (E).** The verse directly names all three subjects. Quotable.

**2. "The 'tormented for ever and ever' formula appears once in Scripture (Rev 20:10), applied only to the devil, beast, and false prophet -- never to human beings."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** The formula's single occurrence is an observable textual fact. Its application to these three subjects and not to humans is verifiable by distribution. Both sides accept the textual observation. They disagree about whether the formula extends beyond its stated subjects -- but the distribution itself is a necessary implication of the data.

**3. "The torment described for these three entities in Rev 20:10 applies equally to all unsaved humans who enter the lake of fire."**
- **Inference (I) -- specifically I-C (Compatible External).** The text names three non-human/symbolic subjects. It does not state that humans experience the same. The claim adds "all unsaved humans experience what Rev 20:10 describes" -- not stated in text. It does not contradict any explicit statement (the text does not deny humans could experience torment), but extends beyond what the text contains. What is added: "same destination = same experience."

**4. "Basanizo is not a technical term for eschatological eternal punishment because 7 of 12 uses are non-judgment contexts and one judgment use has an explicit 5-month time limit."**
- **Necessary Implication (N).** The distribution data is observable: 7 of 12 non-judgment, Rev 9:5 has a five-month limit. Both sides accept these translation facts. The conclusion that the word is not a technical eschatological term follows unavoidably from the distribution.

**5. "Rev 20:10 proves eternal conscious torment for all inhabitants of the lake of fire because the torment formula is stated for entities in the lake, and humans are also cast into the lake."**
- **Inference (I) -- specifically I-B (Competing Evidence).** FOR: Rev 20:10 states the torment formula for entities in the lake; Rev 20:15 states humans are cast into the same lake. AGAINST: the text uses different vocabulary for different subjects (E), the formula is applied only to non-human/symbolic entities (N), the text defines the lake as "the second death" for humans (E), basanizo is not used for human fate outside Revelation (E). The FOR side rests on 2 Ambiguous items; the AGAINST side rests on 10+ Plain items. What is added: "same lake = same experience" when the text provides different terminology.

**6. "The text of Rev 20:7-15 uses distinct vocabulary for distinct subjects: 'devoured' for humans (v.9), 'tormented' for devil/beast/false prophet (v.10), 'second death' for humans (vv.14-15)."**
- **Explicit (E).** Each vocabulary choice is directly observable in the text. *Katephagen* (Rev 20:9), *basanisthesontai* (Rev 20:10), *ho thanatos ho deuteros* (Rev 20:14). The vocabulary pattern is a textual observation, not an interpretation.

**7. "No epistle, no Gospel, and no OT passage uses basanizo or basanismos for the final fate of generic human wicked."**
- **Explicit (E).** This is a verifiable distribution fact. One can survey every occurrence of *basanizo* (12x) and *basanismos* (6x) and confirm that none applies these words to generic human eschatological fate outside Revelation's apocalyptic framework (and Matt 8:29, where demons reference their own future).

## Section H: What CAN Be Said / What CANNOT Be Said -- Answers

**What CAN be said (the Bible explicitly states or necessarily implies):**

1. Rev 20:10 names three subjects for "tormented day and night for ever and ever": the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. The devil is a non-human supernatural spirit being (Rev 12:9; 20:2). The beast is a symbolic apocalyptic entity (rises from sea, seven heads, ten horns, "was, and is not" -- Rev 13:1; 17:8). The false prophet is a symbolic apocalyptic entity (the second beast of Rev 13:11-17). None is a literal human being.

2. The Subject Gate FAILS for all three subjects. The devil is a non-human spirit being. The beast and false prophet are symbolic entities in an apocalyptic vision. The Genre Gate FAILS because Revelation is classified as VISION (Nave's) and communicates through signs (*semaino*, Rev 1:1).

3. The "tormented for ever and ever" formula appears once in Scripture -- Rev 20:10 -- applied to three non-human/symbolic entities. It is not applied to human beings anywhere in the Bible.

4. The text of Rev 20:7-15 uses distinct vocabulary for distinct subjects: "devoured" for human armies (v.9), "tormented" for the three non-human/symbolic entities (v.10), "the second death" for humans entering the lake (vv.14-15; 21:8). Rev 19:20-21 maintains the same distinction: symbolic entities cast "alive," humans "slain."

5. Basanizo has a wide semantic range (illness, waves, rowing, distress, childbirth, demonic fear, judgment). Seven of twelve uses (58%) are non-judgment contexts. Rev 9:5 uses basanizo with a five-month limit. It is not a technical term for eschatological eternal punishment.

6. All five basanizo judgment uses are in Revelation's apocalyptic framework or involve demons speaking of their own future (Matt 8:29). No epistle, no Gospel, and no OT passage uses basanizo or basanismos for the final fate of generic human wicked.

7. Outside Revelation, the NT consistently uses destruction/death vocabulary for the final fate of human wicked: "destroy" (*apollymi*, Matt 10:28; John 3:16), "everlasting destruction" (*olethros aionios*, 2 Thess 1:9), "death" (*thanatos*, Rom 6:23), "destruction" (*apoleia*, Phil 3:19; 2 Pet 3:7; Matt 7:13), "corruption" (*phthora*, Gal 6:8), "devour" (*katesthio*, Heb 10:27).

8. The everlasting fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matt 25:41). The human punishment vocabulary in Matt 25:46 is *kolasis* (punishment), not *basanizo* (torment).

9. The "for ever and ever" formula in Rev 19:3 describes Babylon's smoke -- an entity that is "found no more at all" (Rev 18:21), "utterly burned with fire" (18:8), destroyed "in one hour" (18:10). Within Revelation's apocalyptic framework, the formula coexists with total annihilation.

10. Rev 20:6 characterizes the second death as having "power" (*exousia*) -- death language, consistent with its identification as "the second death" rather than "the second torment."

**What CANNOT be said (not stated or necessarily implied -- requires adding something):**

1. **It cannot be said that Rev 20:10 teaches eternal conscious torment for human beings.** The three named subjects are the devil (non-human), the beast (symbolic), and the false prophet (symbolic). The Subject Gate FAILS for all three. The torment formula is applied to these entities, not to humans. When humans appear in the same passage, the vocabulary is "devoured" (v.9) and "second death" (vv.14-15; 21:8).

2. **It cannot be said that "same destination = same experience."** The text itself provides different vocabulary for different subjects at the same destination (the lake of fire). The devil/beast/false prophet receive the torment formula. Humans receive death terminology. Extending the torment formula to humans requires adding a concept the text does not contain.

3. **It cannot be said that basanizo is a technical term for eternal conscious punishment.** Seven of twelve uses are non-judgment (illness, waves, rowing, distress, childbirth, demonic fear). One judgment use has a five-month limit. The word covers physical suffering, labor exertion, emotional distress, and divine judgment. Its eschatological uses are confined to Revelation's apocalyptic framework.

4. **It cannot be said that the "tormented for ever and ever" formula is applied to humans anywhere in Scripture.** The formula appears once (Rev 20:10) and names three non-human/symbolic entities. Rev 20:15 casts humans into the lake without the formula. Rev 21:8 names eight categories of human wicked and calls the lake "the second death." Extending the formula to humans is an inference, not a textual statement.

5. **It cannot be said that the Bible uses torment vocabulary for the final fate of human wicked outside Revelation.** No epistle, no Gospel, and no OT passage applies basanizo or basanismos to generic human judgment. The consistent vocabulary across epistles and Gospels is destruction, death, perdition, corruption, and perishing.

6. **It cannot be said that the extension of Rev 20:10 to humans is a necessary implication.** The text provides the opportunity to state the torment formula for humans (Rev 20:15; 21:8) and instead uses "second death." The text provides the opportunity to use basanizo for human fate across the NT and instead uses apollymi, olethros, thanatos, apoleia, and phthora. The consistent choice to use different vocabulary when describing human fate is an observable textual pattern.
